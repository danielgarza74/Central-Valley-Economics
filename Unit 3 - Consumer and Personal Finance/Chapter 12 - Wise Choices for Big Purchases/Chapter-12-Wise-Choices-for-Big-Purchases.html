Concept Review

Chapter 10 taught you to plan with a budget and to save, and Chapter 11 showed what borrowing costs and how your credit record is built. This chapter uses those tools on the largest decisions in a household budget. Housing and transportation together take about half of what the typical household spends, as you saw in Chapter 10. You will study how a smart consumer compares options, how to think about renting and buying, how to count the full cost of a vehicle or a college education, and how insurance protects a household from risks that no budget could absorb.

Chapter 12 Key Concept

A big purchase is a decision with a large price, a long time horizon, or both. A wise consumer compares the total cost over time, not just the sticker price or the monthly payment, weighs benefits against opportunity costs, and uses insurance to share the risk of rare but costly events.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine that you are 19 and have just been offered a full-time job at a distribution center on the edge of Bakersfield. You need a car to get there. At the dealership, a salesperson asks, "What monthly payment are you looking for?" That question is designed to move your attention from the price of the car to a number that feels manageable. If you answer, the salesperson can stretch the loan to 84 months, add extras, and still hit your payment. Another buyer in the same lot might ask different questions: what is the total price, what is the interest rate, what will insurance and repairs cost, and what could I do with a cheaper car? Two buyers, same car, thousands of dollars apart. This chapter teaches you to be the second buyer.

Key Concepts

The Consumer's Role in a Market Economy

Every time you spend money, you vote. In Chapters 3 and 6 you learned that in a market economy, prices and the choices of buyers tell producers what to make. A consumer is a person who buys goods and services to use them. When millions of consumers choose a store-brand cereal over a name-brand one, or choose a phone with a longer battery life, businesses respond by offering more of what people buy. Economists call this idea consumer sovereignty: consumers, by their spending decisions, have a great deal of say over what the economy produces.

That power comes with responsibility, because your choices are limited by scarcity. Your income is limited, and every dollar spent on one thing cannot be spent on another. The opportunity cost of a purchase is the next-best alternative that you give up, as you learned in Chapter 1. If you spend $400 on a used phone, the opportunity cost might be two months of gas, a start on an emergency fund, or a month of groceries. A wise buyer asks not only "Is this worth the price?" but "Is this worth more to me than the best other way to use the money?"

Economists also look at the margin. The marginal benefit of a purchase is the extra satisfaction from one more unit, and the marginal cost is the extra price. For many goods, marginal benefit falls as you buy more. The first pair of work boots is very valuable. The fifth pair of boots is not. A smart consumer buys until the marginal benefit of the next unit no longer outweighs its marginal cost, and then stops. In practice, most people do this by feel, but the habit of asking "Do I need more of this?" can prevent a lot of waste.

This chapter focuses on big purchases: decisions in which the price is large, the time horizon is long, or both. A car, an apartment or a home, college, and insurance are all big purchases. Mistakes on these cost far more than mistakes on a $5 lunch, and they are harder to undo. In California, the personal finance course that will soon be offered in every public high school includes the "types and costs" of insurance, the means to finance college, and consumer protection skills, so this chapter covers each of them.

Key Concepts

A Five-Step Buying Process

Good decisions about big purchases follow a process, and Figure 12.2 shows one that financial educators recommend. It begins before you ever go shopping.

Step 1: Identify the need and set a budget. Be specific. "I need a way to get to work 18 miles away for under $350 a month in total costs" is a much better starting point than "I want a truck." Use the budget skills from Chapter 10 to find the amount you can afford, including the extras. Step 2: Research. Look for independent sources, such as government agencies, consumer-testing organizations, and reviews from many buyers, not only from the seller. Check safety recalls, repair histories, and prices. Step 3: Compare options. Put at least three choices side by side and compare them on the same measures: total price, features, reliability, and long-run costs. Step 4: Decide, then negotiate and read before you sign. Ask for the full price in writing, including fees, taxes, and add-ons. Be willing to walk away. Step 5: Evaluate after buying. Keep your receipts and records, use the warranty if you need it, and think about what you would do differently next time.

Notice that the process uses a pause. Most expensive mistakes are made under pressure, when a seller says that a price will rise tomorrow or that someone else is about to buy the item. A simple rule is the 48-hour wait for any non-emergency purchase over a set amount. If a deal is truly good, it will usually survive until you have checked the facts.

Key Concepts

Comparison Shopping and Unit Prices

Comparison shopping means looking at similar products from different sellers, or different versions from the same seller, before you decide. The most useful tool is the unit price: the price per ounce, per pound, per sheet, per gallon, or per item. Unit price allows you to compare packages of different sizes on the same basis. Most grocery stores in California show the unit price on the shelf tag.

The formula is simple: unit price = total price ÷ number of units. Figure 12.1 compares four hypothetical boxes of cereal. A 12-ounce name-brand box for $4.20 costs $0.35 an ounce. An 18-ounce box of the same brand for $5.76 costs $0.32 an ounce, and a 27-ounce box for $7.56 costs $0.28 an ounce. The store brand in an 18-ounce box for $4.14 is the best value at $0.23 an ounce. If you buy the 27-ounce box in place of the 12-ounce box, you save seven cents an ounce, but only if you will actually eat it before it goes stale. A larger package is not a bargain if part of it is wasted. In the same way, a store brand is not always the same quality, but for many basic goods independent tests find little difference.

Unit pricing works beyond groceries. A phone plan that costs $45 a month for 10 gigabytes of data costs $4.50 a gigabyte, and a plan with 30 gigabytes for $60 costs $2.00 a gigabyte. A cheaper plan with less data is the better choice only if you use that little data. For clothing and tools, the equivalent measure is cost per use, which you will study in the next subsection.

Key Concepts

Total Cost and Cost per Use

The price on the tag is only the first cost. A total cost comparison adds up everything you will pay over the time that you own or use a product: the purchase price, taxes and fees, interest if you borrow, operating costs such as electricity or gasoline, maintenance and repairs, and the loss in value over time. Two products with the same price can have very different total costs. A refrigerator that costs $100 less but uses $60 more electricity a year is not a bargain after two years.

A related idea is cost per use: the price divided by the number of times you expect to use the item. Figure 12.3 compares three hypothetical pairs of work shoes. Pair A costs $35 and lasts about 100 wears, so each wear costs $0.35. Pair B costs $60 and lasts about 250 wears, which is $0.24 a wear. Pair C costs $140 and lasts about 400 wears, which is again $0.35 a wear. The middle choice is the best value, although neither the cheapest nor the most expensive. The example also shows why "cheap" and "expensive" are poor guides: what matters is the cost for the service that you actually receive.

Cost per use has limits. It depends on estimates of how long a product will last, and those estimates can be wrong. And the lowest cost per use does not help if the up-front price is more than your budget allows. A buyer with $40 to spend cannot buy Pair B, no matter how good the value is. This situation, in which people with less money pay more over time for cheaper goods that wear out sooner, is sometimes called the poverty premium. It is one reason that saving even a small amount for a better item can pay off.

EXAMPLEThe Phone Plan Trap

This is a hypothetical example. Dani, 18, sees an ad: "Get a new $900 phone for only $25 a month!" The offer is a 36-month installment plan. Dani multiplies: $25 × 36 = $900, so the price is the same as paying cash, with no interest. But Dani also needs a service plan at $40 a month, which is not included. Over 36 months, the phone and service together will cost $900 + (40 × 36 = $1,440) = $2,340. A different offer is a $350 phone paid in cash, plus a $30-a-month plan. Over 36 months that costs $350 + $1,080 = $1,430, which is $910 less.

Key Concepts

How Sellers Influence Your Choices

Businesses spend billions of dollars each year on advertising and sales strategies because they know that people respond to incentives and that attention is limited. Understanding common techniques will not make you immune, but it can help you notice when your decision is being shaped.

Anchoring. A seller shows a high "original price" crossed out, so that the "sale price" looks like a bargain. The crossed-out price may never have been charged. Scarcity and urgency. "Only three left!" and countdown timers push people to buy before they have thought. Bundling and add-ons. A seller adds an extended warranty, a protection package, or accessories when you are already committed to the purchase, so you are less likely to question the price. Free trials and subscriptions. A free month may roll into a paid subscription that renews automatically, and canceling can be difficult. Set a calendar reminder, and check how to cancel before you start. The monthly-payment frame. A seller who asks, "What payment do you want?" can meet any payment by lengthening the loan, which increases the total cost, as you saw in Chapter 11. Social proof and reviews. A high rating or many reviews build confidence, but some reviews are paid for or written by the seller. Look for patterns in detailed reviews and check independent sources.

Another influence is how you pay. Studies by behavioral economists have found that people often spend more when they pay by card or by installment than when they hand over cash, because the pain of paying is delayed or hidden. "Buy now, pay later" plans can be convenient, but they can lead to many small commitments that add up. Use the same budget rule: only commit to payments that fit comfortably in your net income.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEA Minimum Guarantee: The European Union

Consumer protection varies widely around the world. In the European Union, a law sets a minimum legal guarantee of two years from the day that a buyer receives most goods. If the goods are faulty, the seller must repair or replace them at no cost, and if that is not possible within a reasonable time, the buyer can receive a full or partial refund. If a defect appears within the first year after delivery, the law generally presumes that it existed at the time of delivery unless the seller shows otherwise. The guarantee does not apply to goods bought from private individuals.

In the United States, protections come from many sources: federal laws, state laws such as California's, and the terms of each warranty and contract. A U.S. buyer therefore needs to read the specific warranty and know the rules where he or she lives. Economists note that such rules change incentives. When sellers must stand behind a product, they have more reason to make it well, and buyers have more reason to trust sellers they do not know. But rules also raise costs, which can be passed along in prices.

Key Concepts

Contracts, Warranties, and Returns

A contract is a legally binding agreement between two or more parties. When you sign a lease, a loan agreement, or a purchase agreement, you are agreeing to follow its terms, including the ones you have not read. Before you sign anything, read it in full, ask about anything that you do not understand, and be sure that every promise made aloud, such as a free oil change or a price cap, is written on the paper. If something is left blank, do not sign until it is filled in. Ask for a copy, and keep it.

A warranty is a promise by a seller or manufacturer about the quality of a product, and it states what will be repaired or replaced and for how long. Read what is covered and what is not. Extended warranties and service contracts, which are sold separately, cover only what the document says, and some cover very little. The California Attorney General advises buyers to find out exactly what an extended warranty covers before buying it. Return policies also differ from store to store: some give refunds, some give store credit only, and some allow no returns on certain items. Check the policy before you buy, and keep the receipt.

Some contracts give buyers a right to cancel. The Federal Trade Commission's Cooling-Off Rule gives consumers three business days to cancel certain sales made at their home or other places away from the seller's regular place of business, such as a door-to-door sale of more than $25. California also lets a buyer purchase a two-day contract cancellation option when buying a used car from a dealer, which allows the buyer to return the vehicle for a full refund for any reason, as long as the buyer follows the rules; dealers may charge a nonrefundable fee for the option, and some vehicles are excluded. Many contracts, however, give no right of cancellation at all, and "as is" sales mean the buyer accepts the product in its current condition. For a big purchase, the safest assumption is that you cannot back out after you sign.

Consumer Protection and Where to Get Help

Government agencies and laws exist to protect consumers from unfair and deceptive practices. The consumer protection system in the United States has many parts. At the federal level, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) enforces laws against unfair or deceptive business practices and collects consumer reports, and the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) takes complaints about financial products such as loans and credit cards. In California, the Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI) oversees many lenders, the Department of Consumer Affairs licenses many professions and runs programs such as the lemon law arbitration program, and the Attorney General enforces consumer protection laws.

California's lemon law, for example, covers defective vehicles that cannot be fixed after a reasonable number of repair attempts. According to the California Attorney General, a vehicle is presumed to be a lemon if the problem appeared within 18 months or 18,000 miles and the vehicle either went to the manufacturer for repair four or more times for the problem, or was out of service for more than 30 days. If the manufacturer cannot fix it, it may have to repurchase or replace the vehicle. The law generally applies to new vehicles under the manufacturer's warranty. For used cars, California requires licensed dealers to provide a vehicle history report and gives buyers the option to purchase the two-day cancellation right described earlier.

If you have a problem with a purchase, follow a simple order. First, keep records: receipts, contracts, emails, photos, and the names and dates of every call. Second, contact the seller in writing, state the problem, and say what you want, such as a repair, a replacement, or a refund. Third, if the seller does not respond, use any dispute process offered by your card issuer; many card issuers let you dispute a charge for goods that were not delivered or were not as described. Fourth, file a complaint with the FTC, the CFPB, or a California state agency, depending on the problem. Reporting helps you, and it also helps agencies see patterns that protect others. And as you learned in Chapter 11, never pay a stranger with gift cards, cryptocurrency, or a wire transfer, because these are the favorite payment methods of scammers.

Key Concepts

Housing: Renting

For most young adults, the first big monthly bill is housing. In Chapter 10 you saw that housing is the largest category in the average household's budget, at 33.9 percent of spending in 2025. A young adult's first choice is usually to rent. When you rent, you sign a lease, a contract that gives you the right to live in a place for a set time, usually 6 or 12 months, in exchange for monthly rent. The lease also states the security deposit, the rules about guests and pets, who pays for utilities, and what happens if you leave early. A lease is a legally binding contract, so a 12-month lease at $900 a month commits you to $10,800 even if you move out in month five, unless the lease allows you to end it or sublet.

How much rent can you afford? Housing experts commonly use a guideline that rent and utilities should be no more than about 30 percent of gross income. A household that spends more than that is often described as cost burdened, because little remains for food, transportation, and savings. The guideline is a starting point, not a law. The median household income in Kern County was $70,210 a year in 2024, according to the Census Bureau's American Community Survey as presented by Data USA. Divide by 12 and you get about $5,851 a month. Thirty percent of that is about $1,755. A single worker earning a lower wage, such as California's statewide minimum wage of $16.90 an hour for full-time work, earns about $2,929 a month before taxes (16.90 × 40 × 52 ÷ 12), and 30 percent of that is only about $879. This gap is one reason that many young adults share housing with roommates or relatives.

Before signing a lease, calculate the total move-in cost: first month's rent, the security deposit, any application fees, utility deposits, and furniture. Ask what is included in the rent and what is not. Check the lease for late fees, rules for raising the rent, and the process for getting the deposit back. Take dated photographs of the unit's condition on the day you move in, so that you are not charged later for damage that was already there. And consider renters insurance, which you will study in Section 3. If you share a lease with roommates, remember that each person may be responsible for the whole rent if someone leaves.

Key Concepts

Buying a Home: How a Mortgage Works

For many families, buying a home is the largest purchase of a lifetime, and it works through borrowing. A mortgage is a long-term loan used to buy real estate, with the property as collateral. If the borrower stops paying, the lender can take the home through a legal process called foreclosure. Most buyers must pay part of the price in cash, called a down payment. A 20 percent down payment is the traditional target. On the median Kern County home value of $338,300 (American Community Survey 2024, via Data USA), 20 percent is $67,660, and the loan would be $270,640. A smaller down payment is possible, but borrowers who put down less than 20 percent usually must pay for private mortgage insurance, an added monthly cost that protects the lender, not the buyer.

The mortgage payment has four main parts, often remembered as PITI: principal, the part that pays down the loan; interest, the lender's price; property taxes, which in California are generally about 1 percent of the assessed value plus local charges; and homeowners insurance. Buyers also pay closing costs, fees for the appraisal, title, loan processing, and other services, which can add up to several thousand dollars. After the purchase, the owner pays for all maintenance and repairs, which financial planners often suggest budgeting at around 1 percent of the home's value each year.

Mortgage rates matter enormously, because the loan is large and long. According to Freddie Mac's weekly survey, the average rate on a 30-year fixed-rate mortgage was 7.28 percent on November 19, 2026, compared with 6.26 percent a year earlier. The average 15-year fixed rate was 6.66 percent. Mortgage rates are long-term interest rates, which are influenced by the market's expectations of inflation and by the actions of the Federal Reserve, as you will see in Chapter 20. A person who must borrow when rates are high pays much more for the same house. Figure 12.5 shows the effect on a hypothetical loan of $270,640. At 6.26 percent, a 30-year loan would have a principal-and-interest payment of about $1,668 a month. At 7.28 percent, the payment is about $1,852, which is $184 a month more, or about $66,000 more over the life of the loan.

A 15-year mortgage has a higher monthly payment but a much lower total cost. At 6.66 percent, the 15-year payment on the same loan is about $2,381 a month, but the total interest is about $158,000, compared with about $396,000 for the 30-year loan at 7.28 percent. The trade-off is between a payment you can afford now and the total cost over time. Many borrowers choose 30 years for the lower payment and pay extra when they can. Others choose an adjustable-rate mortgage, in which the rate starts lower but can change later, which adds risk that the payment will rise. Whatever the choice, the lender will look at your credit score and your debt-to-income ratio from Chapter 11, so a strong record can lower the rate you are offered.

Equity: the savings account inside a home. When you make mortgage payments, part of each payment is principal, and that portion increases your equity, the difference between what the home is worth and what you still owe. In the first years of a 30-year loan, most of the payment goes to interest, and equity grows slowly. Later, more of each payment is principal. Equity can also rise or fall with the market value of the home. If prices in Kern County rise, a homeowner's equity grows even when the owner makes no extra payments; if prices fall, equity can shrink and even become negative. For most families, home equity is the largest asset that they own, which is why a home is both a place to live and an investment.

Equity is also the reason that lenders ask for a down payment. A buyer who puts down $67,660, or 20 percent, on a home worth $338,300 begins with that much equity and a smaller loan. A buyer who puts down only 3 or 5 percent starts with little equity and pays more interest over time. A fall of only a few percent in the home's value could leave that buyer owing more than the house is worth. This is the same risk of being underwater that you saw with vehicles, and it was one of the problems that made the housing crisis of 2007 to 2009 so painful for many families, including in the Central Valley. A home is a long-term decision, and the buyer should plan to stay in it for several years.

A renter pays for a service: a place to live without the costs and risks of ownership. An owner also pays, through interest, property taxes, insurance, repairs, and the opportunity cost of the money used for the down payment. Whether it is cheaper to buy depends on the price of the home compared with the rent for a similar place, the mortgage rate, how long the household will stay, and what happens to prices. Vehicles: The Total Cost of Ownership

For most people in Kern County, a vehicle is not a luxury. It is the way to get to work, school, and the grocery store. That makes a vehicle the second-largest purchase in most households and the largest for many young adults. It is also a purchase in which sellers expect buyers to focus on the monthly payment. According to Cox Automotive's Kelley Blue Book, the average new vehicle sold for about $51,109 in October 2026, after an average incentive of $3,490. The median U.S. household income in 2025 was $87,460, according to the Census Bureau. That means that the average new vehicle costs about 0.58 years of the median household's income, or about seven months of earnings, before taxes.

The price is only the beginning. Add up the total cost of ownership: the sum of purchase price, taxes and fees, financing interest, insurance, fuel, maintenance, repairs, and the loss of value. The biggest hidden cost is depreciation, the decline in a vehicle's value as it ages and is used. A new vehicle usually loses value fastest in its first years. Figure 12.6 shows a hypothetical five-year total for a $30,000 car: $13,500 for depreciation, $9,000 for fuel, $7,500 for insurance, $4,500 for maintenance and repairs, $4,000 for loan interest, and $2,500 for registration and fees. That makes $41,000, which is about $683 a month, or 37 percent more than the purchase price. These numbers are made up for teaching, and your own costs will differ, but the pattern is real: owning a vehicle costs much more than its price.

New, used, or lease? A new vehicle has a warranty and the latest safety features, but it depreciates most. A used vehicle costs less and has already absorbed the steepest drop in value, but it may need more repairs and may not have a warranty. Before buying a used vehicle, get an independent mechanic's inspection and a vehicle history report. A lease is a long-term rental: you pay for the vehicle's depreciation during the lease, usually two or three years, plus fees. Monthly payments can be lower than a loan, but you build no ownership, you have mileage limits, and you may owe charges for excess wear. At the end, you have nothing but the option to buy. Leasing can make sense for people who want a new car every few years and drive a predictable number of miles, but it is usually more expensive over a long period than buying and keeping a car.

Financing. The financing is a separate purchase. The California Attorney General advises buyers to compare loan rates and notes that going directly to a bank may cost less than dealer financing. Get a preapproved loan from a credit union or bank before you visit the dealership, so that you know your rate and can compare the dealer's offer. Avoid very long loans. Because a vehicle depreciates, a buyer with a long loan and a small down payment may be underwater, owing more than the vehicle is worth, which becomes a serious problem if the vehicle is totaled or must be sold. In Chapter 11 you saw that the same $20,000 loan costs hundreds of dollars more in interest when stretched from 60 to 72 months.

Key Concepts

Paying for College and Career Training

Education is also a big purchase, and unlike a vehicle, it is meant to increase your income for the rest of your life. Economists call spending on education and training an investment in human capital, the knowledge and skills that make workers more productive. In Chapter 9 you studied how the labor market pays workers with more skills higher wages. The case study at the end of this chapter looks at the data. For now, the question is how to pay for it.

California's personal finance requirement asks students to identify ways to pay for college, low-cost community college options, workforce education, and apprenticeships. There are many pathways. Community college is the lowest-cost route to a degree or a transfer. Bakersfield College lists an enrollment fee of $46 per unit for California residents. A full-time load of 15 units a semester, or 30 units a year, would cost $1,380 a year in enrollment fees, and two years of full-time study at 60 units would cost about $2,760 in fees. Books, transportation, and living costs are extra, and many students qualify for financial aid that can cover the fees. Career technical education and apprenticeships combine paid work with training, so a student earns while learning. Four-year universities cost more and offer more fields of study; students should look up the current published price at each school, including living costs.

Financial aid comes in several kinds. A grant is money for school that does not have to be repaid, such as the federal Pell Grant or state aid for eligible students. A scholarship is a similar award, often based on merit or on a special group or interest. Work-study programs pay students for part-time jobs on campus. Students start by filing the Free Application for Federal Student Aid (FAFSA), or the California Dream Act Application for eligible students, which are used to determine eligibility for federal and state aid. Aid rules and amounts change from year to year, so check the current details with a counselor and at studentaid.gov.

Loans are the last resort among these options, because they must be repaid with interest. Federal student loans have fixed rates set each year by law. For Direct Loans first disbursed between July 1, 2026, and June 30, 2027, the rate is 6.52 percent for undergraduates, 8.07 percent for graduate and professional students, and 9.07 percent for PLUS loans, which parents and graduate students may borrow. These rates are based on the 10-year Treasury note auction in May 2026 plus an add-on set in law, and they stay fixed for the life of each loan. Compared with other borrowing, as Figure 12.7 shows, federal loans have lower rates than personal loans and credit cards, and they also offer repayment plans and protections that private loans may not. On a subsidized federal loan for an eligible undergraduate, the government pays the interest while the student is in school. On an unsubsidized loan, interest accrues from the start: $20,000 at 6.52 percent adds about $1,304 of interest each year before any payments begin.

Borrowing too much can be a burden for years. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York reported that Americans owed $1.656 trillion in student loans in the third quarter of 2026. A common guideline is that total student loan borrowing should not exceed the amount you expect to earn in your first year after leaving school. Programs that forgive part of student loan debt exist for some borrowers, and the rules for repayment plans and forgiveness have changed repeatedly, so any borrower should check the current rules at studentaid.gov before relying on them.

Key Concepts

Why Insurance Exists

Every big purchase carries a risk. A house can burn, a car can be wrecked, and a sudden illness can produce a hospital bill larger than a year's pay. These events are unlikely for any one person in a given year, but when they happen, the loss can be enormous. Insurance is a contract in which a company agrees to pay for certain losses in exchange for a regular payment. The payment is called the premium. The insurance company, or insurer, collects premiums from many people and uses the money to pay the claims of the few who suffer a loss. A claim is a request for payment under the contract.

The idea behind insurance is risk pooling. Suppose that 10,000 households in Kern County each face a 1-in-100 chance of a $20,000 loss in a year. No household knows whether it will be one of the unlucky 100. But the insurer, by the law of large numbers, can predict that about 100 households will have losses totaling about $2,000,000. If each of the 10,000 households pays a premium of $300, the pool collects $3,000,000. That covers the expected claims of $2,000,000 and leaves $1,000,000 to pay the insurer's costs, such as salaries and claims processing, and to earn a profit. Each household trades a small, certain cost for protection against a large, uncertain one. Economists say that people are risk averse: most prefer a predictable $300 expense to a small chance of a $20,000 shock, even when the average cost is similar. Figure 12.8 shows the cycle.

Insurance is also a requirement in many cases. A lender will not give you a mortgage unless you carry homeowners insurance, and California law requires drivers to show financial responsibility for the damage they may cause to others. Employers who offer health insurance pay a large share of the premium as part of the pay package, which you studied in Chapter 9. And in some cases the government itself runs the insurance: Social Security and Medicare, for example, are social insurance programs paid for by payroll taxes, and Medi-Cal is California's version of Medicaid, which serves people with low incomes. This section focuses on the private insurance that individuals and families buy or receive through work.

The Language of a Policy

To compare policies, you need to know four words that appear on almost every one. The deductible is the amount that you must pay yourself on a covered loss before the insurer starts to pay. If your car repair costs $3,400 and your deductible is $1,000, you pay the first $1,000 and the insurer pays $2,400. A copayment is a fixed fee, such as $30 for a doctor visit. Coinsurance is a percentage of the cost that you pay after the deductible, such as 20 percent. The out-of-pocket maximum is a limit on what you will pay in a year for covered health care; after you reach it, the plan pays 100 percent of covered services. Together, these features decide how the cost of a loss is divided between you and the insurer, which is called cost sharing.

Cost sharing creates a trade-off. A policy with a low deductible has a higher premium, because the insurer expects to pay more on each claim. A policy with a high deductible has a lower premium but leaves you responsible for more of the loss. Figure 12.10, later in this section, shows the trade-off with hypothetical numbers. The best choice depends on how much risk you can afford to carry. A deductible should be an amount that you could pay from your emergency fund without borrowing, which links back to the budgeting and saving you studied in Chapter 10.

Here is an example of cost sharing for a health plan. Suppose that your plan has a $1,500 deductible, 20 percent coinsurance, and a $4,000 out-of-pocket maximum, and you receive a $10,000 bill for covered services. You pay the first $1,500. The remaining $8,500 is shared, so you pay 20 percent of it, which is $1,700, and the plan pays 80 percent, which is $6,800. Your total is $1,500 + $1,700 = $3,200, which is under the $4,000 limit, so the limit does not apply. If the bill had been $30,000, the 20 percent share would have brought your costs above the limit, and the plan would have covered the rest. Also remember that a policy has exclusions, things it does not cover. Read the exclusions before you need the policy, not after.

Health Insurance

Health care is the insurance purchase that matters most to most families, and the one with the largest costs. In 2025, the Census Bureau reported that about 26.7 million people in the United States, or 7.9 percent of the population, had no health insurance at any time during the year. Key Concepts

Auto, Home, Renters, and Life Insurance

Auto insurance has several parts. The most important is liability coverage, which pays for the injuries and property damage that you cause to other people. In California, the minimum liability limits for policies issued or renewed on or after January 1, 2025, are $30,000 for injury or death to one person, $60,000 for injury or death to all people in an accident, and $15,000 for property damage. These are called 30/60/15 limits, and the California Department of Insurance notes that they are only minimums. A serious accident can easily cost more, and the driver is responsible for the rest. Other parts of auto insurance are collision, which pays to repair your own car after a crash, comprehensive, which covers theft, fire, and other non-collision damage, and uninsured motorist coverage, which protects you if the other driver has no insurance. If you have a car loan, the lender will require collision and comprehensive coverage.

Renters insurance covers your belongings if they are stolen or damaged by a covered event such as a fire, and it includes liability coverage if someone is injured in your home. It does not cover the building, because the landlord is responsible for that, and it usually does not cover floods or earthquakes without a special policy. It is generally inexpensive compared with the value of the belongings it protects, and many landlords require it. Homeowners insurance covers the structure and belongings, and it protects the owner from liability. It is required by mortgage lenders, and its cost is part of the PITI payment you studied in Section 2. Standard homeowners policies do not cover floods and earthquakes, which require separate policies, so buyers in some parts of California may need to look at several kinds of coverage.

Life insurance pays money to a beneficiary when the insured person dies. It matters most for people who have dependents, such as children or a spouse, who rely on the person's income. Term life insurance covers a set period, such as 20 years, and has a low premium. Whole life and similar permanent policies last for a lifetime and build a cash value, but the premiums are much higher. Many consumer advocates suggest that most families who need life insurance start with term coverage. Disability insurance replaces part of your income if you cannot work because of illness or injury. Many people overlook it, though a worker is more likely to be disabled for a time than to die during the working years. Some employers offer it as a benefit.

No one carries every policy, and no one can insure against everything. The skill is to decide which risks are big enough to hurt you and unlikely enough that insurance is a good deal. A good rule is to insure against losses that you could not pay out of savings, and to self-insure against small ones. That is why people buy health and liability coverage but do not buy a policy to cover a $50 phone case. An extended warranty, which you studied in Section 1, is a form of insurance, and the same test applies: would the loss ruin you, and is the price fair?

Key Concepts

Adverse Selection and Moral Hazard

Insurance markets have two problems that economists study closely. Both arise because one side of the contract knows more than the other, a situation called asymmetric information. Adverse selection occurs when the people who are most likely to have losses are the most likely to buy insurance. Imagine that an insurer offers a health plan at an average price, without knowing who is sick. A person who knows that he will need expensive care will happily buy it, while a healthy person may decide that the price is not worth it. If healthy people drop out, the average cost of claims rises, and the insurer must raise the premium, which drives out still more healthy buyers. In the worst case, the market could break down. This is one reason that employers and governments often use groups: a large group of workers includes both healthy and sick people, so the pool stays balanced. It is also one reason that health insurance laws have required most people to be in the pool.

Moral hazard occurs when a person who is protected from a risk takes less care to avoid it. A driver with full coverage may park in a less safe place; a patient with no copay may seek more care than needed. The insurer cannot see every action, so it designs the policy to keep the policyholder's interests aligned with its own. Deductibles and coinsurance make you share in each loss; discounts reward safe drivers and homes with smoke detectors; and policies do not pay for losses caused on purpose. In the language of Chapter 1, these are incentives: people respond to them. Insurers also use underwriting, the process of evaluating the risk of each applicant, to set the price. Underwriting can include a driving record, the age of a roof, or a medical history, depending on the kind of insurance and on what the law allows.

The OpenStax Principles of Economics textbook uses these ideas to explain why insurance markets are regulated. In California, the Department of Insurance supervises insurers and handles consumer complaints, and the state sets rules for how insurers may set rates. A consumer who has a complaint about a claim can file one with the department. The key idea for consumers is that insurance is a bargain with both sides watching the other: you are asked to disclose facts honestly on your application, and the insurer is expected to pay valid claims promptly. Misstating facts, such as who drives the car or where it is kept, can void a policy when you most need it.

Ralph NaderThe Consumer Who Demanded Safety

Before the 1960s, car makers and most Americans treated crashes as the fault of careless drivers. Ralph Nader, a lawyer who had graduated from Princeton University and Harvard Law School, argued something different. In his 1965 book Unsafe at Any Speed, he claimed that the design of cars, not only the driver's behavior, determined whether people survived a crash, and that manufacturers had resisted spending money on safety features. The book drew national attention to a simple economic point: when a seller does not bear the full cost of the injuries its product causes, it has little reason to spend on safety.

In 1966, Congress passed the National Traffic and Motor Vehicle Safety Act, which authorized the federal government to set safety standards for new vehicles. Over the following decades, standards led to features that are now taken for granted, such as seat belts and stronger vehicle structures. Nader went on to found and support consumer organizations, including Public Citizen in 1971, that represent consumers in front of Congress and regulators. He also helped to found the Center for Auto Safety.

Nader's career is connected to this chapter in two ways. Insurance companies pay for crashes, so they have a financial interest in safer cars, and many insurers fund research on vehicle safety. And consumer protection depends on information and incentives: a buyer cannot judge a car's crash performance by looking at it. Critics of regulation argue that safety rules raise the price of vehicles and that drivers may take more risks when they feel safer, which is a form of moral hazard. Supporters answer that the lives saved are worth the cost. The debate is a good example of weighing costs and benefits.

THINKING ECONOMICALLYWhy might a seller spend too little on safety when buyers cannot judge it? How could a government rule or an insurer's pricing change the seller's incentives?

Shopping for insurance. Insurance is a product, and you can shop for it with the same tools as any other product. Ask for quotes from several insurers or from an independent agent who represents more than one company, and compare the same coverage and the same deductible so that you are comparing like with like. Ask about discounts: many insurers offer lower prices for safe-driver records, good grades for students, bundling a renters or homeowners policy with an auto policy, and safety features such as smoke detectors and anti-theft devices. Compare the company's record for paying claims and handling complaints, which the California Department of Insurance tracks.

Read the declarations page, which lists the coverage, the limits, the deductibles, the premium, and the people and property covered. Check that the information is correct. If your situation changes because you move, buy a car, or add a driver, tell your insurer. Pay your premium on time, because a policy that lapses can leave you uncovered, and for a driver a lapse can raise the price of the next policy. And remember that the cheapest policy is not always the best. A policy with a very low price may have low limits, high deductibles, or exclusions that matter when you need it.

Insurance can also teach a lesson about personal responsibility. Safe habits, such as driving carefully, locking doors, and keeping smoke alarms working, lower the chance of a loss for you and for the whole pool, and they keep your premium lower over time. In that way, insurance connects your choices with the cost of risk. The same idea applies in the next chapter, where you will learn how a paycheck is divided among taxes, benefits, and take-home pay, including the payroll deductions for insurance.

Is College Worth the Price?

Education may be the biggest purchase a young person ever makes, and it is unlike a car or a house, because its main payoff is a stream of future earnings that no one can see yet. This case study asks you to weigh the evidence: what the data say about earnings, what borrowing costs, what a made-up student could do, and what different people argue.

Background

An Investment With an Uncertain Payoff

Economists call spending on education an investment in human capital. You give up money and time now, which are opportunity costs, in the hope of higher earnings, better job security, and more choices later. In Chapter 9 you saw that wages depend on the supply of workers with a skill and the demand for that skill. Workers with more education, on average, supply skills that employers want, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) reports that they earn more and are less likely to be unemployed.

But an average is not a promise. Earnings depend on the field of study, on the job market, and on whether a student finishes. The price also differs: a year of community college costs far less than a year at a university, and a student who borrows to pay will owe interest. A decision that looks easy for one student may be risky for another. That is why financial educators say that the question is not simply whether college pays, but which education, at what cost, financed how.

Chapter 12 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Smart Consumer Decisions
A consumer's choices shape what markets produce, and every purchase has an opportunity cost. The smart-buyer cycle is: define the need, research, compare total cost, decide, and review. Unit price and cost per use make comparisons fair. Sellers use techniques such as anchoring and urgency. Contracts bind both sides; warranties promise repair or replacement; the FTC, the CFPB, and California agencies enforce consumer protections, including the lemon law and the used-car cancellation option.
Section 2 · Housing, Vehicles, and College
Renting offers flexibility and a lease; buying builds equity but requires a down payment and added costs. A mortgage payment includes principal, interest, taxes, and insurance, and a higher rate raises the payment: the 30-year rate was 7.28 percent on November 19, 2026. A vehicle's total cost of ownership includes depreciation, fuel, insurance, maintenance, and interest. Education is an investment in human capital; community college, apprenticeships, grants, and federal loans (6.52 to 9.07 percent) are ways to pay.
Section 3 · Insurance and Risk
Insurance pools risk: many pay premiums and a few receive payments for losses. The deductible, copay, coinsurance, and out-of-pocket maximum divide costs between you and the insurer. Employer health coverage in 2026 averaged $9,804 for single coverage and $28,303 for family coverage. California requires minimum auto liability limits of 30/60/15. Adverse selection and moral hazard are problems of unequal information, and insurers respond with underwriting, deductibles, and discounts.
Case Study · Is College Worth the Price?
Workers with more education earn more and have lower unemployment on average: the median weekly pay was $1,578 for a bachelor's degree holder and $966 for a high school graduate in 2025. But the price, the interest on loans, and the risk of not finishing are real. The best choice depends on the program, the cost, the financing, and the pathway, and the total-cost approach helps compare them.
