Concept Review

In Chapter 4 you learned that demand is the willingness and ability to buy and slopes downward. In Chapter 5 you learned that supply is the willingness and ability to produce and slopes upward. In this chapter you will see what happens when the two meet: the price at which the amount buyers want matches the amount sellers offer, and what happens when something moves either curve or when government tries to hold a price in place.

Chapter 6 Key Concept

Equilibrium is the situation in which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. The equilibrium price is the price at which that balance occurs. At any higher price there is a surplus, and sellers cut prices. At any lower price there is a shortage, and buyers bid prices up. A change in demand or supply moves the market to a new equilibrium.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine you are selling a used phone online. You list it at $400, and no one writes. You drop it to $300, and a few people ask. At $200, three people want it, and you can tell that you priced it too low, so you raise your price. Buyers and a seller can find a price both accept without anyone telling them what it should be. A market works the same way on a bigger scale. By the end of this chapter you will be able to find an equilibrium on a graph, predict how it changes when demand or supply shifts, explain how prices carry information and incentives, and judge what happens when a government sets a maximum or minimum price.

Case Study Preview

Between 2022 and 2025, the price of a dozen eggs in the United States more than tripled and then fell again. A disease among hens and the way that people shop for eggs both played a part. At the end of this chapter, you will examine the markets for eggs and use supply and demand to explain what moved the price.

Key Concepts

Where Demand and Supply Meet

Chapter 4 examined buyers alone. Chapter 5 examined sellers alone. Neither tells you the price at which a product will actually sell, because that price depends on both. A market is a place or system, such as a grocery store, a farmers market, or a website, where buyers and sellers exchange. The two curves can now be put on the same graph.

Figure 6.1 shows the market for navel oranges, with the demand and supply schedules you met in earlier chapters. At $10 per box, buyers want 500 boxes but growers supply only 100. At $20, growers supply 600 but buyers want none. At just one price, $14, the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied: 300 boxes. The situation in which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied is called market equilibrium. The price at which this happens is the equilibrium price (here, $14), and the quantity bought and sold is the equilibrium quantity (here, 300 boxes). On a graph, equilibrium is the point where the demand curve and the supply curve cross.

The word equilibrium comes from Latin words meaning “equal balance.” At the equilibrium price, every buyer willing to pay that price finds a seller, and every seller willing to sell at that price finds a buyer. For that reason it is also called the market-clearing price: the market is cleared because nothing is left over and no one is left waiting. Once a market is at equilibrium, no buyer or seller has a reason to change anything, so it tends to stay there until something else changes.

Key Concepts

Reading a Market Schedule

Every graph in this section starts with a table. A market schedule lists, for each possible price, the quantity that buyers demand and the quantity that sellers supply. To find equilibrium without drawing anything, read down the table one row at a time and ask the same question in each row: Is the quantity demanded larger than, smaller than, or equal to the quantity supplied? In the first row of the table for Figure 6.1, at $10, buyers want 500 boxes and growers offer 100, so the quantity demanded is larger. That row shows a shortage of 400. At $12, the shortage is 200. At $14, the two quantities match, so that row is the equilibrium. At $16, the quantity supplied is larger, and the row shows a surplus. Notice the pattern: as you move down the table, the shortage shrinks, disappears, and then turns into a surplus. The row where the sign flips is where equilibrium sits.

Be careful with the vocabulary. In a market schedule, quantity demanded refers to one price at a time, a single number in a single row. Demand refers to the whole column: the entire relationship between price and quantity demanded. Changing the price moves you from one row to another, which is a change in quantity demanded. Changing something else, such as income or tastes, rewrites the whole column, which is a change in demand. Chapter 4 explained this difference, and it matters here because the two are easy to mix up. A rise in the price of oranges never changes the demand for oranges. It only changes the quantity demanded.

Key Concepts

Gains from Trade at Equilibrium

Why do buyers and sellers go along with the equilibrium price? Because each side gains. Think about the buyers in the orange market. Some would have paid as much as $18 for a box, because they love oranges or are making juice for a party. They pay only $14, and the $4 difference is a bargain they get to keep. Other buyers would have paid $14.50, and they keep a few cents. Every buyer whose willingness to pay is above $14 comes away with some value left over. On the other side of the market, a grower whose cost of producing a box is $10 sells it for $14 and keeps the $4 difference as profit. A grower whose cost is $13.75, perhaps because the trees are on poor soil, earns only a quarter. Both sides gain from trading, and the lowest-cost growers gain the most.

Equilibrium, then, is about efficiency more than fairness. A market at equilibrium makes all of the trades that both sides are willing to make. But the result may not feel fair. A family with a low income may be unable to pay $14 for a box even though they would enjoy the oranges as much as a wealthier family. Economists therefore separate two questions: Is the market using resources efficiently, and is the outcome fair? The first can be tested with graphs and data. The second also involves values, and reasonable people answer it differently. Many disagreements about price controls in Section 3 come from the difference between these two questions.

Key Concepts

Surpluses and Shortages

What if the price is not at equilibrium? Suppose growers hope for a high price and set it at $18. At that price growers supply 500 boxes, but buyers want only 100. The extra 400 boxes are a surplus: a situation in which the quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded at a given price. A surplus is also called excess supply. Boxes pile up unsold, and growers have an incentive to cut the price to attract buyers and to supply less. As the price falls from $18, the quantity demanded rises and the quantity supplied falls, and the surplus shrinks until the price reaches $14.

Now suppose the price is $10. Buyers want 500 boxes, and growers supply only 100. The 400 boxes that buyers cannot find are a shortage: a situation in which the quantity demanded is greater than the quantity supplied at a given price. A shortage is also called excess demand. Buyers compete to get the few boxes available, and some offer more. Sellers see this and raise the price. As the price rises, the quantity demanded falls and the quantity supplied rises, until the market reaches $14.

This is the key to how a market finds the price without anyone in charge. It is the process described in Standard 12.2.5: competition among buyers and sellers determines a market price. Competition among sellers pushes a price down when there is a surplus. Competition among buyers pushes a price up when there is a shortage. The market moves toward equilibrium from both directions. Economists call this "the invisible hand," a phrase first used by Adam Smith, whom you met in Chapter 1.

Key Concepts

Prices Do Not Adjust Instantly

In the model, a surplus disappears quickly because sellers cut the price. In real life, the adjustment takes time. A grocery store does not change the price of a box of cereal every hour. A grower who agrees to deliver oranges to a packer at a set price cannot change that price in the middle of the season. Printing new menus costs money, and many customers dislike prices that change without warning. Economists describe a sticky price as a price that adjusts slowly when demand or supply changes. When prices are sticky, a surplus or a shortage can last for days, weeks, or longer before the price catches up.

Some markets adjust fast, and others adjust slowly. The prices of crude oil and many farm commodities are set by exchanges, where buyers and sellers post offers all day and the price changes in seconds. Online marketplaces and ride-hailing apps can change prices every few minutes. An auction is the fastest of all: the auctioneer calls out higher prices until only one buyer remains, which reveals the market price on the spot. In the middle are grocery stores and restaurants, which change prices a few times a year. At the slow end are rents under a one-year lease and wages in a job with an annual raise.

While the price adjusts slowly, quantities and the shopping experience adjust fast. A store with a shortage shows empty shelves, limits how many items one customer can buy, or tells shoppers to come back next week. A store with a surplus puts the extra goods on sale, stores them, or throws them out. The day after Halloween, stores mark unsold candy down by half, because candy left on the shelf has become a surplus. These responses are signs that a market is out of equilibrium and moving toward it. Looking for them is a good way to tell whether a price is too high or too low.

Key Concepts

When Demand or Supply Changes

A market at equilibrium stays there only as long as nothing changes. In Chapters 4 and 5 you learned the determinants that shift demand and supply. When one of them changes, a curve shifts, and the market finds a new equilibrium. You can predict the result with a three-step method: (1) decide whether the event affects demand or supply; (2) decide whether it shifts the curve right (an increase) or left (a decrease); and (3) find the new crossing point.

Figure 6.4 shows an increase in demand. Suppose a heat wave in Bakersfield makes people want more fresh fruit, or a news report says oranges are healthy. Demand shifts to the right, from D1 to D2. At the old price of $14, buyers now want 500 boxes but growers supply only 300, so there is a shortage of 200. Buyers bid the price up, and growers respond by supplying more. The new equilibrium is $16 and 400 boxes. A rise in demand raises both the equilibrium price and the equilibrium quantity. A decrease in demand does the reverse.

The same method works for supply. If an increase in supply (see Chapter 5) shifts the supply curve to the right, the equilibrium price falls and the equilibrium quantity rises. A decrease in supply, such as a freeze that destroys part of the orange crop, shifts the curve left, which raises the equilibrium price and lowers the equilibrium quantity. The figure in Economics Essentials summarizes the four cases. The Case Study will show that the price of eggs rose sharply when the supply of eggs fell.

Key Concepts

A Decrease in Supply: The Freeze of 2007

Start again from the original orange market, with an equilibrium of $14 and 300 boxes, and suppose a hard freeze strikes. This has actually happened in the Central Valley. In January 2007, a long stretch of freezing weather hit California's farm country. The National Weather Service office in Hanford reported that Bakersfield fell to 23°F on January 14, and it estimated the damage to crops across the state at almost $1.3 billion. Early estimates put citrus losses at 50 to 70 percent of the fruit still on the trees. Fruit that has frozen cannot be sold at a normal price, so the supply of oranges decreased. In the model, the supply curve shifts to the left, from S1 to S2 in Figure 6.5.

Here is the logic. The demand curve has not moved, because people's tastes and incomes have not changed. But at every price, growers now have fewer boxes to offer. At the old price of $14, growers supply only 100 boxes while buyers still want 300. The shortage of 200 boxes makes buyers compete, and the price rises. As the price climbs along the demand curve, some buyers give up on oranges. As it climbs along the new supply curve, growers whose fruit survived, and growers in other regions, have a reason to send more boxes to market. The new equilibrium is $16 and 200 boxes: the price rises by $2 and the quantity falls by 100.

Notice two things. First, the quantity falls by less than the supply curve shifted. The curve moved left by 200 boxes at every price, but the equilibrium quantity fell by only 100. The rest of the loss was made up by a movement along the new supply curve, because the higher price rewarded the growers who still had fruit. Second, the price carried the news. A grocery buyer in another state did not need to read a weather report. The higher price was the message, and it told that buyer to economize and told other growers to ship more. This is the price system at work, and Section 2 looks at it in detail.

Key Concepts

When Both Curves Shift

So far only one curve has moved at a time. Real events often move both. Suppose a freeze cuts the supply of oranges in January just as a news story about vitamin C and winter colds increases the demand for them. In Figure 6.6, demand shifts to the right, from D1 to D2, and supply shifts to the left, from S1 to S2. Both shifts push in the same direction on the price: more demand raises it, and less supply raises it. The new equilibrium price is $18, up from $14. This result is certain, however large the two shifts are.

The quantity is a different story. The rise in demand tends to increase the quantity, while the fall in supply tends to decrease it. The two effects pull in opposite directions, and the result depends on which shift is larger. In the figure, the demand curve moves right by 200 boxes and the supply curve moves left by 200 boxes, so the effects cancel and the quantity stays at 300. If the demand shift had been larger, the quantity would have risen. If the supply shift had been larger, it would have fallen. Without numbers, the honest answer is that the change in quantity is uncertain.

A short rule helps: when both curves shift, one result is certain and the other is not. Work it out by checking each shift separately. Ask what the demand shift alone does to the price and the quantity. Then ask what the supply shift alone does. When the two effects on the price agree, the price result is certain. When they disagree, the result depends on size. The same rule covers the other combinations. If demand falls and supply rises, the price falls for certain and the quantity is uncertain. If demand and supply both rise, the quantity rises for certain and the price is uncertain. If both fall, the quantity falls for certain and the price is uncertain.

Key Concepts

Real Markets Move Too: The Price of Gasoline

The three-step method works on real markets. Figure 6.8 shows the average price of a gallon of regular gasoline in the United States every month from January 2019 to October 2026, using data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. The line has three large swings, and each one can be explained as a shift of demand or supply. In the spring of 2020, businesses closed and people stayed home, so there was far less driving. The demand for gasoline decreased, the demand curve shifted to the left, and the price fell from $2.57 in January 2020 to $1.88 in April, the lowest point on the graph.

The second swing runs the other way. As the economy reopened, driving came back and demand recovered. In 2022 the supply side added a second push. Russia invaded Ukraine in February 2022, and the war disrupted world oil markets, which decreased the supply of fuel. With demand recovering and supply tight, the price rose to $5.06 in June 2022, the highest on the graph. That is an increase of about 170 percent from the low in April 2020. After June 2022 the price fell, as supply adjusted and as the high price led some drivers to cut back.

The third swing happened in 2026. According to the U.S. Energy Information Administration, military action in the Middle East beginning on February 28, 2026, and the closing of the Strait of Hormuz to most shipping, cut the supply of oil and fuel reaching world markets. Brent crude oil, the world benchmark, began the year at $61 per barrel and finished March at $118. The average price of regular gasoline in the United States rose from $2.96 in January to $4.65 in May, an increase of about 57 percent. Notice how quickly the price moved. Gasoline is a product that most drivers cannot easily stop using, so the quantity demanded changes little when the price jumps. A price spike of this size shows how strongly inelastic demand magnifies the effect of a supply shock, an idea explored on the next page.

Key Concepts

Elasticity and the Size of a Price Change

The size of a price change depends on more than the size of the shift. It also depends on how responsive buyers and sellers are, which is the idea of elasticity from Chapters 4 and 5. Figure 6.9 shows the same decrease in supply in two markets. In both, the equilibrium before the shift is $10 and 50 thousand units, and in both, the supply curve moves left by the same amount. Only the demand curves differ. In panel A, demand is inelastic: buyers do not cut back much when the price rises, so the demand curve is steep. In panel B, demand is elastic: buyers cut back sharply, so the demand curve is flat.

The results are very different. With inelastic demand, the price rises from $10 to $12.40, which is 24 percent, and the quantity falls by only 6 thousand units, which is 12 percent. With elastic demand, the price rises only to $11, which is 10 percent, but the quantity falls by 20 thousand units, which is 40 percent. When buyers have few substitutes, as with gasoline or a dozen eggs, a decrease in supply shows up mostly as a higher price. When buyers have many substitutes, as with a particular brand of orange juice, it shows up mostly as fewer units sold, because buyers just switch.

Supply elasticity matters too. In the short run, a farmer cannot quickly produce more eggs or oranges, so the supply curve is steep. When demand then increases, the price has to rise a lot to ration the limited quantity. Over a longer period, farmers plant more trees and build more barns, the supply curve becomes flatter, and prices come back down. This is one reason that prices of farm products spike and then fall back. The Case Study at the end of the chapter shows exactly this pattern in the market for eggs.

Key Concepts

A Checklist for Predicting Changes

You can now use the model to analyze almost any news story about prices. A five-step checklist keeps the process organized. First, identify the market: name the good, and think of its price and quantity. Second, identify the event and decide whether it changes demand or supply. Events that change tastes, incomes, the prices of related goods, expectations, or the number of buyers shift demand. Events that change input costs, technology, taxes or subsidies, expectations, the number of sellers, or the weather shift supply. Third, decide the direction: right for an increase, left for a decrease. Fourth, find the new equilibrium and read off the changes in price and quantity. Fifth, check your answer against common sense and, if possible, against real data.

Certain mistakes appear again and again. The first is shifting a curve because the price changed. A change in the price moves you along a curve and never shifts it. The second is confusing a shortage with scarcity. Scarcity is the permanent fact that wants exceed resources. A shortage is the temporary situation of a price that is too low. The third is forgetting that both curves can shift, and the fourth is assuming that equilibrium is the same thing as fairness, which the earlier part of this section showed is not true. A fifth mistake is giving a story without a curve: saying that “prices rose because of inflation,” for example, without saying which curve moved.

EXAMPLEA Bumper Crop of Grapes

Grapes are one of Kern County's leading crops, and the 2024 county crop report ranked them second by value. Suppose a year of ideal weather gives growers a bumper crop. The supply of grapes increases, and the supply curve shifts to the right. Demand has not changed. At the old price, growers now have more grapes than buyers want, so there is a surplus. Packers and wineries bid less aggressively, and the price falls. As it falls, buyers purchase more, so the market settles at a lower price and a higher quantity.

Because most buyers do not eat many more grapes just because the price falls, demand for grapes is fairly inelastic. A large increase in supply may therefore cut the price so much that growers' total revenue falls even though they harvested more. A good harvest is not always good news for farmers. GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEWhen Rice Became Scarce in Japan

Rice is the staple food of Japan, and for years its price was stable. Then, in 2024 and 2025, it jumped. Japanese government data show that the price of rice in April 2025 was 98.4 percent higher than in April 2024. Analysts pointed to several causes: a poor harvest after the unusually hot summer of 2023, a recovery in tourism and restaurant business that raised demand, and panic buying after a rumor of a major earthquake. Some shoppers switched to imported rice. The government tried to bring the price down by releasing rice from its emergency reserves. Whatever the exact mix of causes, the pattern is the one you learned in this section. A decrease in supply and an increase in demand both push the price up, and the higher price signals buyers to economize and signals sellers, including importers, to bring in more rice.

Key Concepts

Prices Reflect Scarcity

A price is more than the amount on a tag. It carries information. When a good becomes harder to get, its price tends to rise, and when it becomes easier to get, its price tends to fall. In this way a price reflects the relative scarcity of a good compared with the wants of buyers. A good that is scarce relative to the demand for it commands a high price. A good that is plentiful relative to demand sells for a low price.

Figure 6.10 shows a real example. The cattle herd in the United States has been shrinking for several years. On January 1, 2026, the U.S. Department of Agriculture counted 86.2 million cattle and calves, and 27.6 million beef cows, down 1 percent from a year earlier. Fewer cattle means less beef supplied. Over the same period, the average retail price of a pound of ground beef rose from $3.80 in January 2019 to $6.75 in January 2026. Buyers saw the higher price and bought less beef or switched to chicken or beans. Ranchers saw the same price and had a stronger reason to keep and breed cows. The price told both groups something about scarcity without anyone sending a message.

This is the allocative function of prices. To allocate means to distribute resources among competing uses. In a market economy, no committee decides how much beef, how many cars, or how many tomatoes to produce. The allocative function of prices is the way that prices direct resources toward the uses that buyers value most and away from the uses they value least. A high price pulls land, labor, and capital into producing that good. A low price pushes them away. Standard 12.2.4 asks you to understand how prices reflect relative scarcity and perform this function.

Key Concepts

Prices Carry Knowledge That No One Person Has

In 1945, the economist Friedrich Hayek published an essay called “The Use of Knowledge in Society.” His question was how a society can use knowledge that is scattered among millions of people and that no single person possesses. His answer was the price system. He gave an example using tin. Suppose that somewhere in the world a new use for tin appears, or a source of tin is cut off. Almost nobody who uses tin needs to know which of these happened. They need to know only that tin has become more valuable and should be used more sparingly, and the rising price tells them. A manufacturer looks for a substitute, and a miner opens a new shaft, and neither knows why the price moved.

Prices therefore carry two things at once: knowledge and incentives. The knowledge is that a good has become scarcer or more plentiful. The incentive is a reason to act on that knowledge, because the price changes what is profitable. A signal without an incentive would be ignored, and an incentive without a signal would point in the wrong direction. When the price system works well, buyers and sellers who know nothing about each other's reasons make choices that fit together. When a price is held away from its equilibrium by law or by some other force, the knowledge it carries becomes distorted, which is the subject of Section 3.

To see the allocative function at work, look at what is grown in Kern County. In 2024, the county's crop report counted a gross crop value of $7.96 billion, down 8 percent from the record $8.63 billion of 2023. The most valuable crop was citrus at $1.31 billion, which passed grapes ($1.18 billion) for the first time in decades. Carrots came third at $1.01 billion, almonds were close to $800 million, and pistachios were $723 million. These are gross values, which means that they are measured before the costs of production and marketing are subtracted. Figure 6.6.12 shows the top five. Kern's farmers are not told by the government how many acres of each crop to plant. They decide, and the prices they expect help to guide them.

Pistachios show how an allocation can change over time. According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture's Economic Research Service, California's pistachio acreage nearly quintupled over the two decades before 2024. Pistachios passed walnuts in 2021 to become the state's second-largest tree nut crop by bearing acres, and in 2024 California had about 488,000 bearing acres of pistachios compared with 370,000 of walnuts and 1.38 million of almonds (Figure 6.6.13). Walnut acreage fell for two years in a row, the first time since 1951. Many things influence these choices, including water, soil, and the cost of trees. But growers who see that a crop earns more than the alternatives have an incentive to shift land, water, and labor toward it.

Two cautions are important. The first is that the farm sector adjusts slowly. A tree crop takes years to bear nuts, so a grower plants on the basis of the price expected years from now, and the expectation can be wrong. That is one reason that crop prices swing between high and low. The second is that other forces matter besides prices, such as access to water, which in California is limited and regulated. Prices guide the use of resources, but they do not act alone. This is why a model of prices is a start rather than the whole story, and why the next chapters turn to competition, firms, and labor.

Key Concepts

Signals, Incentives, and Rationing

Economists call the message carried by a price a price signal. A price signal tells people what to do without anyone giving orders. When the price of a good rises, the signal tells buyers to economize and tells sellers to produce more. When the price falls, the signal tells buyers that the good is cheaper and tells sellers to make less. In a market economy, millions of people respond to signals from prices every day.

Prices also serve as a way of rationing. In any market, buyers want more than there is available, which is the problem of scarcity. Something has to decide who gets the good. When goods are sold at a market price, the rationing function of prices decides: those who are willing and able to pay the price receive the good, and those who are not do not. Other methods of rationing exist, such as first come, first served, lotteries, or ration coupons. Section 3 considers what happens when government sets the price so low that it cannot do the job.

Because prices change quickly, they can coordinate people who never meet. A Bakersfield grocer who orders more strawberries because the price rose is responding to a signal that began with a freeze in Mexico or a rise in demand in New York. The grocer does not need to know the reason. Nobel Prize–winning economist Friedrich Hayek argued that this is one of the most important features of a market: it spreads knowledge that no single person could ever gather.

Key Concepts

Many Ways to Ration

Every society rations scarce goods, because there is never enough of what everyone wants. A market economy rations mostly with prices, but there are other methods, and each has its own strengths and weaknesses. First come, first served gives the good to the people who arrive first. It is simple, but it rewards people who have time to wait, as in the gasoline lines of the 1970s. A lottery gives everyone with a ticket an equal chance. It feels fair, but it ignores how much people value the good, so a winner who does not want the prize may sell it to someone who does. Rationing by coupon gives each household a fixed number of coupons, as in World War II, when ration books covered sugar, meat, and gasoline. This can limit how much a family may buy, but the government must print the coupons, and people often trade them illegally.

Rationing by price gives the good to those who are both willing and able to pay. It needs no ticket office, no lines, and no officials. Its great strength is that it allocates the good to the buyers who value it most and sends a signal to producers to supply more. Its weakness is that people with a low income have less ability to pay, even when their needs are great. Economists recognize this and often respond with help for low-income households, such as cash assistance or food benefits, rather than by changing the price itself. That keeps the signal intact while helping the people who cannot pay.

In practice, the methods are often combined. A popular concert may sell tickets at a stated price until they run out, which is price rationing with a first-come, first-served element. A hospital may serve patients in order of medical need rather than ability to pay, a choice that society has made for reasons of values. A school may hold a lottery for places in a popular program. When you hear that a good is being “rationed,” ask what method is being used and who gains or loses from it. Price is only one answer to the problem of scarcity, but it is the one that works most automatically.

Key Concepts

Profit and the Entrepreneur

Signals have little effect unless people have an incentive to act on them. In a market economy, the incentive for businesses is profit. An entrepreneur is a person who organizes resources to produce goods or services, takes the risk of a loss, and hopes to earn a profit. The profit motive is the desire to earn a profit, and it is the reason that entrepreneurs respond to price signals. If the price of a good is above its cost of production, the profit attracts producers. If the price falls below cost, losses push producers out.

This is the idea in Standard 12.2.8: profit is the incentive to entrepreneurs in a market economy. A taquería owner in Delano who sees that customers want late-night food, and who opens a food truck to serve them, is responding to a signal and a possible reward. If customers do come, the truck earns a profit and others may copy it. If not, the owner loses money and must change or close. Either way, resources have been redirected toward what customers value.

Profit is not the only goal people pursue, and markets do not always produce results that people consider fair. But the combination of price signals and the profit motive explains why a store in Shafter has strawberries in February that were grown in another country the day before. The next chapters on competition and business organization build on this idea.

Key Concepts

Revenue, Cost, and Profit

Profit is easy to define and sometimes hard to measure. A firm's total revenue is the amount it receives from selling its product, which is the price multiplied by the quantity sold. Its total cost is everything it pays to produce that output. Profit is total revenue minus total cost. When revenue is greater than cost, the firm earns a profit. When cost is greater than revenue, it has a loss. When they are equal, the firm is at its break-even point, where it earns just enough to cover all costs.

Figure 6.6.15 shows a hypothetical food truck that sells meals for $10 each. The truck has costs that do not change with the number of meals, such as a $1,800 weekly payment for the truck lease, a permit, and insurance. It also has costs that grow with every meal, such as $4 in ingredients. At 100 meals a week the revenue is $1,000 but the cost is $2,200, so the truck loses $1,200. At 300 meals the revenue and cost are both $3,000, the break-even point. At 500 meals the revenue is $5,000 and the cost is $3,800, so the profit is $1,200. Each additional meal adds $10 to revenue and $4 to cost, so profit rises by $6 for every meal sold past the break-even point.

This is the incentive that drives the price system. If the price of meals rises, the break-even point falls, and more owners will want to open trucks. If the price falls, the break-even point rises, and some trucks will lose money. An entrepreneur who can predict the price, control the cost, and find enough customers earns the profit. The profit is not a gift. It is a payment for taking the risk of the lease, for the idea, and for work. It also contains information. A high profit says that customers value what the business provides more than the cost of the resources that it uses, which attracts others to enter.

Key Concepts

When Prices Fall Below Cost: Losses and Exit

If profit is a signal to come in, a loss is a signal to leave. Suppose the price of meals at a food truck park falls because several new trucks open and customers are split among them. A truck that cannot cover its costs loses money week after week. The owner has three choices: cut costs, find customers elsewhere, or close. A firm that closes frees the truck, the cook's time, and the lot space for other uses, which is the allocative function working in reverse. The resources move from a use that customers value less than it costs to a use where they are worth more.

Losses can seem harsh, but they do important work. Without them, a market could not remove businesses that make things that buyers no longer want. The pistachio grower who plants a good crop in the right place earns a profit, and the one who plants on poor land or at the wrong time may lose money and move on. A person who loses a job when a business closes bears a real cost, which is one reason that societies have unemployment benefits and job training programs. Economics describes why exit happens and why it is useful in general. It does not say that it is painless for the people involved.

There is also an opportunity cost in staying. When a business owner works 60 hours a week to earn $20,000 of profit, that owner gives up the pay of a job that might have paid $40,000. Economists therefore say that profit should be measured after subtracting the value of the owner's own time and money invested in the business. A business that earns less than the owner could earn elsewhere is, in economic terms, not earning a true profit even if the checkbook is positive. This is another way that the price system pulls resources toward their best use.

Key Concepts

Competition Keeps Profit in Check

A high profit does not stay high for long when others are free to enter the market. Imagine that one food truck in Shafter earns a large profit by selling birria tacos. Other cooks see the profit and notice the lines, and some of them open trucks nearby. As more trucks enter, the supply of tacos increases. Customers now have more choices, so the trucks must lower prices or offer more to keep them. Profit per truck falls. Entry continues until the profit in this business is no higher than in other businesses of the same risk. This is the process of competition, which Standard 12.2.3 and Standard 12.2.5 describe.

The same process works in the other direction. If many trucks leave because of losses, the supply decreases, and the remaining ones can charge more. In this way, profit works like a thermostat. When it is high, it draws in producers, which brings prices down. When it is low or negative, it pushes producers out, which lets prices rise. The system does not hold profits at exactly zero, since risk and skill differ among owners, but it tends to prevent very high or very low profits from lasting when entry and exit are easy.

Vernon L. SmithTesting the Market in a Classroom

How do you know that supply and demand actually bring a market to equilibrium? In 1956, a young economics professor at Purdue University named Vernon Smith decided to try it. In his classroom, he gave each student a card. Students who were assigned to be buyers received a card that told them the highest price they would pay. Students who were sellers received a card that told them the lowest price they would accept. Then he let the students trade, calling out offers.

No one was told what the equilibrium price was. Each student knew only his or her own card. Yet after a few rounds of trading, the prices students agreed on moved close to the equilibrium price that a supply and demand graph predicted. Smith had shown that a market can find equilibrium even when no participant can see the whole picture.

Smith spent his career building on this idea. The experiments he and others developed became a tool for testing economic theories, designing auctions, and studying how rules affect prices. In 2002, the Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences awarded him the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for establishing laboratory experiments as a tool in economic analysis. Key Concepts

Why Governments Set Prices

Sometimes people do not like the price that a market produces. Renters may find rents too high. Workers may think their wages are too low. Farmers may find that the price of their crop will not cover their costs. In these cases, governments may step in with a price control: a legal limit on how high or how low a price may go. Standard 12.2.6 asks you to describe the effect of price controls on buyers and sellers.

There are two kinds. A price ceiling is a maximum price that sellers may legally charge. A price floor is a minimum price that buyers must pay. Both interfere with the signals and incentives that you studied in Section 2. Economists agree that price controls have consequences for buyers and sellers, and they study these carefully, but they disagree about how large the consequences are in particular cases and about whether the goals of the policy justify them.

Remember the logic of equilibrium. A control matters only if it prevents the market price from reaching equilibrium. A ceiling set above the equilibrium price has no effect, because the market price is already lower. A floor set below the equilibrium price has no effect for the same reason. A control has an effect when it is binding: a ceiling below equilibrium or a floor above it.

Key Concepts

A Short History of Price Controls

Price controls are not new. Governments have tried to set prices for thousands of years, usually in times of war, shortage, or high prices. In the United States, the best-known case is World War II. The war shifted resources from civilian goods to weapons and left many families with more income and less to buy. To keep prices from surging, the Office of Price Administration capped the prices of most goods and issued ration books for scarce items such as sugar, meat, tires, and gasoline. Most of these controls ended in 1946. New York City's system of rent regulation also traces back to this period.

The 1970s brought another round. In August 1971, President Richard Nixon froze most wages and prices for 90 days in an attempt to slow inflation, and controls on the price of domestic oil stayed in place for years. Then, on October 19, 1973, the Organization of Arab Petroleum Exporting Countries (OAPEC) placed an embargo on oil shipments to the United States. According to the Federal Reserve's history of the episode, the price of oil rose from $2.90 per barrel before the embargo to $11.65 in January 1974, nearly a four-fold increase. The embargo was officially lifted in March 1974.

Because controls held gasoline prices down while supply fell, the quantity demanded was greater than the quantity supplied, and drivers waited in long lines at gas stations. Some states, including Oregon, allowed buyers to purchase gasoline only on odd or even days according to the last digit of the license plate, as the photograph in this section shows. Many economists have pointed to the gasoline lines as an example of how a ceiling below equilibrium creates a shortage. The last federal controls on oil prices were removed in January 1981. The history offers a pattern. Governments set controls to protect buyers in a crisis, and the controls often outlast the crisis and cause problems of their own.

Key Concepts

Price Ceilings and Shortages

Figure 6.16 uses a hypothetical market for apartments. Without a ceiling, the equilibrium rent is $1,400 per month, and 300 apartments are rented. Suppose the city passes a rent control law that sets the maximum rent at $1,000. At that rent, renters want 500 apartments, but landlords offer only 100. The result is a shortage of 400 apartments.

Why does the shortage persist? A ceiling stops the price from rising, which is the signal that would draw more apartments onto the market. The low rent lowers the reward for landlords to maintain apartments, build new ones, or rent them out at all, so the quantity supplied is smaller. Meanwhile the quantity demanded is larger, because the low price attracts more renters. When the price cannot ration the apartments, something else must. People may wait on long lists, pay extra payments “under the table,” or rely on connections. Buyers who find a unit pay less. Those who do not, and those who would have supplied more, bear the costs.

A thriving illegal black market can appear when a ceiling holds a price too low. In a black market, goods are sold illegally at prices above the legal limit. Black markets show that the demand and the supply are still there, even when the law says the price cannot move.

Key Concepts

Beyond the Shortage: Quality, Lines, and Long-Run Effects

A shortage is the first and most obvious effect of a binding ceiling, but it is not the only one. When sellers cannot raise the price, they look for other ways to benefit, and buyers look for other ways to compete. Landlords may stop making repairs, since tenants who cannot find another unit will stay anyway. Sellers can reduce the quality of the good, shrink its size, or cut service. Buyers may wait in lines, pay under the table, or rely on friends. Because many renters want each unit, a landlord can pick among them, and a landlord who wishes to discriminate has less to lose by doing so. In each case, the price no longer does the rationing, and a less visible cost takes its place.

Time also matters. In the short run, supply is steep, or inelastic. Apartments cannot be built overnight, so the quantity that landlords supply changes little when the rent is capped, and the shortage is relatively small. Over years, supply becomes more elastic. Landlords sell buildings, convert apartments to other uses, or defer new building, and developers choose projects in other places. Figure 6.6.17 shows the same $1,000 ceiling in two time periods. In the short run, the shortage is 240 apartments. In the long run, when supply is flatter, it grows to 400. This is why a rent ceiling can look harmless at first and cause bigger problems later.

Key Concepts

Rent Control in California

California has some of the highest housing costs in the nation, and rent is a common topic of political debate. State law sets the limits on what cities may do. The Costa-Hawkins Rental Housing Act of 1995 prevents local governments from controlling the rent on single-family homes, on apartments built since 1995, and on units that have a new tenant, which lets landlords raise the rent between tenants. In 2019, the legislature passed the Tenant Protection Act, known as AB 1482. It limits annual rent increases on many older apartments to 5 percent plus local inflation, with a maximum of 10 percent, and it is scheduled to expire in 2030. Several California cities, including Los Angeles and San Francisco, also have their own rent ordinances.

Voters have been asked to expand local rent control three times in recent years. In 2018 and 2020 they rejected statewide ballot measures that would have changed Costa-Hawkins. In November 2024 they rejected Proposition 33, which would have repealed Costa-Hawkins entirely and allowed cities to set more kinds of rent limits. About 62 percent of voters opposed the measure. Supporters said that rent limits would protect families from being forced out of their communities. Opponents said that the measure would reduce the supply of housing. The arguments follow the logic of the model: the benefit goes to renters in the controlled units, and the cost may fall on future renters and on the housing supply.

What does evidence say? One of the most careful studies looked at San Francisco, where a change in law in 1994 extended rent control to some buildings but not to similar ones next door. The economists Rebecca Diamond, Tim McQuade, and Franklin Qian compared the two groups and published their results in the American Economic Review in 2019. They found that rent control limited renters' mobility by about 20 percent. They also found that landlords of covered buildings reduced the supply of rental housing by about 15 percent, mainly by selling to owner-occupants and redeveloping buildings. The authors concluded that this lost supply probably pushed up market rents in the long run, even though the policy protected the renters who were already in the covered units. The study does not settle the debate, but it shows that the effects of a price ceiling can be measured.

Key Concepts

Price Gouging Laws: A Ceiling That Switches On

Some price ceilings are written to apply only in special circumstances. In California, Penal Code Section 396 bans price gouging by making it illegal for a seller to raise the price of certain goods and services by more than 10 percent after the government declares an emergency. The law covers many essentials, including food, emergency and medical supplies, building materials, gasoline, lodging, and repair services, and it also limits rent increases on housing. According to the California Attorney General's office, the protections start as soon as a state of emergency is declared by the President, the Governor, or a local official, and they generally last for 30 days, though they may be extended. A seller who violates the law may face a fine of up to $10,000 and up to a year in county jail, as well as civil penalties. A seller may defend a higher price by proving that its own costs went up.

The purpose of the law is to protect people from being charged very high prices during a wildfire or an earthquake when they have little choice. Supporters say that a person who has just lost a home should not have to bid against others for a room. Many people believe it is wrong to profit from a disaster, and that feeling is a value judgment that economics cannot settle. What economics can do is describe the effects. A binding limit on the price of bottled water holds the price below the level that the market would set when supply is short and demand has jumped. The result is a shortage at the legal price, and stores may run out quickly.

Economists therefore often point to the trade-offs. A higher price in an emergency rations scarce supplies and gives sellers, including those in other states, a reason to bring in more of what is needed. A cap may help those who reach the shelf first but leave others with nothing, and it may discourage sellers from shipping goods to the disaster area. Defenders reply that supply in a real emergency cannot respond quickly because roads are closed, so the supply curve is nearly vertical and the main effect of a high price is to transfer money from buyers to sellers. Both sides have a point, which is why the law gives sellers a defense based on cost increases. The debate shows how the same model can support different conclusions when people weigh values differently.

Key Concepts

Price Floors and Surpluses

A price floor works in the opposite direction. The best-known example is the minimum wage, a legal minimum price for labor. In the labor market, the “price” is the wage, the buyers are employers, and the sellers are workers. Figure 6.18 shows a hypothetical market in which the equilibrium wage is $14 per hour and 300 workers are employed. A wage floor of $18 is above equilibrium. At that wage, 500 workers want jobs, but employers want only 100. The model predicts a surplus of workers, which is unemployment.

This is a simplified picture, and economists debate how well it fits real labor markets. The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 per hour since July 24, 2009, while many states and cities set higher minimums. California's statewide minimum wage was $16.90 per hour in 2026 for most employers, as you saw in Chapter 5 (Figure 5.10). A basic model predicts that a higher minimum wage reduces employment among workers who earn the minimum. Some studies find small effects on jobs, and others find larger ones, in part because employers have ways to respond, such as changing hours or prices, and because a higher wage can lower worker turnover. The size of the effect also depends on how far the floor is above the equilibrium wage.

Key Concepts

The Minimum Wage Over Time

The federal minimum wage began in 1938 under the Fair Labor Standards Act. Congress has raised it many times, but it does not rise automatically with prices. Since July 24, 2009, it has been $7.25 per hour. Figure 6.6.19 shows what that means. The orange line adjusts each year's wage for inflation, expressing it in January 2026 dollars, and the blue line shows the nominal wage, which is the dollar amount written in the law. In January 1968 the federal minimum was $1.60. After adjusting for inflation, that is worth about $15.32 in 2026 dollars. The nominal wage has risen by more than four times since then, but the real value of the wage has been cut roughly in half because prices rose faster than Congress raised the wage.

The graph has two lessons. First, a nominal wage floor loses force as prices rise. A floor that is binding in one year may become non-binding years later if the equilibrium wage rises with inflation and the floor stays the same. For many workers in California today, the federal minimum is not binding at all, because the state minimum is much higher. Second, a rise in the minimum wage by itself says little about its effect on workers. What matters is how high the floor is compared with the equilibrium wage in the local labor market. A $15 floor means little in a market where the typical wage is $25, and it means a lot in a market where the typical wage for entry-level jobs is $13.

States and cities set their own minimums, and many are well above the federal level. California's statewide minimum wage is $16.90 per hour in 2026, according to the Department of Industrial Relations. Since April 1, 2024, covered fast-food workers in California must be paid at least $20 per hour. Because the minimum wage varies so much from place to place, economists can compare nearby areas with different laws and see what happens. That variation is the source of much of the evidence you will read about next. It is also a reminder that a single national number can hide large differences in the local markets that determine what a floor actually does.

Key Concepts

What Do Studies Find?

Economists have studied the minimum wage for decades, and the results have led to a real debate. The basic model predicts that a binding minimum wage reduces employment. For many years, this was the standard view. In 1994, the economists David Card and Alan Krueger compared fast-food restaurants in New Jersey, which had raised its minimum wage in 1992, with those across the river in Pennsylvania, which had not. They did not find that employment fell in New Jersey. Their finding surprised many economists, and it was challenged by others who questioned the data and methods. Card shared the 2021 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for his empirical work on labor markets, including studies of the minimum wage.

Why might a minimum wage not reduce jobs by as much as the simple model predicts? One reason is that employers who pay more may lose fewer workers, so they spend less on hiring and training. Another is that employers may raise prices a little, or accept lower profits, instead of cutting jobs. A third is monopsony, a situation in which an employer has so much power in the local labor market that it can pay less than workers are worth. In that case, a higher minimum wage can raise pay without much loss of jobs. Another possibility is that the floor in the studied market is only slightly above the equilibrium wage, so that the surplus of workers is small.

Other studies find larger effects, especially for large increases. In February 2021, the Congressional Budget Office estimated the effects of raising the federal minimum wage to $15 an hour by 2025. It projected that about 1.4 million fewer people would have jobs, while about 900,000 fewer people would live in poverty, because the higher pay would reach many low-wage workers who kept their jobs. CBO stressed that these numbers were uncertain. The accurate summary is that the evidence shows a trade-off. A higher floor raises incomes for many workers and may cost some workers their jobs, and the size of each effect depends on how high the wage is and on the local market. Reasonable people can weigh these results differently.

Key Concepts

Farm Price Supports

Price floors are common in agriculture. During the Great Depression, farm prices collapsed, and in 1933 Congress passed the Agricultural Adjustment Act to raise them. The government paid farmers to plant less, bought crops, and promised minimum prices, called price supports, and versions of these programs have continued ever since. When a support price is above the equilibrium price, the model predicts a surplus. In Figure 6.6.20, a hypothetical wheat market has an equilibrium of $7 per bushel and 40 million bushels. A support price of $9 raises the quantity supplied to 60 million bushels and cuts the quantity demanded to 20 million. The surplus is 40 million bushels. If the government buys the surplus at $9, the purchase costs $360 million, and the grain must be stored, sold abroad, or given away.

The U.S. sugar program is a real example. The U.S. government supports sugar prices with loans to processors at set rates per pound, with limits on how much sugar processors may sell, and with import quotas and tariffs that restrict sugar from other countries. According to the USDA Economic Research Service, these tools keep U.S. sugar prices usually well above comparable prices in the world market. Figure 6.6.21 compares the U.S. price with the world price from 2015 to 2026. In January 2026, the U.S. price was about 33 cents per pound, compared with about 15 cents for world sugar. Supporters say the program protects American farmers and processors from price swings and from foreign competition. Critics say it raises the cost of sugar for consumers and for candy and food makers.

Because farm supports are complicated, evaluating them requires looking at who pays and who gains. Farmers who grow the supported crop gain. Taxpayers pay when the government buys or stores a surplus. Consumers pay when a program raises the prices they see in the store, as sugar users do. Farmers who grow other crops may lose if the supported crop uses land that would otherwise grow something else. The supports also change incentives: when the price is guaranteed above equilibrium, farmers have a reason to produce more than the market wants. Many economists therefore prefer income supports and crop insurance to price floors, but the debate over farm policy continues, and farm states have a strong voice in it.

Key Concepts

Weighing the Trade-Offs

Price controls are popular because the people who gain are easy to see. A renter in a rent-controlled apartment and a worker who receives the minimum wage know exactly how they benefit. The costs are harder to see: the apartment that was never built, the job that was never offered, the lower quality of goods that sellers can no longer earn extra to improve. Economists look for both kinds of effects.

Economists often suggest other tools for the same goals. To help low-income renters, a government might give housing vouchers or build more housing instead of capping rents. To help low-income workers, it might expand tax credits like the Earned Income Tax Credit. These policies have their own costs and trade-offs, such as the taxes needed to pay for them. In evaluating a price control, ask four questions: Is it binding? Who gains? Who loses? Is there another way to reach the goal?

Standard 12.2.6 asks only that you describe the effects of price controls on buyers and sellers. Deciding whether a particular policy is worth its costs involves values as well as evidence, and reasonable people disagree. A good economic analysis separates what the model predicts from what a person values.

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Seeking Equilibrium: Demand and Supply
Market equilibrium is the situation in which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied, at the equilibrium price. A price above equilibrium creates a surplus, and sellers cut prices. A price below equilibrium creates a shortage, and buyers bid prices up. A change in demand or supply shifts the equilibrium: an increase in demand raises both the price and the quantity, and a decrease in supply raises the price and lowers the quantity. When both curves shift, the direction of one change is certain and the other is not. Sticky prices, such as a menu price, can delay adjustment, and the more inelastic the demand, the larger the price spike from a supply loss.
Section 2 · Prices as Signals and Incentives
Prices reflect relative scarcity, carry information, and give buyers and sellers incentives. They perform the allocative function by directing resources toward the uses that buyers value most and the rationing function by deciding who gets a scarce good. Profit is the incentive for entrepreneurs to respond to signals; a firm breaks even when revenue equals total cost, and Kern County farmers shifting toward nuts show resources following prices.
Section 3 · Intervention in the Price System
Price controls are legal limits on prices. A binding price ceiling, set below equilibrium, creates a shortage; a binding price floor, set above equilibrium, creates a surplus. Rent control, rationing, minimum wages, and farm price supports are examples. Over time, ceilings also lower quality and supply, anti-gouging laws limit emergency price increases, and price supports create surpluses that taxpayers or consumers pay for. Controls help some buyers or sellers and harm others, and economists weigh the effects and alternatives.
