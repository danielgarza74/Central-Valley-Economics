Unit Overview

Money, Markets, and the Big Picture

Every day, millions of Americans make choices that depend on a few things they rarely think about. They trust that a dollar will be accepted at the register. They trust that a bank will return their savings. They watch prices at the grocery store, and they hear on the news that unemployment is up or down, that the economy grew, or that inflation is cooling. Units 1 through 3 focused on the choices of individuals, the workings of markets, and your own personal finances. Unit 4 steps back and asks about the economy as a whole. That is the subject of macroeconomics: total output, total employment, the general level of prices, and the money that links them.

The unit begins with money and the institutions that handle it. In Chapter 14 you will see why money exists, what makes it trustworthy, and how banks turn deposits into loans. You will follow the long history of U.S. banking, from the First Bank of the United States to deposit insurance, and learn why lending by banks can expand the money supply. Chapter 15 widens the lens from banks to the whole financial system. Savings become investment when intermediaries and markets carry money to the people and firms that can use it. Stocks, bonds, and funds are the tools, and risk and return are the trade-off that every investor, from a Kern County farm family to a national retirement fund, must face.

The second half of the unit turns from money to measurement. Chapter 16 introduces the indicators economists use to take the pulse of the economy: gross domestic product, the unemployment rate, and the Consumer Price Index. You will practice the most important habit of a careful reader of numbers, which is separating nominal values from real values so that rising prices do not fool you. You will study the business cycle, the pattern of expansions and recessions, and what makes an economy grow over the long run. Chapter 17 then uses those tools on the problems people actually feel: unemployment, poverty and unequal incomes, and inflation. Each of these has real costs, and each response to them involves trade-offs.

The four chapters build on one another. Money and banks (Chapter 14) make financial markets possible (Chapter 15). Financial markets move savings into investment, and investment is one part of the GDP that Chapter 16 measures. The measurements of Chapter 16 reveal the problems of Chapter 17, and those problems create the questions that government must answer in Unit 5. Throughout, you will test national numbers against the place where you live. Kern County's economy includes farms, oil, and many other industries, and in 2025 its unemployment rate averaged about 9 percent, more than double the national rate (Chapter 16). Learning to read the data for Shafter, Bakersfield, and the Central Valley is one of the best ways to see why macroeconomics matters to your own life.

A final word about how to read. This unit contains more numbers than any other unit in the book, and numbers can mislead when they are read carelessly. Before you trust a statistic, ask four questions. What exactly is being counted? Which place and which time period does it describe? Is it a nominal value or a real value? And who is better off or worse off because of it? Those four questions will help you in every chapter, on every figure, and in every news story about the economy that you read after this class.

Big Ideas

1
Money and banks connect savers and borrowers. Money lowers the cost of trade, and banks and other intermediaries move savings to people who can use them.
2
Financial markets price risk and return. Stocks, bonds, and funds let people share ownership and lending, but higher returns come with higher risk.
3
Indicators measure the whole economy. GDP, the unemployment rate, and the CPI turn a huge economy into numbers, and real values keep prices from misleading us.
4
Economic challenges have costs and trade-offs. Unemployment, poverty, and inflation fall unevenly, and every response gives something up.

Essential Questions

You should be able to answer these by the end of the unit.

  1. What makes something money, and how do banks turn deposits into loans and new money?
  2. How do savings move to people who need to invest, and how do risk and return shape those choices?
  3. How do economists measure whether the economy is growing, and why must they separate nominal numbers from real numbers?
  4. What do unemployment, poverty, and inflation measure, and who is affected when they rise?
  5. Why can a national number look healthy while a place like Kern County still faces hard times?

Why This Unit Matters

It is your first week at a part-time job in Shafter, and your first paycheck lands in a checking account you opened last month. A few days later your parents mention that grocery prices have gone up again and that a relative was laid off. You wonder: Is my money safe in this bank? Does the $200 I saved last year buy as much now? Should I save it, invest it, or spend it? And why does the news say the economy is growing when people you know cannot find work? Unit 4 gives you the vocabulary and the tools to answer each of these questions with evidence instead of guesses.

Key Terms Preview

Twenty terms you will meet in this unit, with the plain-English meaning and the Spanish term the chapters use. Each one is taught in full in the chapter shown.

CH. 14money (dinero)Anything that is generally accepted as payment for goods, services, and debts.
CH. 14fiat money (dinero fiduciario)Money that has value only because a government declares it legal tender and people accept it.
CH. 14fractional reserve banking (sistema de reserva fraccionaria)A system in which banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest.
CH. 14money multiplier (multiplicador monetario)The number by which an initial deposit can be expanded through lending, equal to 1 divided by the reserve ratio.
CH. 14nominal value (valor nominal)A value measured in current dollars, with no adjustment for changes in prices.
CH. 14real value (valor real)A value that has been adjusted for changes in prices, so that it shows purchasing power.
CH. 15financial intermediary (intermediario financiero)An institution that channels money from savers to borrowers, such as a bank or credit union.
CH. 15stock (acción)A certificate of ownership in a corporation, also called a share.
CH. 15bond (bono)A contract issued by a borrower promising to repay a sum of money, with interest, on a fixed schedule.
CH. 15diversification (diversificación)A strategy of spreading investments among different assets to lower risk.
CH. 15risk (riesgo)The chance that an investment's actual return will be different from what is expected, including the chance of loss.
CH. 16gross domestic product (GDP) (producto interno bruto (PIB))The total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period.
CH. 16real GDP (PIB real)GDP adjusted for changes in prices, so that it measures changes in the quantity of output.
CH. 16unemployment rate (tasa de desempleo)The percent of the labor force that is without a job and actively looking for work.
CH. 16Consumer Price Index (CPI) (índice de precios al consumidor (IPC))A measure of the average change in prices paid by urban consumers for a fixed basket of goods and services.
CH. 16business cycle (ciclo económico)The pattern of expansion and contraction that occurs in an economy over time.
CH. 16yield curve (curva de rendimiento)A graph that shows interest rates on bonds of equal risk but different lengths of time to maturity.
CH. 16recession (recesión)A significant decline in economic activity spread across the economy and lasting more than a few months.
CH. 17inflation (inflación)A sustained increase in the general level of prices.
CH. 17poverty line (línea de pobreza)The amount of income that a family needs to meet basic needs; families below it are counted as poor.

LOOK BACK

This unit builds on ideas you already know.

  • Unit 2, Microeconomics (Chapters 4–9). Demand, supply, and prices return as aggregate demand and aggregate supply in Chapter 16. Business organizations (Chapter 8) explain who issues stock and bonds in Chapter 15, and the labor chapter (Chapter 9) helps you understand wages and unemployment in Chapters 16 and 17.
  • Unit 3, Consumer and Personal Finance (Chapters 10–13). Budgeting, credit, and big purchases give you a personal view of the interest rates, loans, and risks that Chapters 14 and 15 explain for the whole economy.
  • Unit 1, Foundations. Scarcity, trade-offs, and opportunity cost return when Chapters 16 and 17 ask what a country gives up to grow or to fight inflation.

LOOK AHEAD

Unit 4 shows what the economy does and where it struggles. Unit 5, Government and Policy, asks what government can do about it.

  • Chapter 18, Government Revenue and Spending: where government gets its money and how it spends it.
  • Chapter 19, Using Fiscal Policy: taxing, borrowing, and spending to influence production, employment, and prices (previewed in Chapter 17, Section 3).
  • Chapter 20, The Federal Reserve and Monetary Policy: how the central bank uses interest rates and the banking system to influence the economy (previewed in Chapter 14, Section 2).
