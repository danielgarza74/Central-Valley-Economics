Concept Review

In Chapter 16 you learned that the economy moves through a business cycle, the series of growing and shrinking periods of economic activity. Growth brings new jobs and rising incomes. Recessions bring layoffs and falling output. In Chapter 17 you studied the problems that follow, including unemployment, inflation, and poverty. In Chapter 18 you learned where the government gets its money and how it spends it. Now the pieces come together. This chapter asks how the government can use its taxing and spending powers on purpose, to try to smooth the ups and downs of the economy, and what happens to the government's own finances when it does.

Chapter 19 Key Concept

Fiscal policy uses taxes and government spending in an effort to smooth out the peaks and troughs of the business cycle. Its aims are to support production and employment and to keep price levels stable. Because fiscal policy changes the government's own budget, it also affects borrowing and the national debt.

Why the Concept Matters

Suppose a farm labor contractor in Shafter loses half of her customers in a downturn. She lays off workers, who stop eating out and put off car repairs. The taquería and the tire shop lose customers and cut hours too. Each decision is sensible for the person making it, yet together they push the economy down further. Fiscal policy is one of the ways a government tries to break that chain. It might send out checks, extend unemployment benefits, or pay for road repairs that hire local workers. Or, in a different view, it might cut taxes so employers can keep more of what they earn and hire. Both approaches cost money, and when the government spends more than it collects it must borrow. By the end of this chapter you will be able to explain what fiscal policy is, compare the two main approaches to it, and weigh the costs and benefits of the federal deficit and debt.

Key Concepts

Fiscal Policy Tools

The word fiscal refers to anything related to government revenue, spending, and debt. It comes from a Latin word, fiscus, that meant a basket or purse used to hold money. In Chapter 18 you studied how the government fills that purse with taxes and how it empties it with spending. Fiscal policy is the use of those same two tools, taxes and government spending, on purpose, in an effort to smooth out the peaks and troughs of the business cycle. When it works as intended, fiscal policy helps keep production growing, keeps people employed, and keeps prices from rising or falling too fast.

Why would the government want to do this? Think back to the business cycle from Chapter 16. In an expansion, businesses hire, households spend, and output rises. In a recession, the pattern reverses: spending falls, businesses cut production, workers lose jobs, and falling incomes cause still less spending. Left alone, markets usually recover, but the recovery can take years, and the human cost in lost jobs, missed mortgage payments, and closed businesses is large. During the Great Recession of 2007 to 2009, for example, real gross domestic product (GDP) fell 2.6 percent in 2009, and the unemployment rate averaged 9.6 percent in 2010. Many economists and citizens believe that government should try to shorten such downturns. Others believe that government actions often do more harm than good. This chapter explains both views.

Fiscal policy has three main tools. The first is government purchases: spending on goods and services such as highways, school buildings, aircraft, and the salaries of teachers and soldiers. The second is taxes: changes in tax rates or in what is taxed, which change how much households and businesses have left to spend and invest. The third is transfer payments, such as unemployment benefits, Social Security checks, and food assistance, which move money to households without buying anything directly. Transfers do not count as purchases in GDP, but they put money into the hands of people who will often spend it. Borrowing is not a separate tool so much as the way the government pays when it uses any of these tools without raising an equal amount of revenue, a topic you will study in Section 3.

In Chapter 6 you learned that prices adjust when demand or supply shifts. Economists who study the whole economy use a similar idea, called aggregate demand, the total amount of goods and services that all the households, businesses, governments, and foreign buyers in the economy want to buy at a given price level. When aggregate demand is too weak, firms cut production and workers lose jobs. When it is too strong for the economy's capacity to produce, prices rise broadly, which is inflation. Most fiscal policy works by trying to push total spending up or down.

Expansionary and Contractionary Policy

Fiscal policy comes in two directions. Expansionary fiscal policy increases total spending in the economy by raising government spending, cutting taxes, or both. Its goal is to boost production and jobs, so it is the usual response to a recession. For example, a government might hire workers to repair bridges, or it might send checks to families so that they will buy more at stores. Contractionary fiscal policy does the opposite: it reduces total spending by cutting government spending, raising taxes, or both. Its goal is to cool an economy that is growing too fast and pushing up prices, so it is the usual response to inflation.

In practice, lawmakers use contractionary policy much less often than expansionary policy. A tax increase or a spending cut hurts people immediately and is rarely popular, while a tax cut or a spending increase is easy to explain to voters. Economists note that this difference can bias policy toward deficits, an issue that returns in Section 3. Figure 19.1 summarizes the two directions and the tools that go with them.

Fiscal Policy and the Business Cycle

Figure 19.2 shows the annual growth rate of real GDP, the output of the whole economy adjusted for price changes, from 1995 through 2025. The line rises and falls, but it also stays above zero in most years. Notice the three dips below zero: 2009, after the housing and financial crisis; 2020, when the COVID-19 pandemic shut down parts of the economy; and the near-zero 2008. The year 2001 also had a recession, even though the annual number stayed positive at 1.0 percent, a reminder that official recessions are measured by the monthly path of output and employment, not just by a single year's figure.

Government policies can change the shape of this line. The best-known theory behind such policies was developed in the 1930s, when the Great Depression threw about one in four American workers out of work. You will meet its author in the Pacesetter in Section 2. For now, notice an important idea: the same tools can be used in either direction. A government that cuts taxes during a recession and then raises them during a boom is practicing counter-cyclical policy, because the action goes against the direction of the cycle. A government that does the reverse, cutting spending in a recession because tax revenue has fallen, is practicing pro-cyclical policy, which makes the swings larger. Many state governments, which must balance their budgets, face this problem, as you learned in Chapter 18.

Fiscal policy also relates to monetary policy, which is the use of interest rates and the money supply by the Federal Reserve to influence the economy. The two policies are run by different institutions. Congress and the President control fiscal policy. The Federal Reserve, an independent agency, controls monetary policy, which you will study in Chapter 20. Because they work on the same economy at the same time, the two can reinforce one another or pull in different directions.

Key Concepts

Gaps, Slack, and Warning Lights

How do lawmakers decide that the economy needs help? One starting point is an idea called potential GDP: the amount of output an economy can produce when its workers and factories are being used at a normal, sustainable pace. Potential GDP is not a speed limit on what the economy can ever do. It is closer to a comfortable cruising speed, the level of production that can continue year after year without pushing prices up faster and faster. The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) estimates potential GDP regularly, and the estimate changes as the labor force, the supply of machines and buildings, and technology change.

Compare actual GDP with potential GDP and you find the output gap. When actual GDP is below potential, the economy has slack: some workers are unemployed, some factories sit idle, and some stores have too few customers. Economists call this a recessionary gap, and it is the usual reason lawmakers consider expansionary policy. When actual GDP is above potential, workers and machines are being pushed past their normal pace. Employers compete for scarce workers, wages rise, and prices follow. Economists call this an inflationary gap, and it is the usual reason to consider contractionary policy. Because the gap is measured as a percentage of potential GDP, it lets economists compare economies of different sizes and years.

Potential GDP cannot be seen directly. It must be estimated, and estimates can be wrong. That is why lawmakers also watch two warning lights that can be seen in the data. Figure 19.3 shows both: the unemployment rate and the inflation rate, measured by the price index for personal consumption expenditures, the measure the Federal Reserve uses for its 2 percent inflation goal. The lights often blink at different times. During the Great Recession aftermath, the unemployment rate averaged 9.3 percent in 2009 and 9.6 percent in 2010, while inflation was only 2.1 percent and 1.5 percent. That pattern signals a weak economy. In 2021 and 2022 the pattern reversed: inflation was 6.0 percent and 5.6 percent, while the unemployment rate had fallen to 5.3 percent and then 3.6 percent. That pattern signals an economy running hot. In 2020 the unemployment rate jumped to 8.1 percent while inflation stayed at 1.3 percent, a reminder that a sudden shutdown can look different from an ordinary recession.

National averages also hide local differences. Fiscal policy is aimed at the national economy, but people feel the economy where they live. In August 2026, the unemployment rate in Kern County was 8.4 percent, compared with 5.4 percent for California as a whole in the same state report (neither figure is adjusted for the seasons). Kern's rate is usually higher than the state's, in part because farm work is seasonal: fields and packing houses hire many workers at harvest and let them go afterward. A national program designed for the average American may fit Shafter or Delano poorly, and that mismatch is one of the limits you will weigh later in this section.

Key Concepts

Discretionary Policy and Automatic Stabilizers

Some fiscal policy requires a vote. Discretionary fiscal policy refers to deliberate changes in government spending or taxes made by lawmakers to influence the economy. When Congress passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act in February 2009, it was practicing discretionary policy: it voted to add spending and cut some taxes in response to the recession. The law's total cost was estimated at about $787 billion at the time it was passed. In March 2020, Congress passed the CARES Act, a far larger package, in response to the pandemic.

Other fiscal policy happens on its own. Automatic stabilizers are features of the tax and spending system that change government spending and tax collections automatically when the economy changes, with no new vote by Congress. Consider a progressive income tax. In a recession, incomes fall, so people pay less income tax, and the fall is larger than the fall in income because some income drops into lower brackets. That leaves households with more money than they otherwise would have had. Unemployment insurance works similarly: when layoffs rise, more workers qualify for weekly benefits, so the government's spending rises just when household incomes are falling. Programs such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) and Medicaid also grow when more families qualify. In an expansion, the process reverses: tax collections rise faster than income, and fewer people receive benefits, which helps cool an overheating economy.

Automatic stabilizers have two advantages: they act quickly, and they need no political agreement. They have a cost, too: they widen the budget deficit in a recession, as Figure 19.4 shows. Look at the deficit in fiscal year 2009 and in 2020. In 2009, the deficit was 9.8 percent of GDP; in 2020, it was 14.7 percent. Both years were recession years, and both deficits include automatic changes in taxes and benefits as well as the discretionary spending that Congress approved. Economists measure these separately, because the automatic part happens without new laws while the discretionary part reflects choices that lawmakers made.

How Big Are the Stabilizers? A California Look

Automatic stabilizers are easier to understand when you look at the rules of a real program. Unemployment insurance (UI) in California is run by the state's Employment Development Department (EDD) and is paid for mainly by taxes on employers. A worker who loses a job through no fault of his or her own, and who earned enough in the past, can receive weekly benefits while looking for work. In September 2026, the maximum weekly benefit in California was $450 and the minimum was $40, according to the EDD. A packing-house worker who had earned $900 a week would therefore receive at most $450, about half of her old pay.

Look closely at what is automatic and what is not. The number of people who receive benefits rises and falls on its own with the economy. But the rules about who qualifies and how much they receive change only when lawmakers vote. That distinction matters in a deep recession. In 2009 and again in 2020, Congress added temporary benefits on top of the regular system. A policy that begins as an automatic stabilizer can therefore be supplemented with discretionary policy, and the two often work together.

How large are the automatic effects over time? In August 2026, the Congressional Budget Office reported that from 1976 to 2025 automatic stabilizers increased the federal deficit by an estimated average of 0.3 percent of potential GDP per year. That average combines large effects in recession years with small or even opposite effects when the economy ran above its potential. CBO projects that the effect will be close to zero on average from 2026 to 2036, because its baseline does not predict a recession. A real downturn would change that. The added deficit is not a failure of the system. It is the system doing what it was built to do: cushioning households when incomes fall, and collecting more when incomes rise.

Economists who study unemployment insurance see both benefits and costs. Supporters point out that benefits keep money flowing to grocers, landlords, and car repair shops, which protects local jobs, and that a worker with a little income can search longer for a good job match instead of taking the first offer. Critics point out that when benefits are generous, some workers may take longer to return to work, and that the taxes that pay for the program fall on employers. Studies find both effects. The debate is mostly about their size, and the answer can depend on how weak the job market is at the time.

Key Concepts

Problems and Limits of Fiscal Policy

If fiscal policy worked perfectly, recessions would be brief and inflation rare. It does not work perfectly. Economists point to several reasons.

Time lags. Policy takes time at every step. First, officials must recognize that the economy has turned. The National Bureau of Economic Research, the private group of economists that dates U.S. recessions, announced in December 2008 that a recession had begun a year earlier, in December 2007. Second, Congress must debate and pass a law. The American Recovery and Reinvestment Act became law in February 2009. Third, agencies must carry out the spending, and projects such as highway construction must be designed, bid, and built. By the time the spending reaches the economy, conditions may have changed. In 2020, the lags were short: the recession lasted only two months, February to April 2020, and Congress passed the CARES Act on March 27, 2020, but the crisis moved so fast that the aid had to be designed in the middle of it, before anyone knew how long the shutdown would last.

Politics. Elected officials respond to voters, and a tax cut or spending program that helps the economy may also help the officials who vote for it. Spending programs are easy to start and hard to end, and a temporary measure may become permanent. Economists with different views argue about whether this bias is a bug or a feature. Supporters of active policy say that voters expect government to respond to hardship. Critics say it explains why governments run deficits in good times as well as bad.

Uncertainty. The economy is complicated, and estimates of what a policy will do can be wrong. The same $100 billion could create many jobs or few, depending on how households, businesses, and the Federal Reserve respond. Official estimates, such as those from the Congressional Budget Office, give ranges rather than a single number for this reason.

Rational expectations. The rational expectations theory holds that people use all the information available to them, including what they know about government policy, to make decisions about the future. If households expect a tax cut now to be followed by tax increases later to pay for it, they may save the tax cut rather than spend it, and the policy will have less effect than lawmakers hoped. Supporters of the theory argue that policies that are widely expected will be offset by changes in behavior. Critics reply that many people, especially those with little savings, do spend extra income quickly, so the offset is partial at most.

Crowding out. When the government borrows to pay for stimulus, it competes with businesses and households for the same pool of savings. That can raise interest rates and discourage private investment. You will study this crowding-out effect in Section 3.

COUNTER-ARGUMENTShould Government Try to Manage the Business Cycle?

Economists agree that recessions are costly. They disagree about how much government should try to do about them. Here is the best case that each side makes.

The case for active stabilization
Recessions do lasting damage. Workers who lose jobs lose skills and savings, and businesses that close often do not come back. Wages and prices adjust slowly, so waiting can take years: after the Great Recession the unemployment rate stayed above 8 percent from 2009 through 2012 (annual averages of 9.3, 9.6, 8.9, and 8.1 percent). Automatic stabilizers show that most economists already accept some role for government. When interest rates are already near zero, as they were from 2009 to 2015, supporters argue that fiscal policy is the main tool left. A late or imperfect response, they say, is still better than none.
The case for restraint
Government forecasts the economy poorly, acts late, and finds it hard to undo what it starts. By the time a program arrives, the recovery may be under way, as the lags in Figure 19.5 show. Spending decisions reflect politics as much as economic need, and borrowing today can crowd out private investment or lead to higher taxes later. Critics also note that economies recovered from many downturns before modern stabilization policy existed. Some economists argue that steady, predictable rules produce more stability than constant adjustment, an argument that Milton Friedman made about monetary policy and that others apply to fiscal policy.

Key Concepts

Demand-Side Economics

For most of the nineteenth century and the early twentieth century, most economists believed that markets would correct themselves if the government stayed out of the way. If a recession caused wages and prices to fall, they reasoned, goods would become cheap enough that people would buy them, and production and employment would recover. The Great Depression of the 1930s challenged that belief. From 1929 to 1933, U.S. output fell by roughly a quarter, and by 1933 about one worker in four was unemployed. Prices and wages fell, but the economy did not recover on its own for years.

The British economist John Maynard Keynes (pronounced "canes") offered an explanation in his 1936 book The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money. His argument, called Keynesian economics, is that the economy can get stuck at a high level of unemployment when total spending, called aggregate demand, is too low. Businesses will not hire workers to make goods that nobody is buying, and consumers cannot buy goods if they have no jobs. Keynes concluded that in such a situation, the government should step in and increase total spending itself, even if it has to borrow, until private spending recovers. Demand-side fiscal policy is the use of government spending, transfers, and taxes to change total demand for goods and services in the economy.

Demand-side policy relies on three main methods. First, the government can buy goods and services, such as building bridges, schools, and power lines. Second, it can make transfer payments, such as extended unemployment benefits and direct payments to households, which the recipients then spend. Third, it can cut taxes, especially for households likely to spend the extra money, which raises their take-home pay. Demand-side advocates tend to favor temporary measures that reach people quickly and target those who will spend the money rather than save it.

The United States tried large demand-side measures during the Depression. The New Deal created programs such as the Civilian Conservation Corps (1933–1942), which put young men to work on conservation projects, and the Works Progress Administration, which employed millions of people to build roads, schools, and public buildings. Historians and economists still debate how much these programs helped. Many economists argue that the largest boost to production and employment actually came when government spending surged during World War II. Others note that the New Deal programs reduced hardship even when they did not end the Depression. Both observations can be true at the same time.

The Spending Multiplier Effect

Keynes also argued that a dollar of government spending can produce more than a dollar of total spending. This is the spending multiplier effect: an initial change in spending creates additional rounds of spending, so the total change in output is larger than the first change. The idea is simple. Suppose the government pays a contractor $100 million to repair a canal. The contractor pays workers and buys materials, so those households and suppliers now have $100 million more income. If they spend, say, 80 percent of what they receive and save the rest, then $80 million becomes income for the grocers, mechanics, and restaurant owners who sell to them. Those people spend 80 percent of that, or $64 million, and the process continues with each round smaller than the last.

The share of each extra dollar of income that people spend is called the marginal propensity to consume (MPC). The share they save is the marginal propensity to save (MPS), and MPC + MPS = 1. In this simple model, the multiplier equals 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), or 1 ÷ MPS. With an MPC of 0.8, the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5, so the original $100 million would eventually produce $500 million in total spending. Figure 19.6 shows the first rounds of that process.

Real multipliers are smaller than this textbook example, because some money leaks out of the process. Households pay taxes on the money they receive, so they have less left to spend. They buy imported goods, which sends spending to other countries. They save some, as the MPC already assumes. And if the government's borrowing raises interest rates, it can reduce private investment, which offsets part of the effect. Economists estimate the size of real multipliers from data, and their estimates vary widely depending on the time, the type of policy, and the condition of the economy. Supporters of demand-side policy often argue that multipliers are larger when the economy has many unemployed workers and idle factories, because new spending then puts unused resources to work rather than competing for scarce ones.

Recent recessions have produced the largest demand-side measures in American history. What Does the Evidence Say About the Multiplier?

The simple formula gives a clean answer, but real economies are messy, so economists estimate real multipliers from data. The best-known official estimates come from the Congressional Budget Office. In November 2012, CBO estimated that the 2009 Recovery Act raised real GDP in the third quarter of 2012 by between 0.1 and 0.7 percent and increased the number of people employed by between 0.2 million and 0.9 million, compared with what would have happened without the law. Notice how wide those ranges are. CBO gives a range because the true effect of a policy cannot be observed directly. Nobody can see the economy that would have existed if the law had never passed. Economists call that missing picture the counterfactual.

A later CBO report covering 2013 said that the law's effects on output had peaked in the first half of 2010 and had faded since, and that the law's budget cost, about $830 billion over fiscal years 2009 to 2019, was higher than the $787 billion first estimated. Supporters of the law read these reports as evidence that it helped during the worst of the downturn. Critics read the same reports as evidence that the effects were modest compared with the cost and did not last. Both readings use the same numbers.

Why do estimates of the multiplier differ so much? Three conditions seem to matter. The first is slack. If many workers and factories are idle, new spending can put them to work without pushing up prices, so the multiplier is likely to be larger. The second is the Federal Reserve. If the Fed raises interest rates to offset a stimulus, the multiplier is smaller; if it holds rates near zero, as it did from 2009 through 2015 (the federal funds rate averaged between 0.1 and 0.2 percent a year), the multiplier can be larger. The third is the kind of policy. Money that reaches families who will spend it quickly tends to have more effect than money that reaches families who will save it.

These conditions help explain why economists can look at the same law and reach different conclusions. Those who find large multipliers usually point to years like 2009, when interest rates were near zero and the unemployment rate was above 9 percent. Those who find small multipliers often point to years when the economy was already close to full employment, when new government spending must compete with private spending for the same workers and materials. Neither group is ignoring the facts. They disagree about which situation resembles the one at hand.

Tax Cuts or Spending? Temporary or Permanent?

Demand-side supporters do not treat all fiscal actions alike. Their key question is how much of each dollar will actually be spent, and how soon. Money that goes to a family living paycheck to paycheck is usually spent quickly. Money that goes to a family with large savings is more likely to be saved. That is why many demand-side economists prefer targeted help, such as unemployment benefits or payments that shrink at higher incomes, to broad tax cuts. In March 2020, the CARES Act sent a one-time payment of $1,200 for each adult and $500 for each child, with the payment phasing out at higher incomes.

Tax cuts have also been used for demand-side purposes. In 2011, Congress lowered the Social Security payroll tax paid by workers from 6.2 percent to 4.2 percent of wages, which raised take-home pay for millions of workers during a slow recovery. A worker earning $40,000 a year kept an extra $800 over the year (2 percent of $40,000). Whether such a cut raises spending depends in part on whether workers expect it to last. A family that expects extra pay for years may change its plans more than a family that receives a one-time check. Yet economists have also found that families with little savings spend much of even a temporary payment quickly, which is why the rational expectations idea you read about earlier comes with an important Counter-Argument.

Supply-side supporters make a different point. A one-time payment does not change anyone's reward for working or investing, because the next dollar earned is taxed at the same rate as before. Only a lasting change in tax rates changes the incentive to work, save, and invest. Demand-side supporters answer that the problem in a recession is not a lack of incentives but a lack of customers. A lower tax rate does little for a business owner who has no orders to fill. The two views are examined further in the next part of this section.

The recession that began in 2007 was global, and so was the response. Key Concepts

Supply-Side Economics

Demand-side policy focuses on total spending. Supply-side fiscal policy focuses instead on the economy's capacity to produce, that is, on the supply of goods and services. Its idea is that the best way to increase output and jobs over time is to give people and businesses stronger incentives to work, save, invest, and produce. The main supply-side tool is a cut in tax rates, especially marginal tax rates, the rates that apply to the last dollar earned. Lower marginal rates, supporters argue, raise the reward for working an extra hour, starting a business, or investing in new equipment, and so shift the economy's supply curve to the right. The result, they say, is higher output, more jobs, and lower prices.

Supply-side policy also includes other measures that raise the economy's productive capacity. Reducing regulations that raise the cost of doing business, offering tax credits for investment in new equipment or energy projects, and spending on education, research, and infrastructure can all increase output over time. The tax credits for wind and solar power, for example, have encouraged investment in places like the Tehachapi Pass in Kern County, one of the oldest and largest wind-energy areas in the United States.

Supply-side ideas became prominent in the United States in the late 1970s, when the economy suffered from both inflation and slow growth, a combination that demand-side policy had difficulty fixing because stimulating spending would raise prices even more. Supply-side supporters argued that the answer was to lower tax rates to encourage production. Presidents of both parties had cut taxes before, but the 1981 tax law, passed under President Ronald Reagan, cut the top federal income tax rate from 70 percent to 50 percent, and a 1986 law lowered it further to 28 percent. Tax cuts in 2001 and 2003, the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017, and the 2025 tax and spending law all drew on the supply-side argument, though their supporters and critics also disagree over how much of their effect came from incentives and how much simply from putting money in people's hands.

Investing in Capacity: Roads, Schools, and Research

Not every supply-side idea involves tax rates. Economists use the term public investment for government spending that builds the economy's long-run capacity: highways, ports, water systems, schools, and research. Supporters call it the most promising meeting ground between demand-side and supply-side ideas. A road project creates jobs while it is built, which is a demand effect, and lowers shipping costs for decades afterward, which is a supply effect. If public investment makes private investment more profitable, economists call the result crowding in, the opposite of the crowding-out effect that you will study in Section 3.

Kern County shows why the idea appeals to many people. Almonds, grapes, citrus, and carrots leave the Central Valley by truck, and the speed and cost of that trip depend on roads and rail lines. Oil fields, wind farms, and solar projects depend on electric lines and water. Community colleges and high schools train the workers who run them. All of these are forms of capital that the private market alone might supply too little of, because their benefits are shared by everyone.

Critics raise two cautions. First, projects vary in quality. A road that carries few vehicles raises costs without raising output, so each project's benefits have to be compared with its costs. Second, governments sometimes choose projects for political reasons. Supporters answer that private firms underinvest in things that benefit everyone, such as basic research and roads, which is why government is often the only buyer. The practical question, on which supporters and critics can both agree, is how to pick the projects with the highest return.

The Laffer Curve

The best-known picture of the supply-side argument is the Laffer curve, named for the economist Arthur Laffer, who popularized it in the 1970s. It shows the relationship between tax rates and the amount of revenue a government collects. At a tax rate of 0 percent, the government collects nothing. At a rate of 100 percent, it would also collect almost nothing, because nobody would work to earn income that is entirely taxed away. Somewhere in between is a rate that raises the most revenue. If the tax rate is above that point, supporters argue, then cutting the rate would encourage so much extra work and investment that revenue could actually rise. Figure 19.8 illustrates the idea.

The curve is a useful idea but not a precise map. Economists on all sides agree that very high rates reduce the incentive to earn income and encourage tax avoidance. The disagreement is about where the United States sits on the curve. Most studies find that at the tax rates the United States has had in recent decades, cutting rates reduces revenue in the short run, though some of the lost revenue is regained as the economy grows. Supply-side supporters argue that the regained portion is larger than critics believe. Critics argue that the economy's response is small, so tax cuts add to the deficit. Both sides use data from the same tax laws, and the question of how much a tax cut pays for itself remains a matter of active research.

Figure 19.9 shows how much the top marginal rate on the federal income tax has changed in a lifetime. The top rate was 91 percent in the 1950s, when it applied only to a small number of very high incomes after deductions, and it has been 37 percent since 2018. Remember from Chapter 18 that a marginal rate applies only to income above a threshold. A change in the top rate therefore affects only the highest earners directly. The changes in rates for middle-income taxpayers are smaller and tend to matter more for total revenue.

The Evidence on Tax Cuts and Revenue

Can economists measure how much of a tax cut pays for itself? They try with dynamic scoring. A conventional estimate of a tax cut holds the overall size of the economy fixed and counts only the revenue lost at the old level of income. A dynamic score also estimates how people and businesses would respond, for example by working or investing more, and counts the revenue from that extra activity. Dynamic scoring is a way of asking the Laffer-curve question with numbers.

Studies using dynamic scoring have produced a range of answers. In a widely cited analysis for the National Bureau of Economic Research, the economists N. Gregory Mankiw and Matthew Weinzierl estimated that about 17 percent of the revenue lost from a cut in taxes on labor income is recouped through higher growth in the long run, and about 50 percent for a cut in taxes on capital income. Those are partial offsets: in that model a tax cut recovers some of its cost but not all of it. Supply-side supporters note that half is a large share and argue that the share can be larger when starting rates are higher.

Economists also disagree about where the peak of the Laffer curve is. In 2011, Peter Diamond and Emmanuel Saez estimated that the revenue-maximizing top tax rate, counting federal, state, and local taxes, could be as high as 73 percent if the tax base were broad, and about 54 percent using the existing base. Later critics argued that the peak is lower. Economists Moore, Pecoraro, and Splinter estimated a federal peak of about 48 percent after accounting for several tax bases and behavioral responses, and about 39 percent when state and local taxes are included, which is close to today's 37 percent top federal rate. (These estimates are summarized by the Tax Foundation, a center-right research group.) The estimates matter because the lesson of the curve depends on where a country starts. If the peak is far above the current top rate, a cut in the top rate loses revenue. If the peak is near the current rate, the revenue effects of a change are small in either direction.

The Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 gave economists a recent test. The Congressional Research Service (CRS), which works for Congress, reported that real GDP grew 2.9 percent in 2018, up from 2.2 percent in 2017, and that business investment grew 7 percent. But CRS estimated that the extra growth from the tax cut in 2018 was probably 0.3 percent of GDP or less, which it calculated was 5 percent or less of the growth needed for the cut to pay for itself. CRS concluded: "The data appear to indicate that not enough growth occurred in the first year to cause the tax cut to pay for itself." Supporters of supply-side policy respond that its effects build over years as firms plan and build new capacity, so a single year is too short a test.

ECONOMICS PACESETTER

John Maynard KeynesThe Economist Who Asked About Total Spending

Few economists have changed how governments think as much as John Maynard Keynes. He grew up in Cambridge, England, where his father taught economics, and he studied mathematics and economics at the University of Cambridge. He worked for the British government's Treasury during World War I and wrote a famous book warning that the harsh peace terms imposed on Germany after the war would damage Europe's economy. He taught at Cambridge for many years, advised investors and governments, and became a public voice on economic questions.

The Great Depression pushed him to rethink his field. In Britain and the United States, millions of willing workers could not find jobs, and factories sat idle. Existing theory, with its faith that markets would adjust, seemed to offer little help. In The General Theory, published in 1936, Keynes argued that an economy could settle into a long period of high unemployment if total spending stayed too low, and that a government could help by increasing its own spending until private spending recovered. His ideas shaped economic policy for decades and helped inspire the modern national income accounts that measure GDP.

Keynes had critics from the beginning. Friedrich Hayek, an Austrian-born economist, argued that government spending distorts the signals that prices send and creates problems for the future. Milton Friedman, an American economist, argued that changes in the money supply matter more than government spending, a view you will study in Chapter 20. In the 1970s, when inflation and unemployment rose together, many economists questioned whether Keynesian policy could solve the problem. Later events, including the 2008 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic, brought Keynesian ideas back into prominence. Today, economists of different schools still draw on and argue with his work.

Key Concepts

The Federal Deficit and Debt

In Chapter 18 you learned that the federal government spends more than it collects most years. The language of government finance has a few terms you must keep straight. A budget surplus occurs when government revenue is greater than its spending in a year. A budget deficit occurs when spending is greater than revenue. Spending more than the government collects, and borrowing to make up the difference, is called deficit spending. The national debt is the total amount of money that the federal government owes at a given time, which results from adding up all the years of past deficits and subtracting any surpluses.

The most common mistake in discussions of this subject is mixing up the deficit and the debt. Imagine a bathtub. Water flowing from the faucet adds to the tub, and water flowing down the drain takes away from it. The amount of water in the tub at any moment is the stock, and the rates of water flowing in or out are flows. The deficit is the flow: how much more is being added in a single year. The debt is the stock: the total in the tub. A smaller deficit still adds water to the tub, and the water level falls only when the government runs a surplus, as it did in the four years from 1998 to 2001. Figure 19.11 illustrates the relationship.

On November 18, 2026, the total public debt outstanding was about $40.7 trillion, according to the Treasury Department's Debt to the Penny data. That total has two parts. About $32.9 trillion was debt held by the public: Treasury securities owned by individuals, banks, businesses, pension funds, state and local governments, the Federal Reserve, and foreign investors. About $7.8 trillion was intragovernmental debt, which is money one part of the federal government owes to another, as you will see when you read about trust funds later in this section. Economists usually focus on debt held by the public, since that is the amount the government has actually borrowed from outside its own accounts.

Key Concepts

Why Deficits Occur

Why does the federal government run deficits? Economists describe several causes, and most deficits result from a combination.

Recessions. As you learned in Section 1, a recession lowers tax revenue and raises spending on unemployment benefits and other programs automatically. Part of the deficit that appears in a recession is called the cyclical deficit because it goes away as the economy recovers. The deficit in fiscal year 2009 reached 9.8 percent of GDP for this reason, along with the stimulus and the financial rescue. In 2020 the deficit was 14.7 percent of GDP.

Emergencies and wars. Governments often borrow to pay for sudden needs. The federal government borrowed heavily to pay for World War II, and the national debt held by the public reached about 106 percent of GDP in 1946, a record at that time. After the September 11 attacks in 2001 and the wars that followed, the government paid for military and homeland security spending partly with borrowing, as it did after natural disasters such as Hurricane Katrina in 2005.

Tax and spending decisions. When Congress cuts taxes without cutting spending, or increases spending without raising taxes, the deficit grows. The portion of the deficit that remains even when the economy is at full employment is the structural deficit. In recent years, most of the federal deficit has been structural: the 2018, 2019, and 2023 to 2025 deficits shown in Figure 19.4 all occurred in years of low unemployment. The 2025 tax and spending law, for example, was estimated by the Congressional Budget Office to add $3.4 trillion to deficits from 2025 to 2034.

An aging population. Social Security and Medicare costs rise as more Americans reach age 65. As you saw in Chapter 18, these two programs make up about 37 percent of federal outlays. Because their spending is mandatory, it grows under current law unless Congress changes the rules.

Rising interest costs. Interest on the debt is itself a cause of deficits. In fiscal year 2026, net interest cost the government $1,142 billion, 15.4 percent of all outlays. As the debt grows and interest rates change, interest payments grow, which can add to the debt further.

Financing the Debt

When the government spends more than it collects, it must borrow the difference. It does so by selling Treasury securities, which are promises to repay the borrowed money with interest. Savers who buy them are lending to the government, and the government's promise to repay is regarded as one of the safest in the world. Investors regularly buy new Treasury securities at auctions run by the Treasury Department. The government sells four main kinds.

Treasury bills (T-bills) are short-term securities that mature in a year or less. They pay no regular interest. Instead, buyers purchase them at a discount, for example at $98 for a bill that will pay $100 at maturity, and the difference is the interest. Treasury notes mature in two to ten years and pay interest every six months. Treasury bonds mature in twenty or thirty years and also pay interest every six months. A fourth kind, Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities, adjusts the amount owed for inflation. Individuals can also buy U.S. savings bonds directly from the government.

The interest rates on these securities differ by length, which is the idea behind the distinction in California's economics standards between short-term and long-term interest rates. On November 19, 2026, the Treasury's market yield was about 4.34 percent on a three-month bill, 4.76 percent on a two-year note, 5.04 percent on a ten-year note, and 5.40 percent on a thirty-year bond. Longer securities usually pay more because lenders must tie up their money longer and take a greater risk that inflation or interest rates will change. The rates are also important for the whole economy. Mortgage rates, car loan rates, and business loan rates tend to follow Treasury yields, so the government's borrowing costs affect borrowing costs for everyone.

Who owns the debt? Figure 19.13 shows the two parts. Part of the intragovernmental debt is held in trust funds, government accounts that collect money for a specific purpose and invest any surplus in special Treasury securities. The Social Security trust funds are the largest example: when payroll tax collections exceeded benefits, the surplus was lent to the rest of the government, which spent it, and the trust funds received securities as a promise to repay it. Of the debt held by the public, a large part is owned by Americans: households, banks, mutual funds, pension funds, state and local governments, and the Federal Reserve. Foreign investors hold the rest. In July 2026, foreign holdings of Treasury securities totaled about $9.2 trillion, with Japan, the United Kingdom, and China the largest holders, as Figure 19.13 shows.

Foreign ownership of U.S. debt is sometimes described as a danger, since it means interest flows overseas. Economists point out that foreign investors buy Treasury securities because they are safe and easy to sell, which keeps U.S. borrowing costs lower than they would otherwise be. They also note that the debt is owed in dollars, and the United States controls its own currency, which makes an outright default less likely than for countries that borrow in foreign currencies. Others respond that a heavy dependence on outside lenders could become a problem if they lost confidence. Both views point to the same practical issue: the government's ability to borrow cheaply depends on lenders' trust.

Key Concepts

Can a Country Grow Out of Its Debt?

Most of what you have read about the deficit lumps together two kinds of spending. The primary deficit is the deficit before net interest: it is all federal spending other than interest, minus revenue. Net interest is the cost of the borrowing that has already been done. According to the Congressional Budget Office, the 2025 deficit of $1,775 billion was made up of a primary deficit of $805 billion and net interest outlays of $970 billion. In other words, about 55 percent of the 2025 deficit ($970 ÷ $1,775) was the cost of past borrowing. (CBO's net interest measure is defined slightly differently from the net interest on the public debt used earlier in this chapter, so the figures do not match exactly.)

The primary deficit and interest help explain whether a debt grows faster than the economy. Imagine a government with no primary deficit, which means it collects as much as it spends apart from interest. Its debt still grows each year by the amount of interest it owes. Whether the debt ratio (debt divided by GDP) rises then depends on a race between two rates: the interest rate on the debt and the growth rate of the economy measured in current dollars. If the economy grows faster than the interest rate, the ratio can fall even though the debt in dollars is rising. That is part of how the ratio fell after 1946, and how it fell from 1995 to 2002 (Figure 19.12), when the government also ran surpluses from 1998 to 2001.

If the interest rate is higher than the growth rate, the ratio rises even when the primary budget is balanced, and the government would need a primary surplus to hold the ratio steady. Interest rates and growth are both uncertain. In November 2026 the ten-year Treasury note paid about 5.04 percent, and real GDP grew 2.3 percent in 2025 (Figure 19.2). Adding the inflation rate to real growth gives a rough estimate of growth in current dollars, which you will compare with the interest rate in the Application below.

Economists draw different conclusions from this arithmetic. Some argue that when growth is strong and interest rates are low, a government can carry a large debt safely, and that cutting spending too quickly would slow growth and make the ratio worse. Others reply that interest rates and growth rates can change quickly, and that a government that relies on growth beating interest has no margin of safety if the relationship reverses. Both sides agree on the arithmetic. They disagree about how much to trust the forecast.

Key Concepts

The Effects of the National Debt

Is the national debt a problem? Economists, politicians, and voters give very different answers, and the answers depend as much on values as on numbers. It helps to separate the costs of the debt from its benefits.

The cost of interest. The government must pay interest on what it has borrowed. In fiscal year 2026, net interest was $1,142 billion, more than the federal government spent on defense ($917 billion) according to the figures in Chapter 18. It equaled about 21 percent of federal revenue (1,142 ÷ 5,403), Every dollar spent on interest cannot be spent on schools, roads, or tax cuts. Economists describe this as an opportunity cost of past borrowing.

Crowding out. The crowding-out effect is the reduction in private investment that occurs when government borrowing raises interest rates. The idea is that the government competes with businesses and homebuyers for the same pool of savings. When the government borrows heavily, the interest rate that lenders demand rises, making business loans and mortgages more expensive, so some private projects do not go forward. The size of the effect is debated. Economists note that crowding out is likely to be small when the economy is weak and lenders have idle savings, and larger when the economy is near full employment. Global lenders also supply savings to the U.S. market, which reduces the effect.

Burden on future generations. Debt shifts some costs to the future, because the taxpayers who repay it will be those alive when it comes due. Defenders of borrowing point out that those same future taxpayers also benefit from what the money bought, such as highways, research, and education, and that a growing economy can handle a larger debt. Critics respond that much federal borrowing pays for current benefits, not lasting assets, so future generations pay for spending they did not enjoy.

Risk and confidence. A government that borrows too much may find lenders demanding higher interest rates or losing confidence. Credit rating agencies measure this risk: Standard and Poor's lowered the U.S. rating from AAA to AA+ in 2011, Fitch did so in 2023, and Moody's lowered its rating from Aaa to Aa1 in May 2025. Rating changes can raise borrowing costs, although the United States still borrows at lower rates than most countries.

The benefits. Borrowing lets the government respond to emergencies without raising taxes sharply on struggling households, smooths the cost of large projects over the years they will be used, and helps fight recessions, as you saw in Section 1. Treasury securities also give savers, banks, and pension funds a safe asset. Because of these benefits, few economists argue for a debt of zero. The debate is about how large a debt is wise and how fast it should grow.

Economists often measure the debt relative to the size of the economy, using the debt-to-GDP ratio. A debt of $33 trillion would be harder to carry in a small economy than in a large one, in the same way that a $300,000 mortgage is easier to carry for a family with a high income than for one with a low income. The Congressional Budget Office projected in February 2026 that debt held by the public would rise from 101 percent of GDP in 2026 to 120 percent in 2036, higher than the previous record of 106 percent in 1946. You will examine this projection in the Case Study.

Key Concepts

Rules and Limits: The Debt Limit and Balanced Budgets

Can rules keep deficits in check? Congress and the states have tried several. The most visible federal rule is the debt limit, a dollar ceiling set by law on how much the Treasury may borrow. It is separate from the budget. The limit does not decide how much to spend or tax. It decides whether the Treasury may borrow to pay for what Congress has already decided to spend. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed on July 4, 2025, raised the limit by $5 trillion to $41.1 trillion. Total public debt was about $40.7 trillion on November 18, 2026, so the limit is close. A November 9, 2026, news report said that only $557 billion of room was left under the limit, and Bank of America analysts expected the government to reach it as early as February 2027. They forecast that the Treasury would run out of ways to pay its bills, the so-called X-date, in November 2027 if Congress did not act.

Supporters of the debt limit say that it forces a regular public debate about borrowing. Critics say that it does not control spending, since it only applies after the spending has been approved, and that the threat of a failure to pay is dangerous: if the Treasury missed payments, borrowing costs could rise for everyone, including families who borrow for homes and cars. Congress has raised or suspended the limit many times. The question for voters is whether the limit is a useful warning light or a risky obstacle.

Balanced-budget rules are more common in the states. California, like nearly every state, must pass a balanced budget each year; Proposition 58, approved by voters in 2004, requires the Legislature to do so. Because state tax revenue falls in a recession, a balanced-budget rule can force a state to cut spending or raise taxes when the economy is weak, which is the pro-cyclical policy that you read about in Section 1. California tries to soften that problem with a rainy-day fund. Proposition 2, approved in November 2014, requires regular deposits into the state's Budget Stabilization Account and raised the fund's cap to 10 percent of General Fund revenue, according to the Legislative Analyst's Office.

COUNTER-ARGUMENTShould the Constitution Require a Balanced Budget?

Many people support a balanced-budget requirement, and many economists oppose one. Here is the best case that each side makes.

The case for a rule
Without limits, elected officials gain by cutting taxes and raising benefits now and leaving the cost to later taxpayers. The federal government has borrowed in nearly every year since 2001, and net interest ($1,142 billion in fiscal year 2026) now costs more than the military. A binding rule, supporters say, would bring choices into the open, protect future generations from paying for current benefits, and keep interest from crowding out other spending. They point to the states, where balanced-budget rules are common and the government does not borrow to pay its regular expenses.
The case against a rigid rule
In a recession, revenue falls and benefits rise automatically. If the budget had to balance every year, the government would have to cut spending or raise taxes at the worst moment, which would deepen the downturn. Critics add that rules can be evaded with accounting tricks, and that wars, disasters, and pandemics are best paid for by borrowing, which spreads the cost over the years in which the benefits are enjoyed. Many economists who worry about the debt prefer flexible rules, such as a target for the debt ratio over ten years, to a requirement of annual balance.

Design a rule that tries to capture the best point from each side. Would it need exceptions, and who would decide when they apply?

Is the Federal Deficit Too Large?

The federal government has borrowed money in nearly every year since 2001, and the national debt now exceeds $40 trillion. Some economists call that a danger. Others call it manageable. This case study asks you to examine the evidence and decide how worried to be.

Background

A Matter of Size and Purpose

The federal deficit matters to every taxpayer, because taxpayers ultimately pay the interest on the debt. The deficit is the result of a policy of deficit spending, in which the government borrows to make up the difference between its revenue and its spending. There are several reasons to use deficit spending. One is to pay for emergencies, such as wars, natural disasters, and the COVID-19 pandemic. Another is to carry out expansionary fiscal policy during a recession. A third is to pay for investments, such as highways and research, that will help the economy for many years.

Whatever the reason, the larger the deficit grows, the more controversial it becomes. In fiscal year 2025, the federal deficit was 5.8 percent of GDP, a large number for a year when unemployment was low. The Congressional Budget Office (CBO) projected in February 2026 that the deficit would grow to 6.7 percent of GDP by 2036 and that debt held by the public would reach 120 percent of GDP, higher than at any time in American history. Yet the government can still borrow, and credit markets have not stopped lending. The question for this case study is how to weigh these facts.

Chapter 19 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · What Is Fiscal Policy?
Fiscal policy uses government taxes and spending to influence the economy. Expansionary policy raises total spending; contractionary policy reduces it. Discretionary policy requires new laws, while automatic stabilizers act on their own. Economists compare actual GDP with potential GDP and watch unemployment and inflation to decide which direction policy should take. Time lags, politics, uncertainty, rational expectations, and crowding out limit what fiscal policy can do, and economists disagree about how much government should try to manage the business cycle.
Section 2 · Demand-Side and Supply-Side Policies
Keynesian demand-side policy raises total spending through government purchases, transfers, and tax cuts, and the spending multiplier can magnify the effect. Supply-side policy uses lower tax rates and investment incentives to raise the economy's capacity to produce. The Laffer curve illustrates the debate about how tax rates affect revenue, and studies of dynamic scoring suggest that tax cuts recover part, but not all, of their cost. Estimates of the multiplier vary widely with the condition of the economy.
Section 3 · Deficits and the National Debt
A deficit is a yearly gap between spending and revenue; the national debt is the total owed. The government finances deficits by selling Treasury bills, notes, and bonds. The debt has costs, such as interest and possible crowding out, and benefits, such as protecting jobs in a recession. Its size relative to GDP is a key measure, and whether the ratio rises or falls depends on a race between interest rates and growth. The primary deficit leaves out interest; the debt limit and balanced-budget rules are attempts to restrain borrowing.
