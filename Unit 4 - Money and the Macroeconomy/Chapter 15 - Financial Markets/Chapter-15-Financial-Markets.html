Concept Review

In Chapter 14 you learned that banks are intermediaries that take in deposits and make loans, and that money is created as lending and deposits expand. Banks are one important part of a larger network. Chapter 1 explained that voluntary exchange makes both sides better off, and that capital (the tools, buildings, and equipment used to produce goods and services) is one of the basic factors of production. This chapter shows how the savings of households find their way to the people who build capital.

Chapter 15 Key Concept

The financial system consists of institutions and markets, such as banks, insurance companies, bond markets, and stock markets, that help transfer funds from savers to borrowers and investors. By pooling savings, spreading risk, and putting a price on the future, financial markets let an economy turn savings into investment. Every investor faces a trade-off: the chance of a higher return comes with a higher risk.

Why the Concept Matters

Suppose you work after school at a Shafter packing house and put $40 from each paycheck into an account. Where does that money go? It does not sit in a drawer. The bank lends it to a family that is buying a house in Bakersfield, to a farmer who is planting a new almond orchard, or to a business that is buying a truck. If you instead buy a share of a company's stock, you become a part-owner of that company and hope it grows. If you buy a bond, you are lending to a corporation or a government, such as a school district that is building a classroom. Each choice puts your money to work in a different way and carries a different chance of gain and loss. Understanding financial markets will help you read the news, make your own decisions about saving, and see how your choices connect to everyone else's.

Key Concepts

The Financial System

There are two things you can do with income: spend it or save it. Savings is income that is not spent on current wants. When you set aside $40 from a paycheck, you give up something you could have bought today. In exchange, you hold a claim on purchasing power in the future. Chapter 1 described this as a trade-off, and economists call what you give up the opportunity cost.

Savings has a second meaning for the whole economy. Economists use the word investment for spending that creates new capital goods: factories, machines, tools, software, and buildings. A farm family that buys a new almond harvester has invested. So has a company that builds a solar plant near Mojave or a school district that constructs a new gym. In everyday speech, people also say they 'invest' when they buy stock, but economists separate the two ideas. Buying a share of stock from another investor does not build anything new by itself. It is a transfer of ownership. This chapter uses investment in the economic sense when it discusses the economy, and it will say buying a financial asset when it discusses a person's decision.

The two ideas are connected, because the funds for investment come from savings. If a family saves $5,000 and a bank lends that money to a business, the business can buy equipment. If nobody saved, nobody would have funds to lend, and less capital would be built. The financial system is the network of institutions and markets that moves savings from those who have extra funds (savers) to those who want to use them (borrowers and investors). It includes banks, credit unions, insurance companies, mutual funds, bond markets, and stock markets. Without it, a person with money and a person with an idea would have a hard time finding each other. Think of the financial system as a set of roads that carry funds between households, businesses, and governments.

How Much Do Americans Save?

Economists measure how much households save with the personal saving rate. The Bureau of Economic Analysis defines it as personal saving as a percentage of disposable personal income, which is income left after taxes. A saving rate of 5 percent means that, on average, Americans kept 5 cents of each after-tax dollar and spent the rest. Figure 15.2 shows the rate for selected years since 1990.

The numbers move for good reasons. The rate fell to just over 2 percent in 2005, as families borrowed against rising home values and spent more freely. After the Great Recession, it rose to nearly 6 percent in 2010. In 2020 it jumped to an average of about 15 percent, with a monthly peak of 31.8 percent in April. Pandemic lockdowns closed many places to spend money, and government relief payments boosted incomes at the same time. In 2022, as prices rose quickly and people spent their pandemic savings, the rate dropped to about 3.5 percent. In 2025 it averaged about 5.4 percent, and the most recent monthly estimates, for the summer of 2026, were between 3.8 and 4.5 percent.

Why does the rate matter? A higher saving rate means that more funds are available to be lent for investment. It also gives families a cushion when income falls. But the connection is not automatic. A country can invest more than it saves by borrowing from savers abroad, and the United States has often done so. Chapter 21 returns to this idea when it discusses international trade and capital flows.

Key Concepts

Financial Assets and Financial Intermediaries

When you save, you receive a financial asset: a claim on someone else's income or property. A savings account is a financial asset. It is a claim on a bank. A share of stock is a claim on a part of a company. A bond is a claim to be repaid with interest. Financial assets are different from physical assets, such as a house or a tractor, because their value comes from a contract and a promise, not from their physical form. They are also different from money, because most financial assets pay interest, dividends, or other returns and their values can change.

Financial assets are bought and sold in financial markets, places (physical or electronic) where buyers and sellers of financial assets meet. But most savers do not trade directly with the people who borrow. A Shafter nurse who saves $200 a month does not want to research every possible business, collect payments, and pursue borrowers who do not repay. She uses a financial intermediary: an institution that gathers savings from many people and lends or invests them. Banks are the best-known intermediaries, but there are others.

Intermediaries provide four services. First, they pool savings, so that a farm needing $2 million can borrow from the combined deposits of thousands of people. Second, they reduce transaction costs, because it is much cheaper for one institution to evaluate loan applications than for every saver to do it alone. Third, they spread risk: if one borrower fails to repay, the loss is shared among many depositors rather than falling on one person. Fourth, they provide information and liquidity, which means that they let savers get their money back quickly when they need it. In exchange, the intermediary charges borrowers a higher interest rate than it pays savers and keeps the difference to cover costs and earn a profit, as Chapter 14 explained.

Key Concepts

Classifying Financial Markets

Economists sort financial markets in two ways. The first is by the length of time before the borrower must repay. A money market is the market for financial assets with a maturity (time to repayment) of one year or less. Examples include U.S. Treasury bills, commercial paper (short-term IOUs issued by corporations), and large certificates of deposit. Money-market assets are very safe and easy to turn into cash, but they pay lower interest. A capital market is the market for financial assets with a maturity of more than one year, including stocks, which have no maturity at all, and bonds. Companies use capital markets to raise the long-term funds that they need to build factories or buy equipment.

The second way to sort markets is by whether the asset is new or already owned. In the primary market, a borrower sells newly issued financial assets directly to buyers, and the borrower receives the money. When a company sells shares of stock for the first time in an initial public offering (IPO), or when the U.S. Treasury auctions new bonds, the transaction takes place in the primary market. In the secondary market, investors buy and sell assets that were issued earlier. The original borrower does not receive the money. If you buy 10 shares of a company on the New York Stock Exchange from another investor, you are using the secondary market, and the company gets nothing from your purchase.

Why does the secondary market matter if the company does not get the money? Because it gives investors liquidity, the ability to sell an asset quickly at a fair price. Few people would buy a 30-year bond or a share of a new company if they were locked in until the borrower repaid. Knowing that they can sell in the secondary market makes them willing to buy in the primary market. A secondary market also produces prices every day, and those prices send signals about how investors rate each company's prospects. Companies use those signals to decide whether to expand.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVETandas: Saving Together Without a Bank

Millions of people around the world save and borrow outside the formal banking system. In Mexico and in many Mexican-American communities in the Central Valley, families sometimes organize a tanda (also called a cundina). A group of people, often relatives, co-workers, or neighbors, agrees to put in a fixed amount of money every week or month. Each period, one member receives the whole pot. Over the course of the cycle, every member contributes the same total and receives the same total, but the members who go first get the use of the money early, like a loan, while those who go last, in effect, make a forced savings plan.

Rotating savings groups exist under different names in many countries, from Nigeria to India to the Caribbean. A tanda works because members trust one another, so it needs no contracts or interest. It also has limits: there is no insurance if a member disappears with the money, and the amounts are small compared with what a bank can lend. Economists study such groups because they show that people everywhere want what financial intermediaries provide: a way to pool savings and turn them into usable lumps of money. Different Assets, Different Interest Rates

Financial assets pay different returns. Figure 15.6 compares four interest rates in November 2026. The first two, the national average rates for savings accounts and 12-month certificates of deposit, come from the FDIC. The second two are yields on U.S. Treasury securities, which are loans to the federal government. Treasury yields were far higher than the average bank rates. The reason is partly that many banks pay savers as little as they can get away with, while savers who shop around, or who use online banks, can usually earn more than the national average. It is also partly that Treasuries and bank deposits are not exactly the same product: a savings account lets you withdraw at any time, while a Treasury note locks your money for its term unless you sell it at the market price.

The interest rate on any asset depends on how long the money is tied up, how risky the borrower is, how easy the asset is to sell, and the general level of rates in the economy. Short-term rates in the economy are strongly influenced by the Federal Reserve, a topic of Chapter 20. Notice that the 10-year Treasury yield was higher than the three-month yield, as it usually is, because lenders ask for more to commit their money for longer. Section 4 returns to the relationship between the length of a loan and its interest rate.

Key Concepts

Why Are You Investing?

Before an investor chooses a stock, a bond, or a savings account, she needs to answer a more basic question: why am I investing? An investment objective is a goal that the investor wants to reach with her money. Different goals call for different choices. A student who wants to buy a used truck in 18 months has a short-term objective. A young couple saving for a down payment on a Bakersfield home in seven years has a medium-term objective. A 20-year-old who wants to retire at 65 has a long-term objective of more than 40 years.

Two issues shape every investment decision. The first is time horizon, the length of time before the money is needed. The second is the investor's willingness and ability to accept risk, the chance of losing some or all of the money. A person with a long time horizon can ride out a bad year in the stock market, because she does not have to sell until prices recover. A person who needs the money next spring cannot. This is why financial planners usually suggest keeping money for short-term goals in safe, easy-to-sell assets, such as an insured savings account, and putting money for long-term goals in assets that offer higher potential returns, even though they can lose value in the short run.

Most advisers also recommend building an emergency fund before investing: cash, usually enough to cover three to six months of essential expenses, kept in an insured account. An emergency fund prevents a car repair or a lost job from forcing an investor to sell stocks at a bad time. Chapter 10 discussed budgeting and the importance of paying yourself first. Once the emergency fund is in place, the decision of how to invest the rest depends on the objective, the time horizon, and the tolerance for risk. This textbook gives general educational information. It does not tell you what to buy, and every household's situation is different.

Key Concepts

The Power of Compound Interest

A good objective is useless without a way to measure how fast money grows. Chapter 14 introduced simple interest, which is paid only on the original principal. Most savings and investments earn compound interest: interest that is paid on both the original principal and on the interest that was already earned. Each period's earnings are added to the balance, so the next period's earnings are calculated on a larger base. The effect is small at first and then very large.

Suppose you put $1,000 in an account that pays 7 percent a year, and you never touch it. After one year you have $1,070. In the second year you earn 7 percent of $1,070, or $74.90, not $70. After 10 years you have about $1,967. After 30 years you have about $7,612. With simple interest at the same rate, the interest would add only $70 a year, or $2,100 over 30 years, for a balance of $3,100. The difference is the extra interest earned on interest.

Compounding makes time the most valuable asset that a young investor owns. Figure 15.8 shows what happens when a person puts away $100 every month, a modest sum that a part-time worker could manage, and leaves it for 40 years. At a 0 percent return (money kept in a drawer), the total is $48,000, exactly what was put in. At a 3 percent annual return, it grows to about $92,600. At 7 percent, it grows to about $262,500. Of that last total, only $48,000 came from the saver's own pocket; the other $214,500 or so came from returns on the money. Notice that the line for 7 percent is nearly flat for the first decade and then bends sharply upward. The early years look dull, but they build the base that the later years multiply.

Key Concepts

Risk and Return

Investors put money to work to earn a return: the gain or loss on an investment, usually expressed as a percentage of the amount invested. A return can come from interest, from dividends (a share of a company's profits), or from a rise in the price of the asset, which is called a capital gain. If you buy a share for $50, receive $1 in dividends, and later sell it for $55, your total return is ($1 + $5) ÷ $50 = 12 percent.

The other side of the coin is risk, the chance that an investment's actual return will differ from what was expected, including the chance of losing money. Risk comes in several forms. Business risk is the chance that a particular company does poorly or fails. Market risk is the chance that an entire market falls, as stocks did in 2008 and 2022. Inflation risk is the chance that rising prices will eat up the real value of a return. Interest rate risk is the chance that rising interest rates will reduce the market price of a bond you hold, as Section 4 explains. Liquidity risk is the chance that you will not be able to sell an asset quickly without accepting a lower price.

Investors do not like risk. A basic principle of finance is that, to attract investors, an asset that is riskier must offer a higher expected return than a safer asset. The extra expected return that compensates investors for taking on risk is called the risk premium. The principle explains why a savings account pays less than a corporate bond and why a corporate bond pays less than the average stock. Notice the word expected. A higher expected return is not a guaranteed return. Risky assets pay more on average over long periods because they sometimes pay far less, or nothing at all, in the short run.

Figure 15.9 shows the pattern using the long-run record of U.S. assets from 1928 to 2025. The horizontal axis shows each asset's standard deviation, a statistical measure of how much its annual returns jump around. The vertical axis shows the average annual return. Stocks earned the highest average return, almost 12 percent a year, but their returns also varied the most: in some years they gained more than 30 percent, and in others they lost more than 40 percent. Short-term Treasury bills earned about 3.4 percent a year, with very little variation. Key Concepts

Diversification: Do Not Put All Your Eggs in One Basket

The old advice about eggs and baskets has a financial name: diversification, the strategy of spreading money among many different investments to reduce risk. If you own stock in only one company, a single piece of bad news, such as a failed product or a scandal, could wipe out your savings. If you own stocks in 500 different companies, the bad luck of any one company is only a small part of your total. Some companies will do poorly while others do well, and the ups and downs partly cancel one another.

Kern County farmers know this principle. A grower who plants only almonds is exposed to a bad almond price, a drought that cuts water deliveries, or a disease that spreads through the orchards. A grower who also plants pistachios, grapes, or citrus, or who has income from solar leases or off-farm work, does not lose everything if one crop has a bad year. The same logic applies to investing, although a farm's crops are tied to local conditions, while an investor can spread across industries, regions, and countries.

Diversification works best when assets do not all move together. It does not eliminate market risk. In a severe downturn, nearly all stocks fall at the same time, which is why investors often combine stocks with bonds and other assets that behave differently. Diversification also does not guarantee a profit or protect against all losses. Jack Bogle's career is the story of an economist's idea turned into a product that millions of families can use. Key Concepts

The Stock Market

In Chapter 8 you learned that a corporation is owned by its shareholders. A stock (also called a share or an equity) is a certificate of ownership in a corporation. If a company issues one million shares and you buy 1,000 of them, you own one-tenth of one percent of the company. As an owner, you have a claim on a share of its profits and, usually, a vote on important decisions, such as electing the board of directors. Your liability is limited: if the company fails, you can lose the money you paid for the stock, but creditors cannot take your house or your car.

Why do people buy stocks? There are two ways to earn a return. The first is the dividend, a payment of part of the company's profits to its shareholders, usually every three months. The second is a capital gain, the profit from selling a stock for more than you paid for it. If you buy a share for $40 and sell it for $52, your capital gain is $12. The reverse, selling for less than you paid, is a capital loss. Many fast-growing companies pay no dividends at all, because they use their profits to expand. Investors in those companies are betting on capital gains. Mature companies are more likely to pay dividends.

Why do companies sell stock? Because it is a way to raise capital without borrowing. When a company issues bonds or takes out a loan, it must repay the money with interest, whether business is good or bad. When it sells stock, it does not have to repay the money, and it does not have to pay a dividend if profits are low. In exchange, the original owners share ownership and future profits with new shareholders. When a private company sells its shares to the public for the first time, the sale is called an initial public offering (IPO). IPOs are a link between the profit motive and the financial markets: an entrepreneur who has a promising idea can raise millions of dollars from investors who hope to share in the profit, and the prospect of a profit encourages both sides to take risks that help the economy grow. That is the idea behind standard 12.2.8, which describes profit as the incentive to entrepreneurs in a market economy.

There are two main kinds of stock. Common stock gives its owner a vote and a share of the profits, but common shareholders are last in line if the company goes out of business, after creditors and preferred shareholders. Preferred stock usually pays a fixed dividend, and its owners are paid before common shareholders, but preferred shareholders normally do not vote. Most stock that people hold, including the stock in their mutual funds and retirement accounts, is common stock.

Stock Exchanges and Stockbrokers

A stock exchange is an organized marketplace where shares of stock are bought and sold. The two largest in the United States are the New York Stock Exchange (NYSE) and the Nasdaq Stock Market. The NYSE dates to 1792. For two centuries its brokers traded in person on a crowded floor, as in the photograph that opens this chapter. Nasdaq, which began trading in 1971, was the first fully electronic stock market and has no trading floor at all. Today even the NYSE does nearly all of its trading by computer. A company must meet financial and size requirements and pay fees to have its stock listed on an exchange, and an exchange publishes the prices at which each stock trades throughout the day.

Individual investors do not trade directly on the exchange. They use a stockbroker, a person or firm licensed to buy and sell securities for customers. In the past, a customer had to telephone a broker and pay a commission on every trade. Today most people use online brokerage accounts or smartphone apps, and many brokers charge no commission on stock trades, although they earn money in other ways, such as interest on cash balances and payment from the firms that execute the trades. When you place an order, your broker sends it to a market, where it is matched with a seller. Since May 28, 2024, most stock trades in the United States are settled, meaning that the shares and the cash are officially exchanged, one business day after the trade (called T+1), down from two business days.

Investors can place different kinds of orders. A market order says, 'buy or sell now at the best price available.' A limit order says, 'buy only if the price is at or below this limit' (or 'sell only if it is at or above it'). Limit orders give investors more control, but the order may never be filled.

Measuring the Market: Stock Indexes

On a typical day, the news says that the 'market' is up or down. But there are thousands of stocks, and they do not all move together. To summarize their behavior in one number, analysts use a stock index, a number that is calculated from the prices of a selected group of stocks and used to track the performance of a market or part of it. Three indexes are in the news most often.

The Dow Jones Industrial Average (the Dow), first published in 1896, tracks 30 large U.S. companies. It is price-weighted, which means that stocks with higher share prices have more influence on the index, regardless of the size of the company. The S&P 500, introduced in its present form on March 4, 1957, tracks 500 large U.S. companies and is weighted by the market value of each company's publicly traded shares, so that bigger companies matter more. Because it covers about 500 companies and a large share of the total value of the market, most professionals consider it the best single measure of the U.S. stock market. The Nasdaq Composite, started in 1971, tracks all of the stocks listed on the Nasdaq exchange, a group of more than 3,000 companies that includes many technology firms. Because it is concentrated in technology, it tends to swing more widely than the S&P 500.

Bull Markets and Bear Markets

Investors use animal names for the direction of the market. A bull market is a period in which stock prices are rising, usually defined as a rise of 20 percent or more from a recent low. A bear market is a period of falling prices, usually defined as a decline of 20 percent or more from a recent high. A smaller decline of 10 percent or more is called a correction. The names come from the way the animals attack: a bull thrusts its horns up, and a bear swipes its paws down.

Figure 15.14 shows the four bear markets that began in the 2000s and 2020s. The first two were long. After the dot-com boom (which you will study in the Case Study), the S&P 500 fell 49.1 percent from March 24, 2000, to October 9, 2002. After the housing and financial crisis, it fell 56.8 percent from October 9, 2007, to March 9, 2009, a decline of more than half in 17 months. The 2020 decline was different. In just 33 days, from February 19 to March 23, 2020, the index fell 33.9 percent as the COVID-19 pandemic shut down the economy, among the fastest declines of that size on record. And in 2022, as the Federal Reserve raised interest rates to fight inflation, it fell 25.4 percent over about nine months.

Stock prices fall because investors revise their expectations about future profits or about the interest rates they could earn elsewhere. The point of these episodes for an investor is not to predict them, because no one does so reliably. It is to understand that declines are a normal feature of owning stock. Each of these four bear markets was followed by a new high, but the recovery took years in the first two cases. An investor who needed money in the middle of a bear market had to sell at a loss. An investor with a long time horizon who did not sell, and who kept investing, eventually saw prices recover. This is why the time horizon discussed in Section 2 is so important.

Key Concepts

Why Buy Bonds?

A bond is a contract in which a borrower promises to repay borrowed money, with interest, on a fixed schedule. When you buy a bond, you become a lender. Governments and corporations issue bonds when they need to borrow large sums for long periods. A company that wants to build a new plant may sell bonds instead of stock because bonds do not dilute ownership, and the interest payments are a business expense that reduces its taxes. A city that wants to build a new library may sell bonds because it can repay the debt over decades with future tax revenue.

To understand bonds, you need four terms. The par value is the amount that the bond's issuer promises to pay the holder when the bond comes due, also called the face value. A typical corporate bond has a par value of $1,000. The maturity is the date on which the bond is repaid. The coupon rate is the annual interest payment, stated as a percentage of par value. A $1,000 bond with a 5 percent coupon rate pays $50 a year in interest. Finally, the yield is the return that an investor actually earns on the bond, taking into account the price that she paid for it. If she buys the same $1,000 bond in the secondary market for $920, the $50 annual interest payment is a higher return on her investment than the 5 percent coupon rate. (The simplest measure of yield, the current yield, equals the annual interest payment divided by the price: 50 ÷ 920 = 5.4 percent.)

Why do people buy bonds? There are three reasons. First, bonds provide a steady income: a fixed payment on a known schedule, which is appealing to retirees. Second, bonds are usually less risky than stocks. A bondholder is a creditor, and creditors must be paid before shareholders, so a company that cannot pay its bills may still pay its bondholders something. Third, bonds help diversify a portfolio, because they often do not move in the same direction as stocks. The price of this safety is lower expected returns, as Figure 15.9 showed. Over 1928–2025, 10-year Treasury bonds averaged about 4.8 percent a year, against about 11.9 percent for stocks.

Kinds of Bonds and Credit Ratings

There are four main kinds of bonds. Treasury bonds are issued by the federal government. Corporate bonds are issued by companies. Municipal bonds, or munis, are issued by states, cities, counties, and other local governments, including school districts. Interest on most municipal bonds is exempt from federal income tax, which lets governments borrow at lower interest rates. Federal agency bonds are issued by government-sponsored organizations, such as those that support the housing market.

Kern County voters have used municipal bonds to pay for schools. In November 2016, they approved Measure J, a bond of about $500 million for the Kern Community College District, which includes Bakersfield College, and Measure K, a bond of nearly $280 million for the Kern High School District, which the district planned to use for classroom modernization at campuses such as North High and Bakersfield High. Both measures passed with a majority of about 62 to 63 percent, more than the 55 percent that California requires for school bonds. These are general obligation bonds, which are repaid from property taxes. The local property taxpayers become, in effect, the borrowers, and the investors who buy the bonds are the lenders. The bonds let the districts build today and spread the cost over many years, which is one reason that people disagree about when governments should borrow.

Not all borrowers are equally likely to repay. To help investors judge, independent firms such as S&P Global Ratings, Moody's, and Fitch assign credit ratings. On the S&P scale, AAA is the highest rating and means that the issuer has an extremely strong ability to pay. Ratings from AAA down to BBB- are called investment grade. Bonds rated BB+ and lower are speculative, and a bond issued by a company with a low rating is known as a junk bond or a high-yield bond. To attract buyers, a lower-rated borrower must offer a higher interest rate, which is another example of the risk premium that you studied in Section 2. Junk bonds can pay generous yields, but a higher share of them default, which means that the issuer fails to make the promised payments.

What Moves Bond Prices?

Once a bond is issued, its price in the secondary market can rise or fall, even though the coupon payments and par value are fixed. The reason is that the price of a bond and its yield move in opposite directions. Suppose that you buy a newly issued 10-year bond with a 4 percent coupon rate, and soon afterward, market interest rates rise, so that newly issued bonds like it pay 6 percent. Who would pay $1,000 for your bond, which pays only $40 a year, when $1,000 buys a new bond that pays $60 a year? To sell, you must lower the price until the bond gives the buyer a yield similar to the new 6 percent bonds. Using the standard formula and annual coupons, the price at which a 10-year 4 percent bond gives a 6 percent yield is about $853, a loss of nearly 15 percent for the seller. If rates had fallen to 2 percent instead, your bond would be worth about $1,180.

Figure 15.18 shows this relationship. This is the interest rate risk mentioned in Section 2: a bondholder who must sell before maturity can lose money when rates have risen. A bondholder who keeps the bond until maturity receives the par value no matter what the price did in between, as long as the issuer does not default. The longer the maturity, the more sensitive the price is to a change in rates, because the fixed payments are locked in for longer.

Other factors also affect bond prices. Credit risk: if a company's profits fall and investors worry about default, its bond prices fall and its yields rise. Inflation: because a bond's payments are fixed in dollars, higher inflation reduces their purchasing power, so investors demand higher yields when they expect prices to rise faster. Supply and demand: when the Treasury sells more bonds, or when foreign investors buy fewer, prices can fall. And Federal Reserve policy: the Fed sets short-term rates and also buys and sells bonds, so its decisions affect the whole structure of interest rates. Chapter 20 explains how.

Key Concepts

Short-Term and Long-Term Interest Rates

The California standards ask students to distinguish between short-term and long-term interest rates and to explain their relative significance. Bonds make the difference easy to see. A short-term interest rate applies to loans of a year or less, such as a three-month Treasury bill. A long-term interest rate applies to loans of many years, such as a 10-year Treasury note or a 30-year mortgage. The two do not always move together.

The pattern of yields at different maturities is called the yield curve. Figure 15.19 shows the Treasury yield curve on November 20, 2026. The three-month bill yielded 4.32 percent, the 10-year note 4.92 percent, and the 30-year bond 5.31 percent. The curve slopes upward, which is the usual pattern: lenders ask for a higher rate to tie up their money for longer, to compensate for the chance that inflation or interest rates will rise during that time. In this case, the 10-year yield was 0.60 percentage point above the three-month yield.

Sometimes the curve slopes downward, so that short-term yields are higher than long-term yields. This is called an inverted yield curve. It happens when the Federal Reserve has raised short-term rates to fight inflation, and investors expect rates, and the economy, to be weaker in the future. Figure 14.12 in Chapter 14 showed that short-term rates were above long-term rates in January 2023 and January 2024. Inverted curves have often come before recessions, which is why economists watch the spread between long-term and short-term yields. But an inversion is a signal, not a guarantee.

Why do the two kinds of rates matter to you? Short-term rates, strongly influenced by the Fed, determine what you earn on a savings account and what you pay on a credit card. Long-term rates determine what a family pays on a 30-year home mortgage, what a business pays to finance a new plant, and what a school district pays on its bonds. When long-term rates rise, borrowing for big purchases becomes more expensive, and spending on houses and factories tends to fall. That is how financial markets connect to the rest of the economy.

Key Concepts

Other Financial Instruments

Stocks and bonds are not the only choices. A certificate of deposit (CD) is a deposit at a bank or credit union that pays a fixed interest rate for a fixed term, such as 12 months, and charges a penalty for early withdrawal. A money market fund invests in short-term, high-quality assets and tries to hold its price steady. Both are safe and low in return, as shown in Figure 15.6. Real estate (land, houses, and rental buildings) is another asset that many families own, and in the long run it has earned an average return between that of bonds and stocks. Commodities, such as gold or oil, are goods whose prices can swing widely, and they do not pay interest or dividends.

For most people, the easiest way to own a spread of stocks and bonds is through a fund. A mutual fund, which you met in Section 1, pools money from many investors, and a professional manager buys a portfolio of stocks, bonds, or both. Investors buy shares in the fund, and prices are set once a day. A fund gives a small investor instant diversification: with $100, a student can own a small piece of hundreds of companies. Funds charge an annual fee called an expense ratio, stated as a percentage of the investor's money. A fund with a 0.50 percent expense ratio takes $5 a year for every $1,000 invested.

An index fund is a mutual fund (or other fund) that does not try to beat the market. It simply buys all the stocks or bonds in a particular index, such as the S&P 500, in the same proportions. Because it needs little research and trades rarely, its costs are low. An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a fund whose shares trade on a stock exchange all day, like a stock. Many ETFs also track indexes. According to the Investment Company Institute, U.S. mutual funds held $31.4 trillion and ETFs held $13.4 trillion at the end of 2025, and 53.9 percent of U.S. households (72.7 million) owned mutual funds. Fees have fallen: the average equity mutual fund charged 0.40 percent in 2025, down from 0.99 percent in 2000, and index equity ETFs averaged 0.14 percent (Figure 15.20).

Many Americans own these assets inside retirement accounts, special accounts that offer tax advantages in exchange for limits on early withdrawal. A 401(k) is offered by an employer, who may add matching contributions. An Individual Retirement Account (IRA) is opened by an individual. These accounts are not investments themselves. They are containers that can hold funds, stocks, bonds, and CDs. The size of the account matters less than starting early, as the compound interest in Section 2 showed. (This is general information, not individual advice.)

A relative gives you $5,000 to put toward a house down payment in about eight years. You are deciding between buying a single corporate bond issued by one company or buying shares of a bond fund that holds hundreds of different bonds.

Buy one corporate bond
You know the coupon rate and the maturity date, and you may receive a higher yield from a lower-rated company. But if that one company runs into trouble and cannot pay, you could lose a large share of your money.
Buy a bond fund
Hundreds of bonds spread the risk and a professional or an index handles the details, but you pay an annual fee, and the fund has no maturity date, so its price can change with interest rates and you may get back less than you put in.

CASE STUDY

The Rise and Fall
of the Dot-Coms

In the late 1990s, investors rushed to buy shares in companies that promised to build the Internet economy. Prices soared, hundreds of young companies sold stock to the public, and then the boom collapsed. The episode is a case study in how financial markets raise capital, how risk and return are linked, and how hope can push prices far above what companies earn.

Background

A Boom Built on a New Technology

The World Wide Web became widely available to the public in the mid-1990s. Entrepreneurs saw that the Internet could change how people shop, search, communicate, and advertise, and a wave of dot-com companies, whose names ended in the '.com' of a Web address, was born. Most were young and lost money. To pay for programmers, advertising, and warehouses, they needed capital, and they raised it from venture capitalists, who invest in new companies in exchange for part ownership, and then from the public, by selling stock in initial public offerings (IPOs).

Investors were eager. The Internet really was a new technology, the economy was growing strongly, and a tax law passed in 1997 lowered the tax rate on capital gains. Stock prices rose, and rising prices attracted more buyers, including many first-time investors who bought through online brokerages. IPOs were popular. According to finance professor Jay Ritter's data, the average IPO share jumped 71.2 percent on its first day of trading in 1999 and 56.3 percent in 2000 (Document B). Some investors paid little attention to profits. Many bought shares of companies that had never earned a dollar and judged them by how many people visited their Web sites or how fast their sales grew. The Nasdaq Composite Index, heavy in technology stocks, closed at 1,052 at the end of 1995 and 4,069 at the end of 1999. It rose 85.6 percent in 1999 alone (Document A).

The boom ended in spring 2000. The index closed at a record 5,048.62 on March 10, 2000. Meanwhile, the Federal Reserve raised its target for short-term interest rates from 4.75 percent in the spring of 1999 to 6.50 percent by May 2000, which made safe investments more attractive and borrowing more expensive. Many dot-coms were running out of cash, and as investors realized that few of them would ever earn enough to justify their prices, they began to sell. The Nasdaq fell 39.3 percent in 2000 and kept falling until it reached a closing low of 1,114.11 on October 9, 2002, almost 78 percent below its peak. Hundreds of companies disappeared. The number of IPOs fell from 380 in 2000 to 80 in 2001. Workers lost jobs, and families that held stock in retirement accounts lost savings. The Nasdaq did not close above its March 2000 peak again until April 23, 2015, more than 15 years later.

The aftermath produced changes. Investors learned to ask whether a company could earn a profit. Congress passed the Sarbanes-Oxley Act in 2002 after accounting scandals at companies such as Enron and WorldCom, requiring tougher financial reporting by public companies. And the surviving companies, among them Amazon, went on to become some of the largest in the world. The boom had overpaid for the Internet's promise in the short run, but the technology itself did change the economy. Both facts are part of the story.

Chapter 15 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Savings and Investment
Savings is income not spent, and investment is spending that creates new capital goods. The financial system, which includes banks, credit unions, insurers, pension funds, mutual funds, and markets, moves savings to borrowers by pooling funds, reducing costs, spreading risk, and providing liquidity. Financial markets are classified as money or capital markets and as primary or secondary markets.
Section 2 · Investing in a Market Economy
Investors choose assets based on their objectives, time horizon, and tolerance for risk. Compound interest rewards early saving, and the Rule of 72 estimates doubling time. Higher expected returns come with higher risk, and diversification reduces the risk that any one investment will ruin a portfolio, though it cannot remove market risk.
Section 3 · Buying and Selling Stocks
Stock is ownership in a corporation. Investors earn returns through dividends and capital gains. Brokers trade stocks on exchanges such as the NYSE and Nasdaq. Stock indexes such as the S&P 500 measure the market, and bull and bear markets show that prices can fall sharply. Regulators protect investors from fraud but not from losses.
Section 4 · Bonds and Other Financial Instruments
A bond is a loan with a par value, coupon rate, and maturity. Treasury, corporate, and municipal bonds differ in risk and yield, and credit ratings measure default risk. Bond prices move opposite to yields, and short-term and long-term rates can differ, as the yield curve shows. Mutual funds, index funds, and ETFs offer diversification at low cost.
