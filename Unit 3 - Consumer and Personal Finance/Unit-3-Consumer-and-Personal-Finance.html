Unit Overview

Units 1 and 2 looked at the economy from the outside. You learned that scarcity forces choices, that every choice has an opportunity cost, and that prices, businesses, and labor markets coordinate millions of decisions. Unit 3 turns the lens around and points it at you. Every household is a small economy: it earns income by selling labor, it decides how to divide that income among competing uses, and it often borrows against the future to buy something today. The tools of economics (opportunity cost, incentives, marginal thinking, and the price of money) are the same tools that help a person run a budget, compare two loans, or decide whether a health plan is worth its price.

The four chapters fit together like the parts of a single financial life. Chapter 10, Budgeting and Money Management, lays the foundation: a plan built on take-home pay, a safe and low-cost place to keep money, and a habit of saving so that interest and time work in your favor. Chapter 11, Credit, shows the borrower’s side of the same ledger. The interest rate that rewards a saver is a cost to a borrower, and your record as a borrower becomes a credit report and a score that follow you for years, which is why protecting your identity belongs in the same chapter. Chapter 12, Wise Choices for Big Purchases, applies budgeting and credit to the largest decisions a household makes (a place to live, a vehicle, an education) and adds insurance, the tool that shares the risk of rare but costly events. Chapter 13, Getting Out on Your Own, returns to where income comes from: choosing a career, reading a pay stub, understanding taxes and tax forms, and planning for retirement. Chapter 10 treats take-home pay as a given, and Chapter 13 explains how that number is produced, which completes the circle.

These ideas are not abstract in Kern County. California’s statewide minimum wage is $16.90 an hour in 2026, and the median household income in Kern County was $70,210 in 2024, but the county’s unemployment rate of 7.9 percent in September 2026 was well above California’s 5.1 percent, so many families know what it means to plan around uncertain hours. Distribution centers near Shafter and Bakersfield, farms and packing houses, oil and energy employers, and community college classes at Bakersfield College all appear in this unit’s examples. A student who takes a first job after graduation, or a part-time job while attending Bakersfield College, will face most of the decisions in these chapters within a few years, often without anyone nearby to explain them.

California has decided that every high school graduate should study these skills. Assembly Bill 2927 (2024) added a stand-alone personal finance course to the graduation requirements, beginning with the graduating class of 2030–31, and requires schools to offer the course starting in 2027–28. This unit connects the closest Grade 12 Principles of Economics standards with the topics listed in California Education Code section 51284.5. As in every chapter of this book, the goal is general education, not individualized financial, tax, or legal advice: no single rule fits every person, so the lasting skill is learning which questions to ask (What is the total cost? What is the interest rate? What is the risk? What happens if my income changes?) and how to find reliable information to answer them.

Big Ideas

  1. A budget makes opportunity costs visible. Every dollar has competing uses, and a plan built on net pay decides them in advance. CH. 10
  2. Interest works in both directions. It pays savers and charges borrowers, and time is the ingredient that makes it powerful. CH. 10CH. 11CH. 13
  3. Total cost beats sticker price. The best choice compares everything you will pay over time, and insurance shares the risk of costly surprises. CH. 12
  4. Information is a financial asset. Your credit record, pay stub, tax forms, and personal data can help you or hurt you, so you must read them and protect them. CH. 11CH. 13

Essential Questions

You should be able to answer these by the end of the unit.

  1. How can a budget built on take-home pay help you choose among needs, wants, and savings goals?
  2. What does credit really cost, and how do your credit report and score change the price you pay?
  3. How do you compare the total cost and risk of a big purchase, such as a car, a home, or college, and when does insurance make sense?
  4. Where does a paycheck go, and how do career choices, taxes, and benefits shape the income you keep?
  5. How can saving early, protecting your identity, and planning ahead make you more financially independent?

Why This Unit Matters

A Made-Up Scenario

Alma is a senior at Shafter High School (a hypothetical student). In May she accepts a job at a distribution center near Bakersfield at $19 an hour, to start in June, and plans to take classes at Bakersfield College in the fall.

Within a few months she faces a string of decisions. Her first paycheck deposit is smaller than hours times wage. A store offers 20 percent off a TV if she opens a card. A car dealer asks, “What monthly payment works for you?” A text message that claims to be her bank asks for a code. She must decide whether to enroll in her employer’s health plan, and in January a form called a W-2 arrives in the mail.

None of these decisions is hard once you know the tools, and each can cost hundreds or thousands of dollars if you do not. This unit gives you the tools.

CHAPTER 11Credit

Credit is the other side of the ledger from saving: when you save, you lend to a bank and earn interest, and when you use credit, you borrow and pay it. Look Back and Look Ahead

LOOK BACK: UNITS 1 AND 2

Unit 1 · Chapter 1. Scarcity and opportunity cost return in every budget and every big purchase (Chapters 10, 12, and 13).

Chapters 4–6 · Demand, Supply, and Prices. The interest rate is the price that coordinates savers and borrowers, and the prices of housing, vehicles, and insurance respond to the same forces you studied there (Chapters 11 and 12).

Chapters 7–8 · Market Structures and Business Organizations. Banks, credit unions, and insurers compete for your business, a credit union is a member-owned cooperative, and a stock is a share of ownership in a corporation (Chapter 10).

Chapter 9 · The Role of Labor. Wages depend on the supply of and demand for skills, education is an investment in human capital, and benefits such as health insurance are part of the pay package (Chapters 12 and 13).

LOOK AHEAD: UNIT 4, MONEY AND THE MACROECONOMY

Chapter 14 · Money and Banking. Your deposit in Chapter 10 is the loan that another person receives in Chapter 11, and Chapter 14 explains how banks stand in the middle.

Chapter 15 · Financial Markets. Bonds, stocks, and other markets connect lenders and borrowers on a much larger scale, and they are where retirement savings are invested.

Chapter 16 · Economic Indicators and Measurements. You will see how the Consumer Price Index is built and how economists tell nominal data from real data, the idea behind inflation’s effect on savings.

Chapter 17 · Facing Economic Challenges. You will study unemployment and how technology and trade can change which jobs are available, a theme that begins with the career choices in Chapter 13. Taxes and the Federal Reserve follow in Unit 5.
