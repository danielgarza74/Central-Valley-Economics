Concept Review

Chapter 10 taught you to plan with a budget, to keep money in an insured account, and to save so that interest and time work for you. Credit is the other side of the same ledger. When you save, you lend your money to a bank and earn interest. When you use credit, you borrow someone else's money, and you pay interest. Chapters 14 and 15 will show how banks and financial markets connect savers and borrowers. This chapter looks at the borrower's side: the kinds of credit, what credit costs, how your record becomes a score, and how to protect that record from thieves.

Chapter 11 Key Concept

Credit is the use of someone else's money now with a promise to repay it later, usually with interest. Using credit wisely means knowing its cost (the interest rate and fees), building a record of on-time payment, and guarding your personal information, because your credit history is an asset that lenders, landlords, and sometimes employers use to judge whether you can be trusted with their money.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine that you are 19 and have just started a job at a distribution center near Shafter. At the register of an electronics store, the cashier says, "Open a store card today and save 20 percent on this $450 TV." The offer sounds like free money. But the card charges a rate above 25 percent, and your first payment is due in a month. Whether it is a smart move or an expensive mistake depends on facts you can learn now: what the rate is, how interest is calculated, how the card will appear on your credit report, and whether you could pay the bill in full. By the end of this chapter you will know how to answer those questions, and how to protect your name if someone else tries to borrow in it.

Key Concepts

What Is Credit?

Think about the last time you bought something before you had the money to pay for it. Maybe a parent covered a school trip and you paid them back from your next paycheck. Maybe a friend fronted you for a concert ticket. In each case you used what economists call credit. Credit is the use of someone else's money now, with a promise to pay it back later, usually with extra money added for the privilege. The extra money is called interest, and it is the price of borrowing. The amount you borrow, before any interest is added, is the principal.

In Chapter 10 you were the lender. When you put money into a savings account, the bank paid you interest, because you allowed it to use your money. Credit reverses the roles. When you use a credit card or take out a loan, you are the borrower, and you pay the interest. The same interest rate that rewards a saver is a cost to a borrower. Chapter 14 explains how banks stand in the middle, collecting deposits from savers and lending them to borrowers, and Chapter 15 shows how bonds and other financial markets connect lenders and borrowers on a much larger scale. In the language of the California standards, these are financial markets, and the interest rate is the price that coordinates them.

Why would anyone pay to use money? The answer comes from the ideas you learned in Chapter 1. Resources are scarce, and people often want something now that they cannot afford from this month's income. A family in Delano may need a reliable truck to get to work in the fields this season, not after five years of saving. A student may need tuition this fall. A small business owner in Bakersfield may need to buy a delivery van before the first sale, so that the van can earn the money to repay the loan. In each case, credit allows a person to move purchasing power from the future to the present. The opportunity cost of that choice is the interest paid, and the future income that is already spoken for when the payments come due.

Credit also has a second role, which you will study in Section 2. Lenders do not lend to everyone on the same terms. They look at a person's history of borrowing and repaying, and they use it to decide whether to lend and how much to charge. That history, summarized in a three-digit score, can make a person's loans cheaper or more expensive for years. Using credit well is therefore not only a question of getting what you want today. It is the way you build a reputation that carries into your future.

Key Concepts

Three Kinds of Credit

Credit comes in several forms, and each works differently. Financial educators group most consumer credit into three types, shown in Figure 11.2. The first is installment credit. An installment loan is a loan that you repay in equal payments, called installments, over a set period of time. A car loan of 60 monthly payments is an installment loan. So is a student loan or a personal loan from a bank. At the start you receive the whole amount, and the contract tells you exactly how many payments you owe and when the last one is due. Mortgages are also installment loans, though they last 15 or 30 years.

The second type is revolving credit. With revolving credit, a lender gives you a credit limit, which is the most you may owe at any one time, and you may borrow, repay, and borrow again up to that limit. Credit cards are the most common example. If your limit is $1,000 and you charge $300, you still have $700 available. When you pay off $200, your available credit rises to $900. There is no fixed end date and no fixed payment, other than a minimum that the lender requires each month. The Federal Reserve tracks this type separately from the others. In its November 2026 report on consumer credit, it measured revolving credit at about $1,368.5 billion and nonrevolving credit, mostly installment loans, at about $3,856.0 billion.

The third type is open credit, sometimes called service credit. With open credit, you use a service first and pay at the end of the period, and you must pay the entire bill. Electric, water, and phone bills are examples, and so are some medical bills. Open credit usually has no interest if you pay on time, but a late payment brings a fee, and an unpaid bill can be sent to a collection agency. Utilities and phone companies may also check your credit history before they give you service, which shows how widely credit records are used.

Key Concepts

The Price of Borrowing: Interest and APR

Lenders quote the price of credit as a percentage. The annual percentage rate, or APR, is the yearly cost of borrowing, expressed as a percentage of the amount borrowed. A 12 percent APR means that you pay about 12 cents a year for every dollar you borrow, if you owe the dollar for a full year. Unlike a plain interest rate, the APR is meant to include certain fees that the lender charges, such as an origination fee, so that borrowers can compare offers. Federal law, through the Truth in Lending Act, requires lenders to disclose the APR and the total finance charge before you sign. Read those lines first.

The simplest way to calculate interest is the simple-interest formula. Interest equals principal times the annual rate times the time in years: I = P × r × t. If you borrow $1,000 at 12 percent for one year, the interest is $1,000 × 0.12 × 1 = $120. If you borrow it for six months, the interest is $1,000 × 0.12 × 0.5 = $60. Credit cards and many loans calculate interest on the remaining balance each month rather than once for the whole term. To estimate one month of interest, divide the APR by 12. A balance of $1,500 on a card with a 22 percent APR accrues about $1,500 × (0.22 ÷ 12) = $27.50 in interest in one month.

Interest rates may be fixed or variable. A fixed rate stays the same for the life of the loan, so the payment is predictable. A variable rate can rise or fall as market interest rates change, which means that your payment may change too. Most credit cards have variable rates that move with interest rates set by the Federal Reserve, a topic you will study in Chapter 20. As the chart below shows, the rate you pay depends heavily on the type of credit. Short-term, unsecured credit, such as a credit card, usually costs far more than a loan that is backed by property, such as a car loan. This difference reflects risk. When a lender takes a bigger chance of not being repaid, it charges a higher price to make up for the losses.

Key Concepts

Installment Loans: Cars, Students, and Others

Installment loans fund the biggest purchases most people make before they buy a home. The most common ones for young adults are auto loans and student loans. An installment loan has a set term, and each payment is the same size. Part of each payment is interest, and part reduces the principal. At the beginning of a loan, most of each payment goes to interest because you owe the most. As the balance falls, the interest part shrinks and more of each payment reduces the principal. This process of paying off a loan over time is called amortization.

Consider the numbers in a hypothetical example. You borrow $20,000 to buy a car, and the loan has a 7.54 percent APR for 60 months. That rate matches the Federal Reserve's average for a new-car loan of that length in the third quarter of 2026, although your own rate may be higher or lower. The monthly payment is about $401.14. Over the five years, you would repay about $24,068 in all, which means $4,068 in interest. In the first month, about $125.67 of your $401.14 payment is interest and only about $275.47 reduces the principal. A longer loan lowers the monthly payment but raises the total interest. A 72-month loan for the same $20,000 at the Federal Reserve's 7.17 percent average would have a payment of about $342.62, but the total interest would climb to about $4,668.

Many installment loans are secured. A secured loan is backed by collateral, property that the lender can take if you stop paying. For a car loan, the car is the collateral, and the lender can repossess it if you default. A mortgage is secured by the house. An unsecured loan, such as most credit cards and personal loans, has no collateral, so the lender relies on your promise and your credit record. Because the lender has less protection, unsecured credit generally carries higher rates, a pattern you can see in Figure 11.3.

If you are young or have little credit history, a lender may ask for a cosigner, a person who agrees to repay the debt if you do not. A cosigner is not just vouching for you. He or she is legally responsible for the whole debt, and a missed payment hurts the cosigner's credit record as well as yours. Before you ask a parent or friend to cosign, you should be sure that you can make every payment. Before agreeing to cosign for anyone, you should be prepared to pay the entire loan yourself.

Key Concepts

Credit Cards: How They Work

A credit card is a form of revolving credit that a bank or other company issues. When you swipe, insert, or tap the card, the issuer pays the seller on your behalf, and you now owe the issuer. Credit cards are convenient and, in the United States, offer certain protections if a charge is unauthorized. They also cost much more than most other forms of credit when you carry a balance.

Cards work on a monthly billing cycle, usually about 30 days. At the end of the cycle you receive a statement that lists your purchases, the balance you owe, the interest charged, any fees, and the minimum payment, the smallest amount you must pay by the due date to avoid a late fee and a late-payment record. If you pay the full statement balance by the due date, most cards give you a grace period: you are not charged interest on new purchases. In effect, you used the issuer's money for free. If you pay less than the full balance, interest is charged on the unpaid amount, and the grace period on new purchases is usually lost until you pay the full balance again.

The minimum payment is where many borrowers get into trouble. It is designed to be small, so it feels affordable, but it keeps you in debt for a long time. Many card agreements set the minimum as the larger of a small dollar amount or the interest for the month plus 1 percent of the balance. With that rule, a hypothetical $1,500 balance at a 22 percent APR would take 111 months, more than nine years, to pay off if you never charged anything else, and you would pay about $1,683 in interest, more than the amount you originally borrowed. If you instead paid $100 a month, the balance would be gone in 18 months at a cost of about $270 in interest. Figure 11.4 compares the choices.

Other fees matter too. Many cards charge an annual fee, a late fee, a cash-advance fee, or a foreign-transaction fee. A cash advance, which means using your card to get cash, usually begins charging interest immediately and at a higher rate, with no grace period. Always check the card agreement for the list of fees. Federal law requires issuers to present the key terms in a standard table, often called the "Schumer box," with the APR and the main fees.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVECredit for the Very Poor: Microloans in Bangladesh

In many parts of the world, a poor person cannot get a bank loan because he or she has no collateral and no credit record. In Bangladesh, the Grameen Bank set out to change that. According to the Nobel Prize committee, it offered small, long-term loans on easy terms to very poor people who could not offer collateral. The average loan was about $100, and more than 95 percent of the loans went to women or to groups of women. By 2006, the bank had made loans to more than seven million borrowers. That year, the Grameen Bank shared the Nobel Peace Prize for efforts to create economic and social development from below.

The idea, known as microcredit, works partly through trust. Borrowers often form small groups in which members encourage each other to repay. A woman who borrows $50 to buy materials for weaving baskets can earn money from the baskets, repay the loan, and borrow again. Microcredit is not a cure for poverty, and later research has found that it helps some borrowers more than others. But it shows an important idea from this section: credit is a tool that can create income, not just spend it, when the borrowed money is used to earn more than the loan costs.

Key Concepts

Costly Credit: Payday Loans and Other Traps

Not all lenders offer fair terms. Some businesses focus on borrowers who cannot get credit elsewhere, and charge high prices because their customers have few choices. The best-known example is the payday loan, a small, short-term loan that is due on the borrower's next payday, usually in two to four weeks. The borrower writes a personal check, or lets the lender withdraw from the bank account, for the loan amount plus a fee. In California, state law limits a payday loan to $300, and the fee to 15 percent of the amount of the check. On a $300 check, the borrower receives $255 and pays $45 as a fee. The Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI) regulates these lenders and says that the term cannot be longer than 31 days.

A $45 fee on a $255 loan for two weeks may not sound like much. But when you convert it to a yearly rate, the cost is enormous. The fee is 45 ÷ 255, or 17.6 percent, for two weeks. There are about 26 two-week periods in a year, so the APR is about 17.6 percent × 26, or roughly 460 percent. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) has estimated that a typical two-week payday loan with a fee of $15 per $100 borrowed has an APR of almost 400 percent, compared with about 12 to 30 percent for credit cards. And many borrowers cannot repay in two weeks, so they borrow again, and pay a new fee, and the debt grows.

Other forms of costly credit include car title loans, in which the borrower gives the lender the title to a vehicle as collateral, so that failure to repay can mean losing the car, and rent-to-own stores, which let a customer take home furniture or electronics for weekly payments that can add up to far more than the retail price. "Buy now, pay later" plans, which split a purchase into several payments, can be low-cost when the payments are on time, but they can encourage spending beyond a budget, and missed payments can bring fees. In each case, ask the same question: what is the total I will pay, and what is the APR? If the lender will not answer clearly, walk away.

The cheaper alternatives are often available even to borrowers with little credit. A credit union may offer small "payday alternative" loans at much lower rates. An employer might offer a paycheck advance. A family member, a community organization, or a local assistance program may help with a one-time emergency. And the best protection of all is the one you studied in Chapter 10: an emergency fund that lets you pay for a $400 shock without borrowing at all.

Key Concepts

Should You Borrow? Questions to Ask First

Credit is neither good nor bad by itself. It is a tool, and the same tool can build or destroy a household's budget. Borrowing can be sensible when it buys something that will last longer than the debt or will raise your future income, such as reliable transportation to get to a job, or education that leads to higher pay. It is risky when it pays for things that are used up quickly, such as meals, entertainment, and clothing that you could not afford, because the debt remains long after the enjoyment is gone.

Before you borrow, ask five questions. First, is this a need or a want? A need that cannot wait is a better reason to borrow than a want. Second, what is the total cost? Look at the APR, all fees, and the total of all payments, not only the monthly payment. Third, can I afford the payment in my budget? Many financial educators suggest that total debt payments, other than a mortgage, should stay below about 15 to 20 percent of take-home pay, though no single rule fits every household. Fourth, what happens if my income falls? If you lose hours at work or a car repair takes your savings, will you still be able to pay? Fifth, what else could I do? Saving up, buying something cheaper, or waiting might have a lower opportunity cost than borrowing.

A useful measure is the debt-to-income ratio, which divides your total monthly debt payments by your gross monthly income. A person with $2,400 a month in gross income and $360 in monthly debt payments has a ratio of 360 ÷ 2,400 = 0.15, or 15 percent. Lenders use this ratio to decide whether you can handle another loan, and a high ratio makes borrowing more expensive or impossible. You can use the same ratio on your own to see when you are near your limit. In Section 2, you will see what lenders learn about you before they decide.

Putting It Together

Credit lets you buy now and pay later, but it is never free. The price is the interest rate, and the real cost shows up only when you look at the APR, the fees, and the length of time you will owe. Installment loans spread the cost over a set schedule, revolving credit keeps the balance open, and open credit is a bill that must be paid in full. Costly forms of credit, such as payday loans, can take far more than they give. In the next section, you will look at how lenders measure the risk of lending to you, through a credit report and a credit score, and you will see why your payment record can be worth as much as your income.

Key Concepts

Your Credit Report: A Financial Biography

When you apply for a loan, a lender wants to know one thing above all: will this person pay me back? A lender cannot read your mind, and it cannot call every business you have dealt with. Instead it asks for your credit report, a record of your borrowing and repayment history. A credit report is something like a financial biography. It lists the accounts you have opened, how much you owe, whether you pay on time, and whether any account has been sent to a collection agency.

In the United States, credit reports are compiled by private companies called credit bureaus, also known as consumer reporting agencies. The three big nationwide bureaus are Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. They do not decide whether you get a loan. They collect information from lenders, credit card companies, collection agencies, and courts, which are called furnishers, and they sell reports to businesses that have a legal reason to see them. Because each bureau gets its data from different furnishers, your three reports may not match exactly. A small credit union, for example, may report to only one bureau.

Who may see your report? Federal law lists the businesses with a "permissible purpose." They include lenders who are considering your application, landlords checking an applicant, insurance companies, and utility and phone companies that are opening an account. Employers may look at a version of your report only if you give written permission, and the report they see does not include your credit score. Curious friends, neighbors, and most businesses with no financial dealings with you may not see it at all.

Figure 11.5 shows the four main parts of a typical report. The first part identifies you with your name, address history, birth date, and partial Social Security number. The second, and the longest, lists your accounts, often called trade lines, with the date opened, credit limit or loan amount, balance, and a month-by-month payment history. The third lists public records and collections, such as a bankruptcy or an account sent to a collection agency. The fourth lists inquiries, which show who has asked to see your report. A credit report does not list your income, your savings or checking balances, your race or religion, or your purchases. It describes how you have handled credit, not how much money you have.

Your Rights Under Federal Law

Because a credit report can decide whether you get a car, an apartment, or a job interview, Congress passed the Fair Credit Reporting Act in 1970. The law gives consumers several rights, and every young adult should know them.

The right to see your reports. The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) says that you can get your free credit reports only through one website, AnnualCreditReport.com. The three bureaus now offer free weekly reports to consumers, and by law you are entitled to at least one free report from each bureau every 12 months. Beware of look-alike sites that ask for a credit card or that sell a "free score." Checking your own report is a soft inquiry, and it does not lower your score.

The right to correct mistakes. Credit reports contain errors. A payment may be marked late when it was on time, an account may belong to a person with a similar name, or an account opened by an identity thief may appear on your file. If you find an error, the FTC advises that you dispute it with the credit bureau and also with the business, the furnisher, that reported the information. The bureau generally has 30 days to investigate and must give you the results in writing. Send your dispute in writing, keep copies, and include any documents that support your claim.

The right to know why you were turned down. If a business denies you credit, charges you more, or turns down an apartment application because of information in your credit report, it must tell you, name the bureau that supplied the report, and let you get a free copy.

The right to a limit on how long negative items stay. According to the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, most negative information, such as late payments, generally stays on your report for seven years. Bankruptcies can remain for up to ten years. These time limits do not apply to reports used for a job paying more than $75,000 a year or for credit or life insurance of more than $150,000. Positive information, such as accounts paid as agreed, can remain longer. And accurate negative information cannot simply be erased by paying a "credit repair" company; the best way to repair credit is to pay on time and wait.

Key Concepts

From Report to Score

Reading a full report takes time, so lenders often rely on a three-digit number that summarizes it. A credit score is a number, calculated by a formula from the information in your credit report, that estimates how likely you are to repay a loan on time. The best-known formula is the FICO Score, created by the Fair Isaac Corporation. FICO Scores range from 300 to 850, and a higher score means lower risk. Other scoring models exist, such as VantageScore, which the bureaus developed, so you may see different numbers from different sources. All of them use the same kind of information.

FICO says that its score is made up of five categories, shown in Figure 11.6. Payment history, about 35 percent, is the biggest factor. Lenders care most about whether you paid your past bills on time, and a single payment that is 30 or more days late can lower your score. Amounts owed, about 30 percent, looks at how much debt you carry compared with your credit limits. Length of credit history, about 15 percent, rewards accounts that have been open for a long time. New credit, about 10 percent, looks at how many accounts you recently opened or applied for. Credit mix, about 10 percent, considers whether you have managed different kinds of credit, such as a card and an installment loan.

Equally important is what the score does not include. FICO states that it does not use your race, color, religion, national origin, sex, or marital status. It does not use your age, where you live, or your salary, occupation, or employment history. It also does not use the interest rate on a particular card. A person who earns $200,000 a year and pays late has a lower score than a person who earns $20,000 and always pays on time. That is why a teenager with a small job can build a strong score.

ECONOMICS PACESETTER

Bill Fair and Earl IsaacTurning Credit Decisions Into Numbers

Before credit scores, a loan officer decided whether to lend by meeting an applicant, looking at the application, and relying on personal judgment. That system had two problems. It was slow, and it was unfair in ways that were hard to see, because two officers could make different decisions about the same person, and personal bias could shape the result.

In 1956, William Fair, an engineer, and Earl Isaac, a mathematician, started a small company in San Francisco with a bold idea: use statistics to predict which borrowers would repay. Instead of asking what an applicant looked like or whom the officer knew, their company studied large numbers of past loans to find which facts about a borrower, such as the number of late payments, actually predicted repayment. A lender could then score every applicant the same way. In 1989, their company, by then known as Fair Isaac, introduced the first FICO Score, which could be used with the reports of all three major bureaus.

The idea changed the credit market. Lenders could make decisions in minutes instead of days, and they could lend to many more people because they could measure risk more precisely. Many economists argue that this made credit available to more people, because the price of credit could be matched to the risk. Scoring has critics, too. It reflects past behavior, which means that a person with no credit history has a hard time getting started, and the formulas are not fully public. Congress has also required lenders to explain adverse decisions. The lesson for consumers is that a credit score is a tool that lenders use to price risk, and so understanding what drives it can save thousands of dollars.

Why a Score Matters: The Price of Risk

A higher score saves real money. Lenders charge higher interest rates to borrowers who seem riskier, so a lower score means a higher APR for the same loan. Figure 11.9 shows how big the difference can be in a hypothetical example. A borrower with an exceptional score might pay 5.5 percent on a $20,000, five-year car loan, a borrower with a good score 8 percent, a borrower with a fair score 12 percent, and a borrower with a poor score 17 percent. These rates are made up for teaching purposes, and real rates depend on the lender, the car, and the market. But the pattern is real. The borrower with a poor score would pay about $9,823 in interest over five years, compared with about $2,921 for the borrower with an exceptional score, a difference of more than $6,900 for exactly the same car.

Your score matters in other places too. Landlords may check your credit before they rent an apartment, and a low score may require a bigger security deposit or a cosigner. Phone and utility companies may ask for a deposit before starting service. Employers, with your written permission, may look at your report for jobs that involve handling money, and you will see in Chapter 12 that credit can also matter when you shop for insurance. In short, a credit score works like a reputation: it shapes how much trust, and how much money, others will give you.

Building Credit as a Young Adult

A young adult faces a puzzle. To get credit, you need a credit history, but to build a credit history, you need credit. The way out is to start small, borrow carefully, and prove to lenders that you pay. Here are the main tools, based on guidance from the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau and the FTC.

Become an authorized user. If a parent or relative with a good record adds you to his or her credit card account, the account's history may appear on your report. You get the benefit of their record, but you are also affected if they pay late or run up the balance, so choose a trustworthy person. Open a secured credit card. With a secured card, you put down a deposit, for example $200, and the issuer sets your credit limit at about that amount. The deposit protects the lender, so approval is easier. Use the card for one small purchase a month, pay the bill in full, and make sure that the issuer reports to the three bureaus. Try a credit-builder loan. Some credit unions and community lenders hold the loan amount in a savings account while you make the payments, and release it when you finish. You save money and build a record at the same time. Have rent and bills reported. Some services will add on-time rent or utility payments to your report, which can help if you have little other history.

The habits that matter most are simple. Pay every bill on time, because payment history is the biggest factor. Set up automatic payments for at least the minimum and then pay the full balance when you can. Keep your balances low. Your credit utilization is the share of your available credit that you are using. If your card limit is $1,000 and your balance is $300, your utilization is 300 ÷ 1,000 = 30 percent. Financial educators commonly advise keeping utilization well below 30 percent, and lower is generally better. Apply sparingly. Each time you apply for credit, the lender makes a hard inquiry on your report, which can lower your score a little and stays on the report for two years. Keep old accounts open if they have no fees, because they lengthen your history. And check your reports at least once a year for errors and for accounts you did not open.

Key Concepts

What Is Identity Theft?

Your name, birth date, and Social Security number work like a key. Together they can open doors that should be open only to you: a credit card, a loan, a phone account, a tax refund, or health care. Identity theft occurs when someone uses another person's personal information without permission to commit fraud or other crimes, usually to get money, credit, or services. The victim may not know until a bill arrives for something he or she never bought, or until a loan application is denied for no obvious reason.

Identity theft takes several forms. In new-account fraud, a thief opens a credit card, loan, or phone account in a victim's name and then never pays. In account takeover, a thief gains access to a victim's existing bank or card account and drains it or runs up charges. In tax identity theft, a thief files a false tax return using a stolen Social Security number to claim a refund. In medical identity theft, someone uses another person's insurance information to get care. In synthetic identity fraud, thieves combine a real Social Security number, often one belonging to a child, with a made-up name and birth date to create a new, fake person who can borrow money. And in child identity theft, a thief uses a minor's number, which may go unnoticed for years because children do not normally have credit records.

The economic damage is not only the stolen money. Victims lose time, often many hours, disputing accounts and fixing their credit records. A fraudulent account that is unpaid can lower a victim's credit score, which can raise the cost of a car loan or lead to a denied apartment application, as you saw in Section 2. Businesses and banks lose money, too, and they pass those costs on to customers through higher prices and fees. The cost of crime, therefore, is spread across the economy.

How big is the problem? The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) collects reports from consumers about fraud, identity theft, and other scams. In 2025, consumers reported losing a record $15.9 billion to fraud, based on about 3 million reports. That was up from more than $12.5 billion reported for 2024 and more than $10 billion for 2023. The actual totals are probably higher, because many victims never report. Figure 11.10 shows the three largest categories of loss in 2024. Investment scams cost consumers $5.7 billion. Imposter scams, in which a criminal pretends to be someone trusted, such as a government agency, a bank, or a relative, cost $2.95 billion. Job and business-opportunity scams cost about $750.6 million.

How Thieves Get Your Information

Criminals use many methods, some high-tech and some surprisingly simple. The most dangerous may be the data breach, in which hackers break into the computer system of a company, a hospital, a school, or a government office and steal records about thousands or millions of people at once. You cannot prevent a breach at a business that you have no control over, and you often have no choice about giving your information to it. That is one reason that you will examine the 2017 breach at Equifax in this chapter's Case Study.

Phishing is the use of fake emails, texts, or websites that look like they come from a bank, a delivery service, or a government agency, to trick people into revealing passwords, account numbers, or other personal information. When it is done by text message, it is sometimes called smishing, and by phone call, vishing. Phishing messages often create urgency: "Your account will be closed in 24 hours," or "You have an unpaid toll." Real companies rarely ask you to confirm a password or a full Social Security number through a link.

Other methods are low-tech. Thieves steal mail, looking for bank statements, pre-approved credit offers, and tax documents. They go through trash, a method sometimes called dumpster diving, to find unshredded papers. They watch a person type a PIN at a gas pump or store counter, and they place a hidden device on an ATM or gas pump, called a skimmer, to copy card data. They look at social media posts for details such as a birth date, a mother's maiden name, or the name of a first pet, which are often used as answers to security questions. They also use malware, or harmful software, that records keystrokes, and they take advantage of unsecured public Wi-Fi networks. Reused passwords make every method worse: if a thief learns the password you used on one site, he or she will try it on others.

A growing source of harm is the stolen data that is sold online. After a breach, the information may be passed through several criminals and used months or years later. For that reason, protection is not a one-time event. It is a set of habits, which you will study later in this section.

Key Concepts

Scams That Target Young People

Fraud is not limited to the elderly. The FTC's data show that scammers use many approaches, and some are designed especially for people your age, who spend time online, look for jobs, and often have little experience with money. Some of the most common are described here.

Fake job offers. A message says that you can earn $500 a week working from home, or that a company wants to hire you right away as a "payment processor." The scam usually has you deposit a check, send part of the money by gift card, wire, or an app, and keep the rest. Days later the bank finds that the check was fake and takes the money back from your account, and you are out the money you sent. Marketplace and payment-app scams. A buyer "accidentally" overpays and asks for a refund, a seller never ships the item, or a person asks you to send money to prove you are serious. Money sent through a payment app to a scammer is often impossible to get back. Imposter scams. Someone calls, texts, or emails pretending to be a bank, the Social Security Administration, a utility company, a school, or a relative in trouble. The FTC reported that government imposter scams alone cost $789 million in 2024. Investment and cryptocurrency scams. A stranger or an online "coach" promises large, guaranteed returns, often after building a friendship by text. These made up the largest category of loss in the FTC's data, at $5.7 billion in 2024 and $7.9 billion in 2025. Fake online stores. A website offers a popular item at an unbelievable price, takes the payment, and ships nothing.

The details change, but the warning signs stay the same. Be suspicious when someone pressures you to act right away, asks you to keep it secret, asks you to pay with a gift card, cryptocurrency, wire transfer, or payment app because these are hard to reverse, says that you have won a prize you never entered, or asks you to deposit a check and send back some of the money. A legitimate business or government agency does not demand payment in gift cards. The safest response is to stop, hang up or do not reply, and contact the real organization using a phone number or website that you find yourself.

Scammers also target older adults. The FTC reported that adults age 60 and over lost about $3 billion to fraud in 2025. Many young people can help by talking to grandparents and other relatives about these scams. A strong family habit is a code word that real relatives can use to prove who they are if someone calls claiming to be in trouble.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEAn Identity Card for the Digital Age: Estonia

Some countries have tried to solve the identity problem with technology. Estonia, a small country in northern Europe, issues a mandatory national identity card, and its government says that about 99 percent of residents have one. The card works as an official photo ID, as a health insurance card, and as a way to prove who you are online. It allows residents to sign documents digitally, file taxes, view medical records, vote online, and verify their identity for online banking.

The system works because a person must prove identity with something he or she has, the card, and something he or she knows, a PIN, which is the idea behind multi-factor authentication. The United States has no national identity card, and a Social Security number was not designed to be used as a password. In the United States, Social Security numbers are widely used to verify identity even though they are easily stolen. Each approach has costs and benefits, and societies disagree about how to balance convenience, privacy, and security.

Key Concepts

Protecting Your Identity

No one can guarantee that a thief will never get your information, but you can make it much harder and much easier to catch. Think about protection as four layers: lock your information, watch your accounts, verify before you share, and respond quickly if something goes wrong.

Lock. Use a different, long password for every important account, and consider a password manager to remember them. Turn on multi-factor authentication wherever it is offered. This requires a second proof of identity, such as a code sent to your phone or generated by an app, so a stolen password alone is not enough. Do not carry your Social Security card, and share your number only when it is truly required. Keep your phone and computer updated. Shred papers that contain account numbers. Collect mail promptly, and avoid public Wi-Fi when you are using a bank account.

Watch. Look at your bank and card statements every month, or better yet, every week. Set up alerts for transactions over a certain amount. Check your credit reports, which are free at AnnualCreditReport.com, and look for accounts that you did not open. The sooner you spot a problem, the less it costs you. Federal law caps your liability for unauthorized credit card charges at $50 if you report them after they occur, and at $0 if you report the loss before anyone uses the card. Debit cards have weaker protection. According to the FTC, your liability for an unauthorized debit or ATM card transaction is $50 if you report within two business days of learning about it, up to $500 if you report later but within 60 days of your statement, and possibly all the money taken after that. A debit card draws directly from your checking account, so the cost of delay is higher.

Verify. Before you click, call, or send anything, ask who is contacting you and whether you can confirm it independently. Go to a company's official website by typing the address yourself, not by clicking on a link in a message. Hang up on unexpected callers who ask for personal information, and call the organization back at a number from your card or statement. Be careful about what you post on social media.

Key Concepts

Credit Freezes and Fraud Alerts

Two tools can protect your credit file directly. A credit freeze, also called a security freeze, blocks anyone from opening new credit in your name by locking your report at a bureau. A lender cannot see the file, so it will not grant new credit, until you lift the freeze. According to the FTC, a freeze is free, lasts until you lift it, and must be placed separately with each of the three bureaus. When you want to apply for a loan, an apartment, or a card, you can lift the freeze temporarily with the bureau the lender will use, and you can place it again afterward.

A fraud alert is a notice on your credit report that tells businesses to take extra steps to verify your identity before they open new credit. It does not block access to the file. An initial fraud alert is free and lasts one year, and anyone who is or suspects that he or she may be a victim may place one by contacting one bureau, which must tell the other two. An extended fraud alert lasts seven years and requires an identity theft report from the FTC or the police. Parents and guardians can also freeze the credit file of a child under age 16, free of charge, which is a good way to prevent thieves from using a child's clean record.

Many consumer advocates recommend that people who are not applying for credit right now keep their files frozen, because it is the strongest protection against new-account fraud. Freezing has costs of its own, mostly inconvenience and time. If you forget that your file is frozen and apply for a store card or an apartment, you may be turned down until you lift it. For a young adult who rarely borrows, that cost is small compared with the cost of repairing an identity theft.

Key Concepts

If You Become a Victim

Even careful people become victims. The key to limiting the damage is to act quickly and in the right order. The FTC runs a website, IdentityTheft.gov, where victims can report the crime and get a personal recovery plan with steps to follow. The main steps are as follows.

Step 1: Contact the businesses involved. Call the fraud department of any bank, card company, or store where the thief opened or used an account. Ask them to close or freeze the account and to remove the charges. Follow up in writing. Step 2: Place a fraud alert or freeze. Contact the credit bureaus to place an alert, a freeze, or both. Step 3: Report to the FTC. File a report at IdentityTheft.gov. The FTC will help you build a recovery plan and give you an identity theft report, which you can use to dispute accounts and obtain an extended fraud alert. Step 4: Review your credit reports. Get your free reports and look for accounts that you did not open. Dispute any errors with both the bureau and the furnisher, as you learned in Section 2. Step 5: Consider other steps. If the theft involves taxes, contact the Internal Revenue Service. If it involves a driver's license, contact the Department of Motor Vehicles. If a thief used your information for a crime, file a police report.

Keep careful records of every call: the date, the name of the person you spoke to, and what was said. Recovery can take weeks or months. Victims of the 2017 Equifax breach, whom you will meet in the Case Study, were offered free identity restoration help through January 2029, because the effects of a breach can last for years.

Several agencies work to protect consumers. The FTC takes fraud reports and enforces laws against unfair and deceptive practices. The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) accepts complaints about banks, lenders, and credit reporting companies. In California, the Department of Financial Protection and Innovation (DFPI) regulates many lenders and money services, and the California Attorney General enforces consumer protection laws. Reporting a scam or a problem lender helps protect not only you but also your neighbors.

Putting It Together

Credit is built on trust, and identity theft attacks that trust. In this chapter you studied three connected ideas. First, credit is a price you pay to use someone else's money, and you can compare its cost by looking at the APR and the total repaid. Second, a credit report and a score are the record that lenders use to judge you, and a good record lowers what you pay. Third, that record needs protection. By locking your information, watching your accounts, verifying before you share, and responding fast, you make it much harder for a thief to borrow in your name. In Chapter 12, you will use these ideas to make wise choices about big purchases, such as a vehicle, a home, insurance, and education.

CASE STUDY

147 Million Files:
The Equifax Breach and Your Credit

In September 2017, Equifax, one of the three nationwide credit bureaus, announced that attackers had stolen the personal information of about 147 million people. Most of them had never chosen Equifax as a company to do business with. The breach raised a hard question: when a company that collects data about you is careless with it, who pays, and what can you do?

Background

The Company You Did Not Choose

In Section 2 you learned that credit bureaus collect information from lenders and sell reports to businesses that have a legal reason to see them. A consumer is not a customer of a credit bureau in the usual sense. You do not sign up, you do not pay, and you cannot easily leave. If you have ever had a credit card, a loan, or a phone account, your information is probably in the files of one or more bureaus. That arrangement creates what economists call an externality problem: the company that holds the data does not bear the whole cost if the data are stolen, because the harm falls on the people whose data they are.

According to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), Equifax learned in March 2017 of a serious security flaw in one of its databases, and the FTC later alleged that the company failed to make sure that the repair had been applied. In July 2017 its security team found suspicious network traffic and the unrepaired database, and the attackers had been in its systems for months. The company announced the breach in September 2017. The stolen information included the names and birth dates of at least 147 million people, 145.5 million Social Security numbers, and 209,000 payment card numbers and expiration dates, along with addresses and other personal information.

This case study asks you to look at the evidence from several angles: the facts of the breach and its settlement, the wider growth in fraud losses, the choices that an ordinary consumer faces after a breach, and three different views about how to respond. As you read, notice which statements are facts that can be checked and which are judgments about what people or companies should do.

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Types of Credit
Credit is the use of someone else's money now, with a promise to repay it later; interest is the price. Installment loans have fixed payments, revolving credit (credit cards) has a limit that can be reused, and open credit is a bill paid in full. The APR lets you compare costs. Paying only the minimum on a card is very costly, and payday loans can carry APRs near or above 400 percent. Borrowers under 21 must show their own ability to pay or have a cosigner.
Section 2 · Credit Reports and Scores
A credit report records your borrowing history; the three nationwide bureaus are Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. You have rights to free reports, to dispute errors, and to know why you were denied. A FICO Score (300 to 850) is based on payment history (35%), amounts owed (30%), length of history (15%), new credit (10%), and credit mix (10%). A better score means lower interest costs. Build credit by paying on time and keeping balances low.
Section 3 · Identity Theft and Fraud
Identity theft is the use of another person's information to commit fraud. Thieves use data breaches, phishing, mail theft, and scams. Protect yourself with four layers: lock, watch, verify, respond. A credit freeze blocks new credit until you lift it; a fraud alert requires extra identity checks. Report problems at IdentityTheft.gov. Liability for unauthorized credit card charges is limited to $50.
Case Study · 147 Million Files
The 2017 Equifax breach exposed the personal information of about 147 million people, including 145.5 million Social Security numbers. The settlement offered at least $575 million in total, free identity restoration help, and free credit reports. The case shows that your credit can be at risk from a company you never chose, so protecting it requires habits as well as laws.
