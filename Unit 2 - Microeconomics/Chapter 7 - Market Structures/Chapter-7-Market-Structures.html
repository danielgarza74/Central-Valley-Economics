Concept Review

In Chapter 6 you saw how supply and demand determine a price. That story assumed that many sellers compete. In the real world, some markets have thousands of sellers, some have a handful, and some have only one. In this chapter you will learn how economists classify markets by their degree of competition, how the number of sellers changes prices and output, and how government tries to keep markets competitive.

Chapter 7 Key Concept

A market structure is a way of classifying a market by the number of sellers, the kind of product, and how easy it is for new sellers to enter. The four main structures are perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly. The more sellers compete, the more pressure on prices and the better the outcome for buyers.

Why the Concept Matters

Think about buying lunch near school. If five taquerías and two pizza places sit within a few blocks, each has to keep its prices reasonable and its food good, or you will walk down the street. If only one place sold food and the next one was a 20-minute drive away, that place could charge more and put less effort into quality. Competition protects buyers. By the end of this chapter you will be able to describe each market structure, explain how a monopoly differs from a competitive seller in price and output, and judge when government should step in.

Key Concepts

Classifying Markets by Competition

In Chapter 6, buyers and sellers met at an equilibrium price. That model quietly assumed that no single buyer or seller could move the price. In real life this is true in some markets and not in others. A farmer selling lettuce at harvest time can do almost nothing to change its price. A company that is the only seller of electricity in a town can change its price quite a bit. Economists describe these differences with the idea of a market structure: a model that classifies a market according to how much competition exists in it.

Three features define a market structure: the number of sellers, the kind of product (identical or different), and the ease of entry (how hard it is for new sellers to join). The four structures that economists use are perfect competition, monopolistic competition, oligopoly, and monopoly. They run from the most competition to the least. Real markets rarely fit a model exactly, but the models help you predict how sellers behave and how buyers fare. Standard 12.2.7 asks you to analyze how competition affects the quality, quantity, and price of goods and services.

Key Concepts

Market Power and the Spectrum of Competition

Behind every market structure is one idea: market power. Market power is the ability of a seller to influence the price of what it sells. A seller with no market power, like the lettuce grower you will meet below, must accept whatever price the market gives it. A seller with a great deal of market power, like the only electric company in a town, can choose its price within the limits of what buyers are willing to pay. Most real businesses fall somewhere between these two extremes, and the four market structures are best thought of as reference points along a dial rather than four sealed boxes.

How much market power a firm has depends on three questions. The first is how many sellers there are. When dozens of sellers compete for the same customers, none of them can raise its price much. The second is how close the substitutes are. A substitute is a good that a buyer can use in place of another. If the taquería on one corner raises the price of a burrito, you can walk to the one across the street, so substitutes are close and the owner has little power. If the only pharmacy within thirty miles raises the price of a prescription, there is no close substitute and the buyer has less choice. The third is how easy it is to enter. If a profitable business can be copied quickly and cheaply, new rivals will arrive and take away the power. If starting up takes a billion dollars or a government license, the power can last.

Economists also have to decide what counts as the market. A market is defined by a product and by a place. Is the market for “food,” or for “tacos,” or for “tacos within a ten-minute drive of Shafter High School”? The answer matters. A carrot grower sells to buyers across the country, so the relevant market is national or even global. A barber sells haircuts to people within a few miles, so the relevant market is local. When regulators review a merger in the Case Study at the end of this chapter, one of the first questions they ask is how to draw the boundaries of the market, because a company can look small in a national market and very large in a single city.

Key Concepts

The Features of Perfect Competition

Perfect competition is a market structure with five features. First, there are many buyers and many sellers, so no single one is large enough to affect the price. Second, sellers offer a standardized product, one that is identical from every seller, so buyers have no reason to prefer one over another. Third, buyers and sellers have good information about prices and quality. Fourth, there is free entry and exit: new sellers can easily enter the market and unprofitable sellers can easily leave. Fifth, each seller is a price taker: a seller who must accept the market price because it is too small to change it.

Think about a grower who sells raw lettuce at harvest time. Thousands of growers sell nearly identical lettuce, and buyers can easily compare prices. If one grower asks for $20 a box when the market price is $14, buyers simply buy from someone else. If the grower sells at $14, he can sell all he can grow. This grower has no reason to charge less than the market price and cannot charge more. The market determines the price, and the grower decides only how much to produce.

True perfect competition is rare, because few products are exactly identical and information is never perfect. Economists use it as a benchmark. Some agricultural markets, such as those for wheat, corn, or raw milk sold to a processor, come close, because the product is standardized and there are many sellers. Kern County's growers sell much of their output into markets of this kind, where they are price takers.

Key Concepts

Price Takers in the Central Valley

Kern County is one of the most productive farming counties in the nation, and its crop report shows how much of its economy depends on markets that look like perfect competition. The county's 2024 crop report, released in October 2025, put the gross value of Kern County agriculture at $7.96 billion, down 8 percent from the record $8.63 billion in 2023. Citrus ($1.31 billion), grapes ($1.18 billion), carrots ($1.01 billion), and pistachios ($723 million) were among the leading crops, and almonds came in at nearly $800 million. Gross value means the value of everything sold before the grower subtracts the costs of land, water, labor, and equipment, so it is not the same as profit.

Why do economists treat many of these growers as price takers? A grower of navel oranges or carrots sells a product that is much like the product of thousands of other growers. Buyers care about the grade, size, and freshness, not whose farm it came from. One grower's harvest is a tiny part of the national supply, so deciding to sell a few more truckloads will not move the price. When a freeze or a drought cuts the crop in several regions at once, the price rises for everyone. When every region has a bumper crop, the price falls for everyone. Each grower simply decides how much to plant and when to harvest.

The picture is not perfectly competitive all the way down the food chain. Some of the companies that pack, ship, and market fruit and vegetables are large and have brand names, and the grocery chains that buy from them are large too. Economists therefore say that growers often sit in a competitive market while the businesses that buy from them may have more market power. The tension between these levels is one reason antitrust officials study food markets, as you will see in Section 3 and in the Case Study.

Key Concepts

How a Price Taker Decides

A price taker cannot choose the price, but it can choose the quantity. The question is how much to produce. In Chapter 5, you learned the rule: produce each unit as long as the price is at least as high as the marginal cost of making it. For a price taker, the price is also the revenue from selling one more unit, so the rule is simple: produce until the marginal cost of the next unit would be higher than the market price.

Figure 7.4 shows a hypothetical lettuce grower with a price of $14 per box. The grower's marginal cost rises from $6 for the first 10 boxes to $22 for the box beyond 40. The grower should supply every box whose marginal cost is below $14, the first 30 boxes, because each adds more to revenue than to cost. The next box would cost $16 to produce and bring only $14, so it would lower profit. The best output is 30 boxes. If the market price rose to $18, the grower would raise output to about 40 boxes, which is why the marginal cost curve is the firm's supply curve.

Free entry has an important effect over time. If growers in the market earn large profits, new growers enter, supply increases, and the market price falls, cutting profits back. If growers suffer losses, some leave, supply decreases, and the price rises. In the long run, competition pushes profits toward the level just high enough to keep the growers in business. For buyers, this is a good outcome: they pay a price close to the cost of production.

Key Concepts

Revenue, Cost, and Profit for a Price Taker

To see how a price taker finds its best output, you need to follow the money. A firm's total revenue is the price times the quantity sold. If a lettuce grower sells 30 boxes at $14 a box, total revenue is 30 × $14 = $420. The extra revenue from selling one more unit is called marginal revenue. For a price taker, marginal revenue is simply the market price, because every additional box sells for the same $14. This is why economists draw a price taker's demand curve as a flat, horizontal line at the market price.

Costs come in two kinds. Fixed costs do not change when the firm produces more or less in the short run. A grower pays the same land payment, equipment loan, and insurance bill whether the field yields 10 boxes or 50. Variable costs rise as output rises: seed, fertilizer, water, fuel, and the wages of the workers who pick and pack. Adding fixed and variable costs gives total cost. Dividing total cost by the number of units gives average total cost, the cost per unit. You first met marginal cost in Chapter 5: it is the extra cost of producing one more unit.

Profit is total revenue minus total cost. Suppose the grower's fixed cost is $90 a day, and the cost of each box rises as more boxes are picked, from $6 for each of the first ten boxes to $22 for each box beyond the 40th. The schedule in Figure 7.5 shows what happens. At 20 boxes, revenue is $280 and total cost is $240, so profit is $40. At 30 boxes, revenue is $420, total cost is $360, and profit is $60, the most the grower can earn. At 40 boxes, revenue is $560 but total cost is $520, so profit falls to $40. The grower earns the most by stopping at the quantity where marginal cost is still below the price but the next unit would not be. This is the same rule you learned earlier, now confirmed with real arithmetic: profit is greatest where price equals marginal cost.

Notice that no part of this rule needs the grower to know what rivals are doing. A price taker looks at one number from the market, the price, and one number from its own books, its marginal cost. That simplicity is part of what makes perfect competition such a useful starting point for economic thinking.

Key Concepts

Profit, Loss, and the Shutdown Decision

Figure 7.5 draws the grower's costs on a graph. Besides the price line and the marginal cost curve, it shows the average total cost curve. Average total cost falls at first, because the fixed cost is spread over more boxes, and then rises once picking costs climb. At the best output of 30 boxes, the price is $14 and the average total cost is $12. The grower earns $2 of profit on each box, and $2 × 30 boxes = $60 for the day. That profit appears on the graph as a shaded rectangle: its height is the gap between price and average total cost, and its width is the quantity.

Prices do not always stay at $14. Suppose a bumper crop across the state pushes the price down to $10. The rule still says to produce until marginal cost reaches the price, which gives 20 boxes (the marginal cost of those boxes is $9, but the next block of boxes costs $12). Revenue is 20 × $10 = $200 and total cost is $240, so the grower loses $40 a day. Should the grower close down? Not in the short run. If the grower produced nothing, the $90 fixed cost would still have to be paid, and the loss would be $90. By producing 20 boxes, the grower loses only $40, because revenue of $200 more than covers the $150 of variable cost and contributes $50 toward the fixed cost.

Now suppose the price drops to $5. Every box costs at least $6 to pick and pack, so each box sold would add to the loss. Revenue would not even cover variable costs. In that case, the grower is better off producing nothing and losing only the $90 fixed cost. This gives the shutdown rule: in the short run, a firm should keep producing as long as the price covers its average variable cost, and should shut down when the price falls below it. In the Central Valley, you can see the shutdown rule in action when growers leave fruit unharvested because the price will not even pay the pickers. In the long run, a grower must cover all costs, including fixed costs, or leave the business.

Key Concepts

The Long Run: Entry, Exit, and Efficiency

The grower's $60 profit is good news, but it will not last. In the long run, firms can enter and leave, and a competitive market with profits invites newcomers. Other growers see that lettuce earns more than other crops they could plant, and they plant lettuce. Supply shifts to the right, and, as you learned in Chapter 6, a rightward shift lowers the equilibrium price. As the price falls from $14 toward $12, each grower's profit shrinks. When the price reaches $12, the lowest point on the average total cost curve, profit is $0 and the entry stops. If the price fell below that level, some growers would take losses and leave, supply would shift left, and the price would rise again. The long-run price settles where growers just cover their costs.

It may sound strange that firms in a healthy competitive market earn zero profit. The reason is that economists count the opportunity cost of the owner's own money and time as a cost. A grower who could earn $50,000 working for someone else counts that salary as part of the cost of farming. Economic profit is revenue minus all costs, including those opportunity costs. When economic profit is zero, the grower is earning a normal return: just enough to stay in the business, but no more. The owner is not poor; the owner is paid exactly what the resources are worth in their best alternative use.

The long-run result also has a happy side for buyers. Economists describe it using two kinds of efficiency. Productive efficiency means producing at the lowest possible average cost per unit; in the long run, competition forces growers to do this, because any grower with higher costs loses money and leaves. Allocative efficiency means producing the quantity at which the price equals marginal cost. The price tells you what the last unit is worth to buyers, and the marginal cost tells you what it takes to make the last unit. When the two are equal, society is producing the right amount of the good: the last box is worth just as much to buyers as the other things its resources could have produced.

Real markets do not meet every condition of the model. Farmers receive crop insurance and subsidies, water is limited and priced unevenly, and growing some crops, such as trees, means a long wait before supply can respond. But the model still helps you predict. When almond and pistachio prices have been high, farmers have planted more trees, and when prices fall, planting slows. The model points to a pattern that appears again and again in the Central Valley: high prices attract entry, and entry brings prices down.

Key Concepts

One Seller

At the other end of the spectrum from perfect competition is a monopoly: a market structure in which only one seller supplies a good or service that has no close substitutes. A monopolist faces the entire market demand, so it is not a price taker. It is a price maker: a seller with the power to set its price. Of course, a monopolist cannot sell any amount at any price. The law of demand still applies, so a higher price means fewer sales.

A monopoly lasts only if something keeps new sellers out. Economists call this a barrier to entry: anything that prevents new firms from entering a market. Barriers include very high startup costs, control over a key resource, legal protection, or a technology that others cannot copy. Without barriers, profits would attract new sellers, as in Section 1, and the monopoly would disappear.

Monopolies take several forms. A natural monopoly arises when one firm can supply the whole market at a lower cost than two or more firms, because the costs of building the system are so large. A local electric or water utility is the classic example. It is far cheaper to have one set of power lines in a neighborhood than three competing sets. A geographic monopoly arises because of location: the only gas station for fifty miles on a desert highway. A technological monopoly arises when a firm holds a patent, which gives its inventor the exclusive right to sell a new product for a number of years. A government monopoly exists when the law gives one organization the exclusive right to provide a service, such as a city's license to one company for trash collection. A related arrangement is a cartel, an agreement among separate sellers to act as one by fixing prices or output. Cartels are illegal in the United States.

Key Concepts

Barriers to Entry: What Keeps Rivals Out

A monopoly cannot last unless something keeps competitors from entering the market. Economists group the most important barriers into several families. The first is economies of scale. When a firm's average cost per unit falls as it grows larger, one big firm can charge less than several small ones. A company that builds a power plant and a network of power lines spreads that enormous cost over millions of customers. A newcomer starting small would have much higher costs per customer, so it cannot match the price. The natural monopoly you read about earlier is the extreme case of this barrier.

The second family is control of a key resource. If a firm owns the only supply of an essential input, rivals cannot make the same product. In the Central Valley, access to irrigation water works a little like this: a would-be farmer who cannot get water cannot compete with growers who already hold water rights, no matter how good the soil is. The third family is legal barriers. A patent gives an inventor the exclusive right to sell an invention for 20 years from the date of filing. Copyrights protect books, music, and software. Licenses are required for doctors, electricians, and barbers. Many licenses protect the public from unsafe work, but economists point out that they can also limit the number of sellers and keep prices higher than they would be otherwise.

A fourth barrier is network effects: some products become more valuable when more people use them. A social media site with a billion users is more useful to a new member than a rival with a thousand, so rivals struggle to catch up even when their features are better. The fifth is aggressive action by firms already in the market. A large company may cut its price below cost for a time to drive out a new rival, planning to raise the price again once the rival is gone. This tactic, called predatory pricing, can be illegal under the antitrust laws in Section 4 when it is used to build or protect a monopoly. Regulators must be careful, though, because ordinary price cutting is a sign of healthy competition.

Key Concepts

Marginal Revenue: The Price of Selling More

Why does a monopolist produce less than a competitive market? The answer lies in the behavior of marginal revenue. A price taker sells every unit at the same price, so selling one more unit adds exactly the price to its revenue. A monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve, and that changes the arithmetic. To sell more, the monopolist must lower the price, and the lower price applies to every unit it sells, not just the new one.

Take the hypothetical market in Figure 7.7. At a price of $18, buyers take 10 units a week, and total revenue is $180. To sell 20 units, the price must fall to $16, and total revenue rises to $320. The 10 extra units brought in only $320 − $180 = $140, which is $14 per extra unit, well below the $16 price. The reason is that the seller gave up $2 on each of the first 10 units in order to sell the second 10. At 30 units the price is $14 and revenue is $420, so the third block of 10 units added $100, or $10 per unit. At 40 units, revenue is $480, and the fourth block added only $60, or $6 per unit. At 50 units, revenue is $500, and the fifth block added just $20, or $2 per unit. Past 50 units, the price falls so much that total revenue actually declines.

A profit-maximizing firm keeps producing as long as the revenue from the next unit is at least as great as the cost. In this market the marginal cost is a constant $8 a unit. The block of units from 21 to 30 brings $10 per unit in revenue, more than the $8 it costs. The block from 31 to 40 brings only $6 per unit, less than $8. So the monopolist stops at 30 units and charges the highest price that buyers will pay for 30 units, which is $14. A price taker, by contrast, would keep expanding until the price fell to $8, because its marginal revenue never falls below the price.

Key Concepts

What a Monopolist Does

How does a monopolist's behavior differ from that of a price taker? A price taker faces a flat demand curve and sells as much as it wants at the market price. A monopolist faces a downward-sloping demand curve. To sell more, it has to lower its price on all the units it sells, so the extra revenue from selling one more unit, called marginal revenue, is less than the price. A monopolist that maximizes profit follows the marginal rule of Chapter 5: produce up to the point at which the extra revenue from the last unit equals its marginal cost.

Figure 7.7 shows a hypothetical market where marginal cost is a constant $8 per unit. The monopolist produces 30 units, where marginal revenue equals $8, and charges $14, the highest price at which buyers will take 30 units. A competitive market with the same costs would produce where the price equals marginal cost: 60 units at $8. Compared with competition, a monopoly produces less and charges more. The monopolist earns a profit of ($14 − $8) × 30 = $180 per week, while competitive sellers would earn only a normal return.

Economists also worry that a monopolist, free from competitors, has little reason to improve its service or cut costs. Defenders of monopolies reply that some of them, such as utilities, achieve lower costs for the whole market, and that the promise of a temporary monopoly through a patent encourages inventors to create new products in the first place. A balance among these considerations is what shapes public policy.

Key Concepts

The Cost of Monopoly: Deadweight Loss

When a monopolist charges more and sells less, who loses and who gains? To answer, economists use two ideas. Consumer surplus is the difference between what buyers are willing to pay and what they actually pay. If you would have paid $20 for a ticket but the price is $14, your surplus is $6. On a graph, consumer surplus is the area below the demand curve and above the price. Producer profit is the area between the price and the cost.

In the competitive version of the market in Figure 7.8, buyers purchase 60 units at $8. Consumer surplus is a triangle with a base of 60 and a height of $12 ($20 − $8), so it equals ½ × 60 × $12 = $360. Under monopoly, only 30 units sell, at $14. Consumer surplus shrinks to a triangle with a base of 30 and a height of $6, or ½ × 30 × $6 = $90. The monopolist earns a profit of ($14 − $8) × 30 = $180. Buyers lost $270 of surplus ($360 − $90), and the monopolist gained $180. A transfer from buyers to the monopolist does not change the total value created, although you may feel that it is unfair.

The remaining $90 simply disappears. It is the deadweight loss: the value of the trades that would have benefited both buyers and the seller but do not happen because the monopolist restricts output. Between 30 and 60 units, buyers are willing to pay more than the $8 it costs to make each unit, but the monopolist will not sell them at that price, because doing so would force it to lower the price on its other units. The area of this lost value is the triangle with a base of 30 and a height of $6: ½ × 30 × $6 = $90. This is the economic argument against monopoly. It is not only that prices are higher; it is that society ends up producing too little of the good.

Monopolies also give firms a reason to spend money to protect their position, such as lobbying for rules that keep out rivals. Economists call this spending rent seeking. It uses resources that could have made goods people want. This is one more reason why economists favor competition when it is practical, and regulation when competition is not.

Key Concepts

Price Discrimination: Different Prices for Different Buyers

Sellers with market power sometimes charge different buyers different prices for the same product. This practice is called price discrimination: charging different prices to different customers for the same good or service when the differences are not based on differences in cost. You see it everywhere. A theater charges less for a matinee, a restaurant offers a senior discount, a store prints coupons, and an airline sells seats on the same flight at many different fares.

Price discrimination works only if three conditions hold. The seller must have some market power, because a price taker that charged more than the market price would lose its customers. The seller must be able to separate buyers into groups with different willingness to pay, for example by age, by time of purchase, or by whether the buyer has a coupon. And buyers who pay the low price must not be able to resell to buyers who would have paid the high price. A haircut or a flight is hard to resell, which is why services are often priced this way.

Consider a hypothetical movie theater in Bakersfield with 100 seats, a cost of $2 per seat, and two groups of buyers. Working adults will pay $12, but students will pay only $8. If the theater must charge one price, it could charge $12 and sell 40 adult seats (profit of $10 each, or $400), or charge $8 and sell 40 adult and 40 student seats (profit of $6 each on 80 seats, or $480). Charging each group its own price earns more: 40 adults × $10 + 40 students × $6 = $400 + $240 = $640. In this case, the students who would have been shut out by the $12 price get to attend. Price discrimination can therefore raise the quantity sold, which reduces deadweight loss, even as it raises the seller's profit.

Most price discrimination is legal. But it is not legal to charge different prices because of race, religion, or other protected characteristics, and the Robinson-Patman Act of 1936 bars some forms of price discrimination among competing business buyers. Whether a particular discount helps or harms buyers depends on the facts: students and seniors often benefit, but buyers with fewer alternatives, such as travelers who must fly on a certain day, may pay more.

Key Concepts

Patents, Generic Competition, and the Price of Medicine

Few products show the effect of competition as clearly as prescription drugs. A company that invents a new medicine receives a patent and can sell the drug without competition for years. During that time it is a monopolist, and it can choose a high price, which helps it recover the large cost of research that may have failed many times before. When the patent and other protections expire, other companies may make a generic version, which has the same active ingredient as the brand-name drug.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration studied what happened to prices when generic makers entered. In an analysis of drugs whose first generic version reached the market between 2015 and 2017, the agency found that the median price of generic drugs was 39 percent lower than the brand price when there was only one generic maker, 54 percent lower when there were two, and 79 percent lower when there were four. With six or more makers, the price was more than 95 percent below the brand price. In other words, every additional competitor pushed the price closer to the cost of production, which is exactly what the model of competition predicts.

The numbers show the whole spectrum of market structures in one product: a monopoly while the patent lasts, something like an oligopoly when two or three firms sell, and something close to perfect competition when many firms sell an identical product. They also show why the patent trade-off is difficult. Shorter patents would lower prices sooner but could reduce the reward for inventing. Longer patents would reward invention but keep prices high for patients. A good policy has to weigh both. People in the Central Valley, where many families have limited incomes, feel that trade-off in their pharmacy bills.

How Regulators Set the Rates

Regulating a natural monopoly poses a puzzle. The ideal price, from an efficiency point of view, is the marginal cost: the price at which buyers get exactly the right amount. But a utility has enormous fixed costs for power plants, pipes, and wires, and its marginal cost of serving one more customer is small. A price equal to marginal cost would not cover the fixed costs, and the company would lose money. If regulators let the company charge as much as it wants, the monopoly's high price would cause a deadweight loss. Regulators therefore look for a price in the middle.

The traditional method is rate-of-return regulation. The regulator estimates the utility's costs of operating and the value of the equipment it has invested in, then sets rates that bring in enough revenue to cover those costs plus a fair return on the investment. In California, the Public Utilities Commission follows a process of this kind. Cal Water, the private company that provides water service in parts of Bakersfield, must ask the commission to approve its rates in a formal rate case, and the commission reviews the company's costs and plans before it decides. Pacific Gas and Electric is the main electric and gas utility for much of the Central Valley, and Southern California Edison serves other parts of Kern County, and each is also regulated by the commission.

A second method is a price cap, which limits how much the price can rise each year, usually by a formula, and lets the company keep any extra profit it earns by cutting costs. This gives the company a stronger reason to be efficient, but it also risks lowering the quality of service if the company cuts too much. Both methods have a deeper problem: regulators know less about the company's true costs than the company does. A utility that is guaranteed a return on its investments may have a reason to build more than it needs, and a regulator who is too harsh may discourage the investment that keeps the lights on. This is why economists describe regulation as a difficult balancing act and not a cure-all.

Key Concepts

Many Sellers, Different Products

Most of the businesses you meet each day are neither perfect competitors nor monopolists. A Shafter taquería has many rivals, but its tacos are not identical to anyone else's. This is the situation of monopolistic competition: a market structure with many sellers, easy entry, and products that are similar but not identical. Restaurants, hair salons, clothing stores, and gyms in Bakersfield are examples.

The key idea is product differentiation: the ways in which sellers make their products different from those of competitors, through quality, features, location, or style. Differentiation gives each seller a small amount of power over its price. If the taquería down the street has the best salsa in town, it can charge slightly more without losing all its customers. But the power is limited, because customers can switch to other taquerías.

Because price cuts are easy for rivals to match, sellers in these markets also use nonprice competition: competition based on factors other than price, such as advertising, brand names, customer service, packaging, or convenience. Advertising informs buyers, but it also persuades them. Economists note that product variety is a benefit of monopolistic competition, since buyers can choose what they like, but that the costs of advertising and differentiation are part of what buyers pay. Free entry limits profits: when a new hot spot opens and earns well, others copy it, and its extra profit shrinks.

Profit in Monopolistic Competition: Short Run and Long Run

A firm in monopolistic competition behaves much like a small monopolist in the short run. Because its product is different from its rivals', it faces a downward-sloping demand curve. It therefore follows the same rule as any profit-maximizing firm: produce up to the point where marginal revenue equals marginal cost, then charge the highest price that buyers will pay for that quantity. The left panel of Figure 7.11 shows a hypothetical firm of this kind. Marginal revenue equals marginal cost at 32 units, and demand says that buyers will pay $8.80 for 32 units. Average total cost at that output is about $7.93, so the firm earns about $0.87 of profit on each unit and about $28 in total.

That profit is a signal. In monopolistic competition there are few barriers to entry, so other entrepreneurs notice the profit and open similar businesses. Think about what happens when a new taquería in Bakersfield does well: within months, someone opens another one nearby, and perhaps a food truck parks down the street. Each new rival takes some of the first firm's customers, so its demand curve shifts to the left. Entry continues as long as profit exists. In the long run, demand shifts until it just touches the average total cost curve, as in the right panel. There, the price equals average total cost, at about $8.24, and the firm earns zero economic profit. The numbers in this figure were chosen to make the arithmetic easy, not to match a real business.

The long-run result has two features that differ from perfect competition. First, the price ($8.24) is still higher than the marginal cost (about $5.41), so the market is not allocatively efficient: some buyers who value another unit above its cost do not get it. Second, the firm produces about 28 units, less than the 63 units at which its average cost would be lowest (about $7.16). Economists call this excess capacity: the firm could produce more cheaply per unit but does not, because it would have to cut its price to sell more. Many people accept this cost because they get something in return, which is variety. A town with only identical lunch counters would be cheaper to run, but fewer people would want to live there.

Key Concepts

Brands, Advertising, and Variety

Why do restaurants, clothing stores, and sneaker makers spend so much effort on brand names and advertising? In monopolistic competition, a firm that cannot win customers with a lower price must win them by being different, or by convincing buyers that it is different. A brand name is a promise: when you see a familiar logo, you expect a certain level of quality. Brands reduce the time buyers spend searching and help them avoid bad purchases. A shopper who has had a good meal at one taquería in Shafter will likely return to it.

Advertising has two faces. On one side, it provides information: it tells buyers a business exists, what it sells, and what it charges. Markets with advertising tend to have lower prices for some goods than markets where advertising is banned, because buyers can compare. On the other side, advertising also persuades, and some of the persuasion tries to make one product seem different from another when the real difference is small. Spending on advertising is a cost, and in the long run, buyers pay for it in the price. Economists disagree about the balance between these effects, and the answer depends on the product.

Product differentiation also creates a form of competition that is hard to measure: competition in quality, convenience, and variety. A shop that stays open late, a salon with a friendly staff, or a gym with newer equipment attracts customers without lowering its price. For buyers, this form of competition can matter more than price. For economists, it is a reminder that a market can be competitive even when prices are not identical, and that the number of sellers alone does not tell you how well a market serves buyers.

Key Concepts

A Few Big Sellers

An oligopoly is a market structure in which a few large firms dominate the market. The word comes from Greek words meaning “few sellers.” Barriers to entry, such as enormous startup costs, keep new firms out. The best-known example is the market for wireless phone service in the United States, in which three national carriers serve most customers. Until 2020 there were four. That year, T-Mobile completed its purchase of Sprint, and the number of national carriers fell from four to three. Other oligopolies include commercial airlines, soft drink makers, and meatpackers.

Firms in an oligopoly depend on one another. Because there are few firms, each one's decisions about price and output affect the others. If one airline lowers its fares, rivals will probably match the cut, so a price cut brings little gain and may start a price war that lowers everyone's profit. If one raises its fares, the others may not follow, and the first loses customers. For this reason oligopolists often avoid price competition and compete with advertising, brand loyalty, and new features instead.

The temptation to cooperate is strong. Collusion is a secret agreement among firms to act together rather than compete. Firms that collude may agree on price fixing: setting prices at an agreed level instead of letting competition determine them. If successful, they could earn the profits of a monopoly, which hurts buyers. Price fixing is illegal in the United States under antitrust laws, which Section 4 explains. Firms are also tempted to cheat on such agreements, which is why they are hard to maintain.

Measuring Concentration: Concentration Ratios and the HHI

How can you tell whether a market is dominated by a few firms? Economists and regulators use numbers. The simplest is the concentration ratio: the share of the market's sales made by the largest firms, usually the four largest. A four-firm concentration ratio of 85 percent means that the four biggest firms make 85 percent of the sales. Figure 7.12 uses this idea for meatpacking: the U.S. Department of Agriculture found that the four largest packers bought 85 percent of the steers and heifers in the latest figure it reported, compared with 36 percent in 1980.

A concentration ratio has a weakness: it ignores how sales are divided among the top firms. A market where four firms each have 20 percent has the same four-firm ratio (80 percent) as a market where one firm has 77 percent and three firms have 1 percent each. Regulators therefore also use the Herfindahl-Hirschman Index, or HHI. To calculate it, square each firm's percentage share of the market and add the results. Squaring gives large firms a heavier weight. Five firms with 20 percent each produce an HHI of 5 × 20² = 2,000. One firm with the entire market has an HHI of 100² = 10,000, the highest possible. A market with 100 equal firms has an HHI of just 100.

Under the 2023 Merger Guidelines issued by the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission, a market with an HHI above 1,800 is considered highly concentrated. A merger that raises the HHI by more than 100 points in such a market is presumed to harm competition, and so is a merger that creates a firm with more than 30 percent of the market and raises the HHI by more than 100 points. When two firms merge, the HHI rises by twice the product of their shares. If a firm with 20 percent buys one with 5 percent, the HHI rises by 2 × 20 × 5 = 200. The measure is only a starting point, because the result depends on how the market is defined, as you saw earlier. Regulators also consider the ease of entry, the way buyers shop, and other evidence.

The Prisoner's Dilemma: Why Rivals Find Cooperation Hard

Because oligopolists depend on one another, economists use game theory, the study of how people and firms make decisions when each one's best choice depends on what the others do. The best-known example is the prisoner's dilemma. In its original form, two suspects are questioned separately, and each is tempted to betray the other even though both would be better off staying silent. The same logic applies to firms deciding on a price.

Look at the hypothetical payoff table in Figure 7.14. Two gas stations stand on opposite corners of an intersection, and each can set a high price or a low price. If both keep prices high, each earns $6,000 a week. If one cuts its price and the other does not, the cutter attracts customers and earns $9,000, while the station with the high price drops to $2,000. If both cut, each earns only $4,000. Suppose you are Station A. If Station B charges a high price, your best reply is a low price ($9,000 beats $6,000). If B charges a low price, your best reply is still a low price ($4,000 beats $2,000). A choice that is best no matter what the rival does is called a dominant strategy. Station B reasons the same way. Both end up charging low prices and earning $4,000, even though they could each have earned $6,000.

The prisoner's dilemma explains why price wars start and why agreements to avoid them are fragile. Both firms would like to be at the high-price outcome, but each has a reason to cheat. In the real world, firms deal with each other again and again, and that changes the incentives. A station that cuts its price today may face retaliation tomorrow, so some oligopolists keep prices high without ever talking to each other, simply by watching and matching their rivals. Economists call this tacit collusion. It is hard for the law to prove, but an explicit agreement to fix prices is illegal, as the next section explains. For buyers, the lesson is that competition among a few firms can produce either a price war or quiet cooperation, and the difference matters a great deal for the price at the pump.

Key Concepts

Cartels and the Temptation to Cheat

A cartel is the most open form of cooperation among rivals. Its members agree to limit their output so that the price will rise, and all of them can earn something close to monopoly profits. The most famous international cartel is the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries, or OPEC, founded in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela. Its member governments meet to set production targets for crude oil. When members agree to produce less, the world supply of oil shifts left, and the price of oil tends to rise; when they produce more, it tends to fall. Because oil is sold in a world market, decisions made in Vienna or Riyadh can change the price of gasoline in Bakersfield within weeks.

Cartels are unstable because of the prisoner's dilemma. Each member earns more if it secretly produces a little above its quota while the others hold back. If all members do this, the price falls and the cartel's benefits vanish. A cartel works only if its members can watch one another and punish cheaters, and if outsiders cannot easily enter the market. Oil cartels have had periods of success and periods of weakness, and their power depends partly on how much oil is produced outside the group.

In the United States, a cartel among private companies is a crime. Price fixing, bid rigging, and dividing up markets violate Section 1 of the Sherman Act, and the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice prosecutes these cases. The maximum penalties are severe: a company can be fined up to $100 million, and an executive can be sentenced to up to 10 years in prison. Prosecutors also use the prisoner's dilemma against cartels. Under the Justice Department's leniency program, the first member to come forward and confess can receive protection from criminal prosecution. That gives every member a reason to rush to confess before a partner does, and makes secret agreements riskier to join.

Key Concepts

Putting the Four Structures Side by Side

Now that you have met all four structures, you can compare them on the questions that matter most to buyers. In perfect competition, firms have no control over price, the long-run profit is zero, and the price equals the lowest average cost and the marginal cost, so the market is efficient. In monopolistic competition, firms have a little control over price, entry pushes profit toward zero, and the price is slightly above marginal cost, but buyers get variety. In an oligopoly, a few firms have considerable power, high barriers can keep profits above normal, and the outcome depends on whether the firms compete or cooperate. In a monopoly, the single firm has the most power, can earn profits for years, and charges a price well above marginal cost, which produces a deadweight loss.

These differences do not mean that more competition is always better, or that large firms are always harmful. Some economists, following the thinking of Joseph Schumpeter, argue that large firms with profits can afford to invest in research and invent new products, and that the hope of a temporary monopoly is what drives innovation. Others reply that competition pushes firms to innovate in order to survive. The evidence supports both ideas in different industries. What economists agree on is that the structure of a market shapes the choices of firms, and that those choices determine the prices and the quality that you experience as a buyer.

The four structures are also not permanent. Technology can change them. A local store that was a near-monopoly can face competition from online sellers. A market with many sellers can become an oligopoly after a wave of mergers. A good consumer and a good citizen asks three questions of any market: how many sellers are there, how hard is it to enter, and what would happen to price and quality if one more rival arrived?

Key Concepts

Why Government Steps In

Competition does not always last on its own. Firms may grow so large or so powerful that they dominate a market, or they may secretly agree to avoid competing. Standard 12.3.1 lists “attempting to make markets more competitive” among the roles of government in a market economy. Regulation is government control of business activity through rules and requirements. It can limit prices charged by a natural monopoly, set safety standards, or prohibit certain practices.

In the late 1800s, American businesses in industries such as oil, railroads, and meatpacking began joining together in trusts: combinations of companies that were run together to reduce competition. The most famous was the Standard Oil trust, which at its peak controlled most of the oil refining in the United States. Farmers, shippers, and small businesses complained that trusts charged high prices and shut out competitors. Public concern led Congress to act.

The antitrust legislation that followed is a group of laws that promote competition and limit monopoly power. The Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890 made it illegal to restrain trade or to monopolize a market. The Clayton Act of 1914 banned specific practices, such as mergers that would substantially lessen competition, and the Federal Trade Commission Act of 1914 created the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) to investigate unfair business practices. The Celler-Kefauver Act of 1950 strengthened the rules on mergers. A merger is the joining of two companies into one. The Hart-Scott-Rodino Act of 1976 requires large companies to notify the government before they merge, so the review can take place before the companies combine. Today the FTC and the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice enforce these laws, and state attorneys general may also bring cases.

Landmark Antitrust Cases: From Standard Oil to Google

Laws matter only if they are enforced, and a few famous cases show how antitrust has worked. In 1911, the Supreme Court ruled that the Standard Oil Company of New Jersey had violated the Sherman Act and ordered it to be broken into separate companies. The same year, it ordered the breakup of the American Tobacco Company. Both cases showed that a company could be too powerful to be left whole, and they set the standard that courts have used since: it is not size alone that is illegal, but the use of power to shut out competition.

The most dramatic modern case involved the telephone. For most of the twentieth century, AT&T and its Bell System provided most local and long-distance telephone service in the United States. The Justice Department sued in 1974, and in January 1982 AT&T and the Justice Department announced a settlement in which AT&T agreed to give up its local telephone companies. The breakup took effect on January 1, 1984, creating seven independent regional companies and leaving AT&T as a long-distance carrier that now had to compete with new rivals. Long-distance competition grew in the years that followed, a point that economists often cite as a case in which antitrust action helped to open a market.

Antitrust cases continue today, and many involve technology. In August 2024, a federal judge ruled that Google had illegally kept a monopoly in general online search, in part by paying to be the default search engine on phones and browsers. In September 2025, the same judge decided on the remedy. He did not order Google to sell its Chrome browser, but he barred exclusive default deals and ordered Google to share some search data with rivals. The decisions can be appealed, and cases like this one change over time, so check current news for the latest. In December 2024, judges blocked the Kroger–Albertsons grocery merger, which you will examine in the Case Study. Notice that these cases look at the effect on buyers and on rivals, not simply at the size of a company.

Key Concepts

Price Fixing, Mergers, and Enforcement

Antitrust enforcement targets two kinds of conduct. The first is agreements among competitors, such as price fixing, bid rigging, and dividing up markets. Courts treat such agreements as illegal in themselves, because they have no purpose other than to avoid competition. Executives who fix prices can be fined and sent to prison. The second is mergers and acquisitions. Not every merger is harmful. Many combine firms in different markets or help companies lower costs. Regulators therefore ask whether a particular merger would substantially lessen competition in a particular market, such as the groceries sold in a city or the wireless phone service in a region.

Regulators also consider remedies. They may let a merger proceed if the companies sell some stores or plants to another company, a step called a divestiture, so that competition continues. Whether such a remedy would work is one of the main disputes in the Case Study at the end of this chapter.

Antitrust policy involves judgment. Critics of strict enforcement argue that large companies can lower costs and compete worldwide and that technology moves faster than the law. Supporters answer that large companies can raise prices, hold down wages, and limit new competitors. Economists weigh evidence about prices, quality, wages, and innovation in each case.

Key Concepts

How a Merger Review Works

When two large companies plan to merge, the Hart-Scott-Rodino Act sets up a process. First, the companies must file a notice with both the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice, describing the deal and their businesses. Then they must wait: the waiting period is generally 30 days (15 days for a cash tender offer) before the deal can close. Only one of the two agencies reviews a given deal, and the staff of the agencies decide which one takes the case in a process called clearance.

Most deals clear quickly. The review begins with the question of where the two companies compete with each other. In fiscal year 2025 (October 2024 through September 2025), 2,006 transactions were reported under the act, and the agencies issued a more detailed request for information, called a second request, in 41 investigations, about 2 percent of the deals. A second request asks both companies for documents and data about their products, prices, and rivals. It extends the waiting period until the companies have complied, and the agency then generally has about 30 more days to decide.

After the review, an agency has three main choices. It can let the deal go forward. It can negotiate a consent agreement, in which the companies agree to a remedy, such as selling stores or plants to a competitor. Or it can go to federal court to seek an injunction, a court order that stops the deal while the case is decided. That is what the FTC did in February 2024 when it sued to block the Kroger–Albertsons merger. In practice, companies often give up on a deal when they learn that a challenge is likely. Throughout the process, the agencies use the tools from Section 3: the definition of the market, concentration measures such as the HHI, evidence about how customers shop, and the likelihood that new competitors will enter. States also enforce competition laws: California's own antitrust law, the Cartwright Act, dates to 1907, and the state Attorney General joined the challenge to Kroger–Albertsons.

Key Concepts

Protecting Consumers Beyond Competition

Standard 12.3.1 lists “protecting consumers' rights” next to “attempting to make markets more competitive” among the roles of government in a market economy. The two are connected, but they are not the same. Competition policy aims to keep markets open and rivals vigorous. Consumer protection aims to make sure buyers receive honest information and safe products, even in markets that are competitive. A market can have many sellers and still contain sellers who lie about what they are selling.

The Federal Trade Commission has both jobs. The Federal Trade Commission Act, amended in 1938, bars unfair or deceptive acts and practices, which include false advertising, hidden fees, and scams. If a company claims that a product has been tested when it has not, or hides a fee until the last step of a purchase, the FTC can order the company to stop and, in some cases, to repay customers. Other agencies protect consumers in particular fields. The Consumer Product Safety Commission, created in 1972, sets safety rules and organizes recalls of products such as children's toys and household appliances. The Food and Drug Administration and the Department of Agriculture oversee the safety and the labels of food, and the FDA reviews medicines before they can be sold. California adds its own consumer protection laws, which the state Attorney General and local district attorneys enforce.

Why does the economy need consumer protection when buyers can choose among sellers? One reason is the idea of information, which you saw in Section 1: perfect competition assumes that buyers and sellers have good information. When buyers cannot easily judge quality, as with a medicine, a used car, or the safety of a ladder, sellers who cut corners can gain an advantage over honest sellers, unless rules require disclosure. Consumer protection therefore helps honest sellers as well as buyers. As with every form of regulation, the question for economists is not whether to have rules, but whether the benefits of a particular rule exceed its costs.

Key Concepts

Deregulation: Lifting the Rules

Regulation can protect competition and consumers, but it can also limit competition. Some rules protect firms already in an industry from new rivals, and others make companies follow costly procedures. Deregulation is the reduction or removal of government regulation of an industry. Beginning in the 1970s, Congress deregulated several industries in the belief that more competition would lead to lower prices and better service.

The best-known example is the airline industry. Before the Airline Deregulation Act of 1978, a federal board controlled the routes that airlines could fly and the fares they could charge. After the act, airlines were free to set fares and choose routes, and new airlines entered. Fares generally fell over time, and many more people began to fly, although some small communities lost service and the industry went through repeated waves of mergers. Congress also eased rules on trucking and railroads in 1980 and rewrote the rules for telephone service in the Telecommunications Act of 1996.

Deregulation does not always work as planned. In 1996, California restructured its electricity market by passing a law that allowed competition in power generation while keeping retail prices regulated. In 2000 and 2001, the state suffered an energy crisis with sharp price spikes and rolling blackouts. Economists and officials continue to debate the causes, which included high demand, tight supplies, and flaws in the design of the market. The episode shows that a market for an essential good such as electricity needs careful rules, whether the structure is regulated or competitive.

Key Concepts

California's Electricity Crisis: A Lesson in Market Design

California's experience in the late 1990s and early 2000s shows how hard it is to design a competitive market for an essential good. In 1996, the state passed a law to restructure its electricity market. The utilities were encouraged to sell many of their power plants, and wholesale power would be bought and sold in a market in which generators competed. The prices that consumers paid, however, were held fixed by the regulators.

In the summer and winter of 2000, the wholesale price of electricity soared. Demand was high, the supply of power was tight, and some generators and traders took advantage of the market's design. Utilities now had to buy power at high prices and sell it to customers at the fixed prices, and the difference threatened to bankrupt them. In January and March 2001, California experienced rolling blackouts, and in April 2001 Pacific Gas and Electric filed for bankruptcy. Investigations by federal regulators later found that some energy companies had manipulated the market. The state spent billions of dollars on emergency power purchases, and the crisis became a major political issue.

Economists draw several lessons. A market for a good that cannot be stored, whose demand swings through the day, and whose supply is limited by wires and plants, is vulnerable to market power even when many sellers exist, because at the moment of highest demand a small number of generators may be essential. Prices that buyers cannot see, and therefore cannot respond to, make it harder for demand to adjust. And rules that fix some prices while freeing others can be dangerous. The episode did not prove that regulated monopolies are always better or that competition always works. It showed that how a market is built matters as much as whether it is regulated.

Key Concepts

The Costs of Regulation and the Risk of Capture

Regulation is not free. Rules require firms to hire lawyers, fill out reports, and change the way they work, and those costs often end up in prices. A good rule is one whose benefits are greater than its costs, and economists try to measure both. A rule that prevents a deadly product from reaching the market may be worth far more than the cost of enforcing it. A rule that requires a license to cut hair or arrange flowers may do little to protect the public while limiting entry.

Economists also worry about regulatory capture: a situation in which an agency created to protect the public ends up serving the interests of the industry it regulates. The economist George Stigler won the Nobel Prize partly for pointing out that industries have strong reasons to influence the rules that govern them, while the public, whose interest is spread thinly across millions of people, has little reason to pay attention. A firm may favor a regulation that raises costs if the rule is more expensive for its new rivals than for itself. Regulators also depend on the industry for information, which can lead them to see problems the way the industry does.

These risks do not mean that regulation is a mistake. They mean that regulators need to be open to review, that their decisions should be explained publicly, and that citizens should pay attention. When you read about a proposed rule, ask three questions. Who gains, and who pays? Is there evidence that the rule works? And could a more competitive market solve the problem without the rule? Asking these questions is a part of what the California standards call being an informed citizen of a market economy.

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · What Is Perfect Competition?
A market structure classifies a market by the number of sellers, the closeness of substitutes, and the ease of entry; these determine a firm's market power. Perfect competition has many buyers and sellers, a standardized product, good information, free entry and exit, and price-taking sellers. A price taker's marginal revenue equals the price, so it produces until marginal cost reaches the price, shuts down in the short run only if the price falls below average variable cost, and earns zero economic profit in the long run, when entry and exit make the market allocatively and productively efficient.
Section 2 · The Impact of Monopoly
A monopoly is a single seller with no close substitutes, protected by barriers to entry such as economies of scale, control of a resource, patents and licenses, and network effects. A monopolist is a price maker whose marginal revenue is below its price, so it produces less and charges more than a competitive market, which creates a deadweight loss. Price discrimination can raise a seller's profit and sometimes the quantity sold. Generic competition shows how prices fall as sellers enter, and regulators set utility rates by rate-of-return or price-cap rules.
Section 3 · Other Market Structures
Monopolistic competition has many sellers with differentiated products; entry erodes short-run profit, leaving zero profit, excess capacity, and variety. An oligopoly has a few large, interdependent sellers, which regulators measure with concentration ratios and the HHI. The prisoner's dilemma explains why price wars start and why cartels, such as OPEC, are unstable. Collusion and price fixing are illegal in the United States because they harm buyers.
Section 4 · Regulation and Deregulation Today
Antitrust laws (Sherman Act 1890, Clayton and FTC Acts 1914, Celler-Kefauver 1950, Hart-Scott-Rodino 1976) and landmark cases promote competition. The FTC and the Department of Justice review mergers through notice, a waiting period, and sometimes a second request, and may clear, settle, or sue. Consumer protection laws guard against deception and unsafe products. Deregulation has had mixed results: airline fares rose more slowly than prices overall, but California's electricity crisis showed that market design matters.
