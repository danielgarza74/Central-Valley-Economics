Concept Review

In Chapter 1 you learned that scarcity forces every person and every society to choose. A society cannot avoid the three basic economic questions: What will be produced? How will it be produced? For whom will it be produced? This chapter asks a bigger question. Who gets to decide? The answer to that question shapes the jobs people can hold, the prices they pay, the businesses they can start, and even how free they are to speak and to choose.

Chapter 2 Key Concept

An economic system is the way a society organizes the use of its scarce resources to answer the three basic economic questions. Systems differ mainly in who makes the decisions (families and tradition, government planners, or buyers and sellers in markets) and in who owns the resources. Every real economy is a mixture, and the mixture a country chooses affects both its prosperity and its freedom.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine that the Shafter City Council passed a surprising rule. Starting Monday, every food business in town (the taquerías, the bakery, the food trucks outside the high school) would receive the same ingredients from a city warehouse, cook from the same menu, and charge the same prices. Workers would be assigned to restaurants by a city office. On the one hand, nobody's food business would fail. On the other hand, you could not open the taco truck you have been dreaming about, your favorite cook could not move to a better kitchen, and no owner would have a reason to try a new salsa. Now flip the rule: the city does nothing at all, and every business has to fend for itself. Some thrive, some close, and some neighborhoods get no grocery store. Neither extreme sounds perfect. Real societies pick a mixture of choices somewhere in between, and by the end of this chapter you will be able to describe the main economic systems, explain who decides in each one, and evaluate how well each protects both prosperity and liberty.

Key Concepts

Economic Systems and the Three Questions

Picture two very different ways to get lunch. In the first, you walk into a cafeteria where an adult has already decided what is on the menu, how it was cooked, and which students may take a second helping. In the second, you walk down a row of food trucks, compare menus and prices, and spend your money where you think you will get the best deal. Both ways answer the same questions about lunch, but different people make the decisions and follow different rules. Societies face the same situation on a much larger scale.

In Chapter 1 you learned that scarcity forces every society to answer three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. A society does not answer those questions by accident. It answers them through a set of rules, customs, and institutions that economists call an economic system. An economic system is the organized way a society uses its scarce resources to produce goods and services and to distribute them among its people. Every country has one, and so does every tribe, kingdom, and city-state that ever existed.

Two features of an economic system matter most. The first is who makes the decisions. In some systems, families follow rules passed down by their ancestors. In others, government officials issue orders. In still others, millions of buyers and sellers make their own choices and trade with one another. The second feature is who owns the resources, which means the land, factories, tools, and businesses used to produce goods. Resources can belong to private individuals and companies, to the government, or to a family or community as a group. As you read this chapter, keep asking those two questions about each system you meet: who decides, and who owns?

Economists usually sort economic systems into three main types, plus a fourth that blends them. A traditional economy relies on custom. A command economy relies on government orders. A market economy relies on voluntary trade among individuals. A mixed economy combines features of the others. No country perfectly matches any single type. The types are best understood as models, simplified descriptions that help us see how real economies differ. In this section you will take a first look at all four, and then study the traditional economy in more depth. Sections 2 and 3 take a closer look at the command and market types, and Section 4 explains mixed economies.

Whoever holds the control also holds a great deal of power over how everyone else lives.

A quick comparison can help. In a traditional economy, a family or community asks, "What did our parents and grandparents do?" In a command economy, a government planner asks, "What does the national plan require?" In a market economy, a business owner asks, "What will customers buy at a price that covers my costs?" And in a mixed economy, all three questions get asked at once, with the balance set by laws, voters, and history.

Key Concepts

Traditional Economies

For most of human history, nearly everyone lived in a traditional economy. In this system, families, clans, or villages answer the three basic economic questions by following custom, the habits and beliefs that people have passed from one generation to the next. Little is written down in a rulebook, and no central office hands out orders. People simply know what their role is, because their parents and grandparents performed it before them.

The central goal of most traditional economies is survival. A community that depends on hunting, fishing, herding, or gathering must make sure that enough food, shelter, and clothing exist for everyone to live. So the answer to what to produce is almost always the same: whatever the group needs to stay alive. The answer to how to produce follows older methods that have worked reliably. Farmers plant the same crops in the same seasons with the same tools, and hunters follow the same trails their ancestors used. The answer to for whom is shared according to custom, with sharing rules that might give priority to children, elders, hunters who made a kill, or families with the greatest need.

Many traditional economies rely on subsistence farming, in which a family grows only enough food to feed itself and has little or nothing left to sell. Because almost every household must farm to survive, there is little room for specialization, and the economy changes slowly. Section 2 of this chapter shows how very different this is from an economy in which a planner or a market directs resources.

Roles in a traditional economy are usually set at birth. A girl may learn to farm or weave because her mother did. A boy may take up herding or craft work because that is the family's trade. Young people learn skills by working beside older relatives, and the group's well-being usually counts for more than any individual's personal ambition. Resources such as grazing land, forests, or fishing waters are often shared by the whole community rather than owned by one person.

Traditional economies exist in many forms around the world and across time. Before Spanish settlers arrived in California in the 1700s, the Yokuts people and their neighbors lived in the San Joaquin Valley and the surrounding foothills. Their economy was based on hunting, fishing, and gathering the region's abundant plants and animals, and they also traded with other California Indian communities. Today, some herding families in East Africa, some farming villages in the Andes, and some Arctic hunting communities still follow economic customs that are centuries old, although almost none are cut off from the modern world.

Strengths of a Traditional Economy

A traditional economy has real advantages, which is one reason it lasted for thousands of years. First, it answers the three economic questions clearly and with little argument. Everyone knows what to produce, how to do it, and how to share the results, so the community avoids the conflict and confusion that come from competing plans. Second, it provides stability and a sense of belonging. People know their place in the group and can count on relatives and neighbors for help. Third, it can pass down deep local knowledge. A community that has farmed, fished, or herded in the same place for centuries may know exactly how to read the weather, protect the soil, and keep animals healthy.

In a bad year, the group's customs of sharing may mean that nobody is left to starve while others eat well, as long as there is enough food somewhere in the community.

Limits of a Traditional Economy

The same features that make a traditional economy stable also make it hard to change. Because custom decides, new ideas may be seen as a threat. A farmer who wants to try a different seed or a faster tool may face pressure from relatives and neighbors to keep to the old ways. When people produce things the same way generation after generation, productivity, which is the amount of output per worker, stays low. Low productivity means that the group produces little beyond what it needs to survive, so people have a lower standard of living than people in societies that find ways to produce more.

Fixed roles also limit individual choice. A young person who dreams of becoming an engineer, a nurse, or an artist may never have the chance if the community expects that person to follow a parent's trade. When talented people are assigned to the wrong jobs, a society wastes its human capital. And because many traditional communities live close to the edge of survival, a drought, flood, or disease can be devastating when the group has no savings, no insurance, and no trading partners to turn to. The standard of living, a measure of the goods, services, and comforts available to people, is therefore usually lower in economies that rely mainly on tradition.

EXAMPLEThree Ways to Run One Taquería

To see how different systems work, imagine that the same small taquería faces the same question: should it add a new menu item, such as birria tacos? The answer depends on which system the community lives in.

In a traditional setting, the cook would probably ask, "What has our family always served?" If the family has always made carne asada and carnitas, the new dish would probably be turned down, even if customers might love it, because custom says to stay with what works. In a command setting, the cook would wait for a government office to decide whether the restaurant may add a new dish, how much meat the restaurant may receive, and what price it may charge. In a market setting, the owner would study customers, count the cost of the beef, chiles, and tortillas, and decide whether the new item can earn a profit. If customers like the dish, it stays; if not, it disappears from the menu.

Notice how each system changes who has the power. Tradition gives it to the past, command gives it to officials, and the market gives it to buyers and sellers.

Key Concepts

Pressure to Change

Today, almost no economy is purely traditional. The forces of change are powerful, and they reach even remote communities. Roads, cell phones, radio, television, and the internet carry information about the outside world into villages that were once isolated. Young people see images of cities, schools, and jobs that offer different ways of life. Some leave to look for wages in a town or a foreign country. Others stay but begin to sell part of what they grow in a nearby market instead of eating all of it.

Governments and companies also push change. A government might build a road, a school, or an irrigation canal that lets farmers grow more. A company might buy crops for export or hire workers for wages. Markets slowly replace sharing and barter with money. Barter, trading goods directly for other goods without using money, is clumsy because it requires a double coincidence of wants: you must find someone who has what you want and also wants what you have. Money removes that problem, which is why it spreads quickly wherever it is introduced.

California itself shows how fast an economic system can change. After the Gold Rush of 1848, newcomers brought commercial agriculture, private land titles, railroads, and wage labor to the Central Valley, and within a few decades the economic life of the valley looked entirely different from what it had been. Kern County today, with its irrigated orchards, oil fields, and wind and solar farms, is the result of that long chain of changes.

Change brings both opportunities and costs. Families that adopt new tools or crops can raise their incomes and educate their children. But the same changes can weaken customs of sharing, break up extended families as young people move away, and make communities depend on prices set in distant markets. When the price of coffee, cocoa, or cotton drops on the world market, a farmer thousands of miles away may suffer.

Key Concepts

Government Controls the Answers

Think about how much government already affects your day. Laws set speed limits and the age to drive. Health departments inspect restaurants. Taxes pay for schools, fire stations, and roads. But in the United States, those rules mostly set the boundaries inside which private individuals and businesses make their own choices. Now suppose the government did far more. Suppose that officials in a central office decided which businesses could open in Bakersfield, how much each one would produce every month, what workers would be paid, and what every product would cost. That is the idea behind a command economy.

A command economy is an economic system in which the government, rather than families or private markets, answers the three basic questions. Economists also call it a centrally planned economy because the key decisions are made by a central authority that creates a plan for the whole economy. A government planning agency decides what to produce: so many tons of steel, so many pairs of shoes, so many tractors. It decides how to produce by assigning raw materials, equipment, and workers to factories and farms. And it decides for whom by setting wages, setting prices, and controlling where goods are sent. Most important resources, such as land, factories, mines, and banks, are owned by the government rather than by private citizens.

Central planning has an obvious appeal. A government that controls all the resources can, in theory, direct them toward national goals: building railroads, guaranteeing everyone a job, expanding schools and hospitals, or equipping an army. Planners can aim to avoid the waste of duplicate businesses competing against each other and to make sure that basic goods reach everyone. Supporters of this system also argue that it can reduce the gaps between rich and poor, since the government decides how income and goods are divided.

Critics respond that a system that gives so much power to a small group of planners creates serious problems of its own. Planners must guess what millions of people want, and individuals have little say in the answer. In the sections that follow, you will see why most economies that began as command economies have since moved away from strict central planning, and why economists continue to debate what the right balance between government direction and individual choice should be.

Key Concepts

Where Command Economies Came From

Most modern command economies trace their ideas to Karl Marx, a nineteenth-century German philosopher and economist. In 1848, Marx and his collaborator Friedrich Engels published The Communist Manifesto, and in 1867 Marx published the first volume of Das Kapital. Marx looked at the factories of industrial Europe and saw a sharp division between two groups. One was the owners of factories, land, and machines. The other was the workers, who had to sell their labor to the owners in exchange for wages. Marx argued that owners kept much of the value the workers created, that this arrangement would create constant conflict, and that workers would eventually take over the factories and share the wealth.

Two terms come from Marx's ideas. Socialism is an economic system in which the government owns or controls some or all of the major resources, such as basic industries, and aims to share the benefits more equally. Communism is a more extreme form in which the government owns nearly all productive resources, private ownership of property is largely eliminated, and one political party controls the government. Communist governments have been authoritarian, which means that they demand strict obedience from citizens and allow little or no political opposition. In these systems, economic power and political power are placed in the same hands.

Not all socialists favor authoritarian government. Democratic socialism is the belief that socialist goals, such as government ownership of key industries and strong public services, should be pursued through elections and the democratic political process rather than through revolution and one-party rule. People use the term differently, and it is common to hear it applied to countries, such as the Nordic nations, that actually have mostly market economies with large government programs and high taxes. Economists therefore pay close attention to what a country actually does (who owns the resources, who sets prices, and who decides what is produced) rather than to the label it uses.

Marx's followers put his ideas into practice in the twentieth century. After the Russian Revolution of 1917, the Soviet Union set up a planning agency in 1921 and began launching five-year plans in 1928 that set production targets for factories and farms across a huge territory. China became a communist state in 1949 and also adopted central planning. Cuba, North Korea, Vietnam, and several Eastern European countries followed similar paths. During the Cold War, the competition between the Soviet bloc and the United States and its allies was a competition between two economic systems as well as two sets of political ideas.

Key Concepts

Command Economies Today

No country today runs a pure command economy, but some come close. The strongest example is North Korea, where the Heritage Foundation's 2026 Index of Economic Freedom ranked the economy 176th out of 176 countries, with state-owned industries accounting for nearly all of the country's output. In North Korea, the government has long decided what factories produce, how farms are run, and how goods are distributed. Cuba, where the government owns most large enterprises and long relied on a ration book to distribute basic foods, is another country with a heavily state-directed economy, although since 2010 the government has allowed some small private businesses to operate.

Other countries that once had command economies have moved sharply away from central planning while keeping one-party political systems. In 1978, China began to introduce market reforms by allowing farmers to keep and sell part of their harvest and by opening the economy to private business and foreign investment. Vietnam began a similar set of reforms in 1986. The Soviet Union and the communist governments of Eastern Europe collapsed between 1989 and 1991, and their economies were rebuilt with private ownership and markets. In 2026, the Heritage index placed China 154th and Cuba 175th among 176 countries, showing that many command-style features remain even where reforms have taken place.

Economists measure the extent of economic freedom in many ways. An index is a number that combines several measures into one score so that countries can be compared easily. Figure 2.4 shows the 2026 scores from the Index of Economic Freedom published by the Heritage Foundation, a research organization that favors limited government. The index scores countries from 0 to 100 on measures such as property rights, government integrity, business regulation, and trade freedom. Like any index, it reflects choices made by its authors about what to measure and how much each measure counts, and other organizations use different methods.

Key Concepts

Strengths and Weaknesses of Central Planning

In theory, command economies have some real advantages. A government that controls resources can move them quickly toward a single goal. The Soviet Union used central planning to build factories, railroads, and power plants at a rapid pace in the decades after 1928, and it launched Sputnik, the first artificial satellite, in 1957. Governments in command economies have also expanded literacy and basic health care in places where those services had been limited. And because the government decides who gets what, planners can try to reduce differences in income and guarantee that everyone has a job and basic goods.

But economists have identified several recurring problems with central planning. The first is information. A modern economy produces millions of different goods, and the people who know what customers want, which materials are running short, and which ideas work are scattered across the whole country. A group of planners cannot gather and process all that information. In a market, prices act as signals that carry information automatically. In a command economy, planners must guess.

The second problem is incentives. When a worker earns the same wage regardless of effort, and when a manager is rewarded for meeting a production target rather than for pleasing customers, there is little reason to work harder, cut costs, or improve quality. Factories may meet their targets by producing large quantities of low-quality goods, such as shoes in only a few sizes, because quantity is easy to count and quality is hard to measure.

The third problem concerns prices. Planners often set a price control, a government-ordered limit on the price of a good. When the government sets a price below the level at which the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers will supply, a shortage results. Buyers wait in long lines, shelves are empty, and goods that are not available in stores may be sold illegally on a black market, where buyers and sellers trade outside the law, usually at much higher prices. In Chapter 6 you will study how prices and shortages work in more depth.

EXAMPLEWhen Planners Set the Price Too Low

Suppose planners in a command economy decide that a loaf of bread must sell for the equivalent of $1.00, because they want bread to be affordable for everyone. At that price, the bakeries can supply only 100,000 loaves per day, because the price is too low to cover the cost of flour, labor, and energy, and the planners are not allowed to raise it. But at $1.00, customers want to buy 140,000 loaves.

The difference is a shortage of 40,000 loaves: 140,000 − 100,000 = 40,000. Because the price cannot rise to match the quantity demanded with the quantity supplied, bread must be distributed some other way. The loaves sell out in the morning. People stand in line, and some who work during bakery hours go without. Someone may buy bread for $1.00 and resell it on the black market for $2.00, making a profit and showing that buyers would have paid more.

Key Concepts

Command Economies and Liberty

The California content standards ask students to analyze how a market economy helps establish and preserve political and personal liberty. To understand that connection, it helps to see how command economies treat freedom. When the government owns the farms, factories, newspapers, and banks, and decides who gets which job, one set of officials holds both economic power and political power. A citizen who criticizes the government risks losing a job, an apartment, or access to goods that the government controls. That is why command economies have usually been authoritarian, and why people who disagree with the government have fewer places to turn for independent work or support.

In contrast, when many people own property and run businesses, no single group controls everyone's livelihood. A newspaper owner, a landowner, or a small business can support causes that the government dislikes because the government is not their employer. Supporters of market economies argue that this spreading out of economic power is one of the most important protections of political freedom. You will study that argument, which traces back to Adam Smith and later thinkers, in Section 3.

Not everyone agrees, and fairness requires considering the other side. Some economists and political scientists argue that markets can also concentrate power, for example when a few large companies dominate an industry or when wealthy people have far more influence in politics than other citizens. They point out that some market economies, such as Chile in the 1970s and 1980s, were governed by authoritarian rulers, and that some countries with strong public programs, such as the Nordic democracies, protect liberty well. These examples suggest that a market economy may help preserve liberty, but it does not guarantee it, and political institutions such as free elections, courts, and a free press matter as well.

CONNECT TO HISTORYThe fall of the Berlin Wall in 1989 became a symbol of the end of the Cold War. Within about two years, the communist governments of Eastern Europe had ended, and the Soviet Union dissolved in December 1991. In the years that followed, the former command economies had to build the markets and property rights they had lacked.

Key Concepts

What Is a Market?

If you have ever bought a snack, sold a used phone, or looked for a part-time job, you have taken part in a market. A market is any arrangement that allows buyers and sellers to trade. A market can be a place, like the Saturday farmers market, a swap meet, or the county fair. It can be a website such as an online store, or a network of phone calls between farmers and truckers. What matters is not the location, but the ability of buyers and sellers to find each other, agree on terms, and exchange goods, services, or resources.

A market economy is an economic system in which individual choices, made by millions of buyers and sellers, answer the three basic economic questions. Consumers decide what to buy with their money. Producers decide what to make, how to make it, and what price to ask. Prices rise and fall as buyers and sellers respond to one another, and those prices help steer resources toward their most valued uses. Economists often use the word capitalism to describe a market economy in which individuals and firms privately own most of the resources used for production, such as land, factories, and equipment.

In its purest form, a market economy needs no central planner. Nobody in Washington, D.C., or Sacramento decides how many tacos Bakersfield will eat on a Friday or how many acres of carrots Kern County will plant. Yet food appears in stores, trucks roll, and workers show up at packing houses. The coordination happens because each person responds to prices and to what they expect to gain.

The idea that a decentralized economy can organize itself comes from Adam Smith, a Scottish thinker whose 1776 book The Wealth of Nations you will meet in this section's Economics Pacesetter. Smith observed that when people pursue their own interests in a system with competition, they often serve the needs of others, as though guided by an "invisible hand." That phrase has become one of the best-known ideas in economics, and, as you will see, it is also one that people debate.

Key Concepts

Seven Features of a Market Economy

Economists identify several features that define a market economy. They fit together like the parts of an engine. Figure 2.6 summarizes them, and the paragraphs below explain each one.

Private property rights. Private property rights are the rights of individuals and businesses to own, use, rent, sell, or give away resources such as land, buildings, equipment, and their own labor. These rights are supported by laws and courts that protect owners from theft and settle disputes over contracts. Without them, people would hesitate to build a business or plant a tree if someone else could take it away. Property rights are so important that Section 3 returns to them below, and Chapter 3 develops them further.

Voluntary exchange. A voluntary exchange is a trade that both parties agree to freely, because each expects to gain. When you hand over $4 for a burrito, you must value the burrito more than the $4, and the seller must value the $4 more than the burrito. If either side believed it would lose, the trade would not happen. This is why economists say that voluntary exchange creates value: both sides end up better off in their own judgment, even though no new physical objects were made.

Profit. Profit is the money left over after a business subtracts its costs from its revenue. A firm that earns a profit has taken in more from customers than it paid for labor, materials, rent, and other costs. Profit rewards owners for taking risks and signals to others which products are in demand. Losses signal the opposite: that resources might be better used elsewhere.

Competition. Competition is the rivalry among sellers to attract buyers, and among buyers to obtain limited goods. When several taquerías near the same school compete, each tries to offer better food, faster service, or a lower price. Competition pushes sellers to keep prices down and quality up, and it rewards those who find better ways to produce.

Consumer sovereignty. Consumer sovereignty is the idea that consumers, through their spending choices, ultimately decide what gets produced. Businesses that make products people do not buy lose money and eventually stop. Those that serve customers well earn profits and expand.

Specialization. Specialization means concentrating on the activity you do best. A nurse, a welder, a pistachio grower, and a truck driver each specialize, and then they trade their earnings for the goods and services they do not produce. Specialization raises the total amount produced because each person gets better at one kind of work, and trade allows everyone to enjoy the results.

Limited government. In a pure market economy, the government's role is small. It protects property rights, enforces contracts, and provides a stable money system and defense. The phrase laissez faire, French for "let them do," expresses the belief that government should mostly leave economic decisions to individuals. In practice, no real country is purely laissez faire, as you will see in Section 4.

Key Concepts

The Circular Flow of a Market Economy

The seven features raise a question: how do they all fit together in an economy with millions of participants? Economists use a simplified model called the circular flow model to answer. A model, as you learned in Chapter 1, is a simplified picture of reality that leaves out details so that the main relationships stand out. The circular flow model shows how money, goods, and resources travel between two groups of decision makers: households and businesses.

Households are people who live together and share income, such as your family. Households own the resources that businesses need: their labor, and often land and capital as well. Businesses are organizations that use resources to produce goods and services. These two groups meet in two kinds of markets. In the product market, businesses sell goods and services, and households buy them with the money they have earned. In the factor market, households sell the use of their resources, such as labor, land, and savings, and businesses buy them. In return, households receive income in the form of wages for labor, rent for land, and interest for the use of money, and owners of businesses receive profit.

Follow the loop in Figure 2.7. A packing house near Shafter hires workers (factor market) and pays them wages. The workers use those wages to buy groceries, gasoline, and clothes (product market). The stores that sell those items pay their own workers, pay rent, and buy more goods from suppliers such as the packing house. The money keeps circling, and at every step, a voluntary exchange is taking place. If households spend less, businesses earn less, hire fewer workers, and pay less in wages, so household income falls, which reduces spending again. This feedback explains why economists in later chapters pay so much attention to total spending.

Key Concepts

Private Property and the Incentive to Conserve and Improve

The first feature, private property rights, has a special role in economics because of the way it changes people's incentives. An incentive, as you learned in Chapter 1, is something that motivates a person to act. When you own something, you usually bear the cost of neglecting it and you keep the benefits of taking care of it. That connection gives owners a powerful reason to protect and improve their property.

Consider an almond grower near Shafter who owns a 40-acre orchard. The trees take years to mature and can produce nuts for decades. If the owner mismanages the water, the soil, or the trees, the value of the orchard falls, and the loss is hers. If she invests in better irrigation, healthier trees, or improved soil, the value rises, and she keeps the gain, either through bigger harvests or by selling the orchard for more. Because she can expect to benefit from her care for many years, she has an incentive to look after the land. A tenant renting land for one season, with no assurance that the lease will continue, may have much less reason to make long-term improvements.

Economic evidence from California agriculture shows how private owners adapt their methods to the cost of water. According to the 2023 Irrigation and Water Management Survey of the U.S. Department of Agriculture, 56 percent of California's irrigated acres were watered with drip, trickle, or low-flow micro-sprinkler systems, which deliver water directly to the roots of crops. The total amount of water applied to California's irrigated land fell from about 24.5 million acre-feet in 2018 to 22.6 million acre-feet in 2023, a decrease of about 8 percent (Figure 2.8). Many things contributed to that decline, including weather, regulations, and water prices, so the data do not prove that property rights alone caused it. But growers who pay for pumping and who own their orchards have clear reasons to buy equipment that uses water more carefully.

The principle also applies to nonrenewable resources, those that cannot be replaced on a human timescale, such as oil and natural gas. Kern County has produced oil for well over a century. A person who owns the right to the oil under a piece of land can decide when to extract it. If the owner expects the price of oil to rise, she may leave some in the ground and sell it later, which spreads the use of the resource over time. Secure rights matter. In the early years of American oil drilling, the legal "rule of capture" allowed anyone with a well to keep the oil he pumped, even if it flowed from beneath a neighbor's land. Each owner raced to pump the shared pool of oil before the neighbors did, which wasted oil and gas pressure. Rules that let owners of a shared oil field coordinate their drilling were developed partly to fix that problem.

When Ownership Is Unclear: The Problem of the Commons

The opposite situation shows why property rights matter. When a resource is open to everyone and owned by no one, each user has an incentive to take as much as possible before others do, because the benefits go to the user while the costs are spread across everyone. The ecologist Garrett Hardin called this the "tragedy of the commons" in a famous 1968 essay, using the image of a village pasture where every herder adds one more animal until the grass is gone.

For decades, groundwater in parts of the San Joaquin Valley behaved like a commons. Many users pumped from the same underground aquifer, and each well owner gained the full benefit from the water pumped while the cost, a lower water table that made everyone's wells deeper and more expensive, was shared by all. By the 2010s, the overuse of groundwater had caused wells to go dry and land to sink in some areas. Chapter 1's Case Study examined how California's Sustainable Groundwater Management Act tries to bring pumping into balance.

Not every shared resource ends in a tragedy. The economist Elinor Ostrom, who won the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 2009, studied communities around the world that created their own rules for managing shared forests, fisheries, and irrigation systems. Her work showed that clear rules, monitoring, and agreement among users can protect a shared resource, even without full private ownership.

Kern County also offers an example of property-like arrangements that encourage saving water. The Kern Water Bank, located southwest of Bakersfield, can store about 1.5 million acre-feet of water underground, according to its operators. In wet years, water is spread on ponds and sinks into the aquifer; in dry years, the stored water is pumped back out for the agencies that put it there. Because the owners of the stored water can recover it later, they have a reason to save water in good years instead of letting it flow away.

Limits of the Property-Rights Argument

Private ownership does not guarantee good stewardship. Owners may care mostly about short-term profits and neglect long-term effects, especially if they plan to sell soon. Owners may also ignore costs that fall on other people. A factory that dumps waste in a river, or a farm that pumps so much groundwater that a neighbor's shallow well goes dry, imposes costs on others that the owner does not pay. Economists call such side effects externalities, and you will study them in later chapters. Because of such problems, governments in market economies often add rules, taxes, or programs to protect resources, as California does with its groundwater law. Most economists therefore describe private property as one important tool for encouraging conservation, but not the only one.

Key Concepts

Advantages and Disadvantages of Market Economies

People who study market economies point to several advantages. Efficiency is the first. Because producers need to cover costs to survive, they tend to avoid waste and to use resources where they are valued most. Responsiveness is the second. If customers start buying more electric cars or fewer printed newspapers, prices and profits send signals that move resources in that direction without any central order. Innovation is the third. The chance to earn a profit rewards inventors, entrepreneurs, and risk takers who come up with new products and cheaper ways to make old ones. Variety and freedom is the fourth. Consumers choose from many goods, workers choose among many jobs, and anyone can try to start a business.

Critics and supporters alike also point to disadvantages. First, markets can produce large differences in income and wealth, because rewards go to people who own valuable resources or have valuable skills, and some people have few of either. People who cannot work because of age or disability may receive little income without a safety net. Second, markets may not account for externalities such as pollution, which is why governments regulate them. Third, markets often fail to provide public goods, such as national defense or street lighting, because it is hard to charge people for something everyone can use. Fourth, markets move in cycles: periods of growth and periods of recession, when many people lose jobs. Fifth, firms sometimes gain so much market power that competition weakens.

Because of these concerns, almost no country has a pure market economy. Voters in market economies debate where to draw the lines: how much the government should tax, regulate, or provide. The next section examines how countries mix market features with government action.

Adam SmithThe Case for Economic Liberty

Many of the ideas in this chapter were organized into one book. In 1776, the same year that American colonists declared independence, a Scottish professor named Adam Smith published An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations. It became the foundation of modern economics.

Smith was born in Kirkcaldy, a small port town in Scotland. He studied at the University of Glasgow and at Oxford, and he later taught moral philosophy at Glasgow, where he wrote The Theory of Moral Sentiments about how people judge right and wrong. In his time, economic life in Britain and Europe was shaped by mercantilism, the belief that a nation grows rich by exporting more than it imports and by closely controlling trade. Governments granted monopolies to favored companies, and guilds limited who could practice a trade.

In The Wealth of Nations, Smith argued that a nation's wealth is not its stock of gold but its ability to produce goods and services. He pointed to the division of labor as a key source of productivity. In his famous example of a pin factory, he described a workshop where ten workers, each doing one or two of the steps in making a pin, could produce thousands of pins a day, while a worker doing every step alone could hardly make a single pin. Specialization, he argued, multiplies output.

Smith also argued that people pursuing their own interests, in a system with competition, often promote the good of others without meaning to. He noted that we get our dinner not because the butcher, brewer, and baker are generous but because they want to earn a living. In one passage, he used the image of an "invisible hand" to describe how individual choices can guide resources toward uses that benefit society. Economists today debate how far that idea extends, but it remains one of the most influential in the field.

Smith's belief in what he called a "system of natural liberty" linked economic freedom to personal freedom. He criticized laws that prevented people from choosing their trade or moving to find work, and he distrusted business owners who asked for monopolies or privileges from the government. He warned that merchants in the same business who meet together often end up plotting to raise prices. At the same time, Smith did not oppose all government action. He believed government must provide defense, administer justice, and support some public works and education. His writing helped create the argument, studied in the California standards, that open markets can help protect personal and political liberty by dispersing power.

Key Concepts

The Mixed Economy

Sections 2 and 3 described two ends of a range. At one end is the command economy, in which government planners answer the three basic questions. At the other end is the market economy, in which individuals do. No country operates at either extreme. Every real economy is a mixed economy, one that combines private decision making with government decisions. The question that divides voters, politicians, and economists is not whether to mix, but how much of each to use and where.

Think of the range as a dial rather than a switch. On the left side of Figure 2.9, the government owns most resources and makes most decisions. On the right side, individuals own nearly everything and the government does little more than enforce rules. Countries sit at different spots in between, and they can move over time. China, for example, moved to the right after 1978 while keeping tight political control, and the United Kingdom moved to the right in the 1980s after decades of expanding the role of government.

The United States is usually described as a mixed economy that leans strongly toward markets. Most of the things you buy come from private businesses, and most workers are employed by them. Yet governments at every level play large roles. They build roads and schools, run courts and police departments, regulate food and medicine, tax incomes, and send money to retirees and low-income families. The next pages describe those roles.

Key Concepts

What Governments Do in a Market Economy

Even Adam Smith, who argued for limited government, believed government had to do some things. Economists describe several roles that governments in market economies commonly play. You will study each of them in detail later in this book.

Protect property rights and enforce contracts. Courts, police, and land records let people trust that agreements will be kept. Markets depend on these rules.

Provide public goods and services. A public good is a good that everyone can use and that is hard to charge for, such as national defense, street lighting, or a flood-control levee. Because private firms cannot easily collect payment, governments usually provide them and pay with taxes. Governments also provide services like schools, libraries, and highways.

Correct externalities. When a business imposes costs on others, such as air pollution from a refinery or dust from a construction site, governments may set limits or charge fees. In Kern County, the San Joaquin Valley Air Pollution Control District sets rules on emissions from trucks, farms, and oil operations to protect air quality.

Promote competition. Government agencies investigate firms that try to fix prices or to block rivals, and they review mergers that could leave consumers with few choices.

Protect consumers and workers. Safety standards for food and drugs, rules about honest advertising, and laws about minimum wages and workplace safety are examples. California's minimum wage and rules on farmworker overtime are examples of these laws.

Stabilize the economy. Governments try to reduce the harm from recessions and from rapid price increases through taxes, spending, and the central bank's control of interest rates. Units 4 and 5 examine these tools.

Redistribute income and provide a safety net. Programs such as Social Security, unemployment insurance, Medicaid, and nutrition assistance move money from taxpayers to people who are retired, out of work, disabled, or poor. People disagree about how large these programs should be, because they involve a trade-off between helping people in need and keeping incentives to work and invest.

Measuring the Role of Government

How can we tell how big a role the government plays? One common measure is the share of the economy that governments spend. Economists compare government spending to gross domestic product (GDP), the total value of all final goods and services produced in a country in a year. You will study GDP in detail in Chapter 16. Spending also does not show how well the government carries out its tasks, or how many rules it places on private businesses. Key Concepts

Nationalizing and Privatizing

Countries do not stay at the same point forever. Voters and governments change the mix, and one way they do so is by shifting ownership between the public and private sectors. To nationalize an industry means for the government to take ownership of it. To privatize an industry means to sell government-owned businesses or assets to private owners.

The United States has done both on a small scale. In the 1960s, many private railroads that carried passengers were losing money, and several threatened to stop service. Congress responded by creating Amtrak, which began operating in 1971 and took over most intercity passenger trains. Amtrak's San Joaquins route runs through the Central Valley, including stops in Bakersfield and Wasco, and receives funding from the State of California as well as from the federal government. In the 1970s, several freight railroads in the Northeast went bankrupt, and the federal government created Conrail in 1976 to take over their lines. Conrail returned to profitability, and in 1987, the government sold it to the public through a stock offering, an example of privatization.

Privatization became a major trend in Britain in the 1980s, when Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher's government sold shares in state-owned companies such as British Telecom (1984), British Gas (1986), and British Airways (1987). Supporters argued that private owners would run these firms more efficiently and give customers better service. Critics argued that some of these industries, like water and rail, are hard to make competitive and could become private monopolies. Debates over whether to nationalize or privatize, and in which industries, continue in many countries today.

These changes show that the placement of a country on the range of economic systems is a choice that voters make over time. When economists evaluate nationalization or privatization, they ask questions such as: Does ownership change give owners an incentive to improve? Does competition exist? Who pays, and who benefits?

Key Concepts

Markets Across Borders: The Global Economy

No economy today stands alone. The global economy is the interconnected system of production, trade, and finance that links the economies of the world. The process by which economies become more closely connected through trade, investment, technology, and the movement of people is called globalization.

Trade is the most visible link. A pair of shoes sold in Bakersfield may be designed in one country, made from materials grown in a second, assembled in a third, and shipped on a vessel that docks in Oakland or Long Beach. Kern County itself is a global trading region. Almonds, pistachios, citrus, and grapes grown here are exported to markets in Asia and Europe, and oil and gas produced here are sold into world markets. Farms and packing houses in Shafter and Wasco depend on customers thousands of miles away, and on railroads and ports to reach them.

Figure 2.11 shows how much more connected the world has become. In 1970, exports of goods and services were about 13 percent of the world's total output. By 2024, they were about 29 percent. The ratio rose steadily during the 1990s and 2000s as new trade agreements, container shipping, and the internet made it easier to buy and sell across borders. It dipped in 2009 during a global recession and in 2020 during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Globalization brings benefits and costs. Consumers gain access to a wider range of products at lower prices. Businesses can sell to larger markets and buy cheaper inputs. But workers in industries that face new foreign competition may lose jobs, and communities that depended on those industries can suffer. Economists generally agree that trade raises total output, but they also recognize that the gains and losses are not shared equally. Governments make choices about trade through tariffs, trade agreements, and programs to help workers who lose jobs. Chapter 21 examines these choices in depth.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEFrom Poor to Rich in One Lifetime: South Korea

South Korea shows how choices about economic systems and trade can change a country's fortunes. After the Korean War ended in 1953, the country was among the world's poorest. According to the Bank of Korea, South Korea's income per person was less than $100 in 1953. By 2023, it had passed $36,000. During those decades, the government guided investment in industries such as steel, shipbuilding, and electronics, while private companies produced goods and sold them abroad, and the country opened itself to world markets.

Economists disagree about how much credit goes to government direction and how much to markets and trade. Some emphasize that Korea's government supported exports and invested heavily in education. Others point out that private competition and exports provided the discipline that forced firms to improve. Most agree that Korea's rise would have been impossible without access to the global economy. The Case Study at the end of this chapter compares South Korea with its neighbor, North Korea, which followed a different path.

Key Concepts

Evaluating Economic Systems

How should a society judge an economic system? Economists often use several criteria. Efficiency asks whether resources are used to produce the most of what people value. Equity asks whether the results are fair, a question on which people hold different views. Freedom asks how much choice people have as workers, consumers, and owners. Growth asks whether the economy is producing more over time. Security asks whether people can count on food, shelter, and income during hard times. Stability asks whether the economy avoids sharp swings in prices and jobs.

These goals sometimes conflict. A policy that increases security by guaranteeing a minimum income may reduce the incentive to work. A policy that increases growth may increase inequality. Because of these trade-offs, no system can claim to maximize every goal at once, and different societies weigh them differently. Economists can describe how a policy is likely to affect each goal, but the choice among them depends on values, which is why these questions are decided through voting, debate, and law.

The evidence from the past century points to some broad patterns. Economies in which governments tried to plan nearly everything, such as the Soviet Union and North Korea, struggled to meet people's needs and limited their political freedom. Economies with strong property rights, competition, and open trade tended to grow faster and to provide higher incomes. At the same time, nearly all wealthy countries today also have large public programs in education, health, and retirement. A lesson that many economists draw is that markets are powerful tools for creating wealth, and that governments in free societies play important roles in making sure the rules are fair and that basic needs are met. Others draw different lessons and argue for a smaller or a larger role for government. The next chapter examines the American version of this mix, the free enterprise system, in detail.

CASE STUDY

One Peninsula, Two Economies:
North Korea and South Korea

In 1945, the Korean Peninsula was one country with one people, one language, and one history. Today it holds two economies that answer the three basic questions in opposite ways. Comparing them is one of the closest things economists have to a natural experiment on economic systems.

Background

A Country Divided

At the end of World War II in 1945, Korea, which had been ruled by Japan since 1910, was divided along the 38th parallel into a northern zone occupied by the Soviet Union and a southern zone occupied by the United States. Each side set up its own government in 1948. The Democratic People's Republic of Korea in the north, led by a communist party, adopted central planning on the Soviet model. The Republic of Korea in the south adopted a system based on private ownership and markets, although its government also played a large role in directing the economy in the early decades.

In 1950, North Korea invaded the South, beginning a war that drew in the United States, China, and other countries. Fighting ended in 1953 with an armistice, a cease-fire agreement, that has never been replaced by a peace treaty. The war killed millions of people, destroyed cities and factories on both sides, and left the peninsula divided along a heavily armed boundary known as the Demilitarized Zone. In 1953, both economies were in ruins, and South Korea was among the poorest countries in the world.

The two countries started from similar conditions in language, culture, and recent history, but they chose different economic systems and developed in strikingly different ways. North Korea's government owns nearly all productive resources and issues production targets through central plans. South Korea's economy relies mostly on private businesses and markets, and sells large quantities of goods abroad. In the last sixty years, the two have diverged by a wide margin, as the documents below show. Remember as you read that the gap has many causes, including war damage, geography, politics, foreign alliances, and international sanctions, and that economists debate how much each one matters.

THE INCENTIVES VIEW
"The two countries started with the same people and a similar history. What differed was the rules. "South Korea was never a pure market economy. In the 1960s and 1970s, the government directed bank loans toward chosen industries and rewarded firms that exported. It also invested heavily in schools. Government and markets worked together, and it was the exporting that disciplined the companies."
THE CONTEXT VIEW
"The North inherited most of the peninsula's mines and heavy industry, and the South inherited most of its farmland, so the starting points were not equal. Since then, the South has had large amounts of American aid and open access to world markets, while the North has faced sanctions and isolation. Economic systems matter, but so do history and alliances."

Chapter 2 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Types of Economic Systems
An economic system is the way a society answers the three basic questions. Traditional economies rely on custom, command economies on central planning, market economies on individual choices, and mixed economies on a blend. Standard of living is one way to compare systems.
Section 2 · Command Economies
In a command economy, the government owns most resources and plans production. Communism and socialism differ in the degree of public ownership, and democratic socialism keeps elections. Price controls can cause shortages and black markets, and planning limits freedom and incentives.
Section 3 · Market Economies
Market economies are built on private property rights, voluntary exchange, profit, competition, consumer sovereignty, specialization, and limited government. The circular flow model shows how households and businesses meet in product and factor markets. Property rights create incentives to conserve and improve resources.
Section 4 · Mixed Economies in a Global Age
Every real economy is mixed, and governments protect rights, supply public goods, correct externalities, and provide a safety net. Nations can nationalize or privatize industries. Globalization connects economies through trade, bringing gains and costs.
