Concept Review

In Chapter 2 you compared economic systems and met South Korea, a country that was very poor in the 1950s and is now one of the world's richest. In Chapter 16 you learned how gross domestic product (GDP) measures a country's output, and in Chapter 21 you saw how trade lets countries specialize and gain. This chapter asks the question that connects them: why are some countries rich and others poor, and what can poor countries do to change that? The answer draws on everything you have learned about incentives, property rights, markets, saving, and trade.

Chapter 22 Key Concept

Economic development is a sustained improvement in people's income, health, education, and opportunities. Countries develop when they build up human capital, physical capital, and technology, protect property rights and the rule of law, and connect with the world through trade and finance. Economists agree on many of these foundations, but they disagree about how fast to change an economy and how large a role the government should play.

Why the Concept Matters

Suppose a farmworker's family near Shafter earns enough to pay rent, buy groceries, and keep a car running, and the children attend a school with books, electricity, and a nurse. Now imagine the same family living in a country where the school has no electricity, the nearest clinic is a day's walk away, a loan costs several times what a bank in Bakersfield would charge, and a harvest depends entirely on the weather. The two families may work equally hard. What differs is the economy around them. Understanding why helps you evaluate claims about trade, aid, and immigration, and about what the United States should do in the world. It also explains why many families in Kern County have relatives who send money home to countries such as Mexico.

Case Study Preview

In 1986, Vietnam's leaders began a set of reforms called Doi Moi ("renovation"). Over the next three decades, the share of Vietnamese living on less than $3.00 a day fell from more than half to under 2 percent. At the end of this chapter you will analyze how Vietnam changed its economy, weigh the evidence, and consider what the story does and does not prove about the best path to development.

Key Concepts

What Is Economic Development?

Take a quick look at where your clothes, phone, and breakfast cereal were made, and you will find that people in dozens of countries had a hand in your day. Those people live in very different economies. In some, a typical family has a car, a refrigerator, and a savings account. In others, a typical family cooks over a wood fire, carries water from a well, and depends on one harvest a year. Economists want to understand why the gap is so large and how it can shrink.

Start with two ideas that are related but not the same. Economic growth, which you studied in Chapter 16, is an increase in a country's output of goods and services, usually measured by the rise in real GDP. Economic development is a broader idea: a sustained improvement in people's standard of living, including their income, but also their health, their education, their safety, and their freedom to make choices about their lives. Growth is usually necessary for development, because a country has to produce more before it can share more. But growth alone does not guarantee development. A country whose output rises while most people stay sick, uneducated, or poor has grown without developing very much.

Economists often sort the world's countries into two broad groups. A developing country is a country with a relatively low standard of living, and a developed country, sometimes called an advanced economy, is one with a relatively high standard of living. The United States, Canada, Japan, South Korea, and most of Western Europe are developed. Most countries in Africa and South Asia, and many in Latin America and Southeast Asia, are developing. These labels are rough. India has some of the world's most advanced technology companies and also hundreds of millions of people living on very little. Mexico has world-class factories and also rural areas with few jobs. Differences within a country can be as large as differences between countries.

The World Bank, an international organization that lends to developing countries and collects data about them, sorts every country into one of four income groups: low income, lower-middle income, upper-middle income, and high income. The groups are based on a country's average income per person. Because the groups change slowly and are tied to a clear number, they are the most common way to describe a country's level of development.

Why does the gap matter to people in the United States? Part of the reason is moral: most people believe that children everywhere should have a chance at a healthy life. Part is practical. Poor countries are potential trading partners and sources of workers, and conflict, disease, and migration in one part of the world can affect other parts. The countries that were poor a generation ago, such as South Korea, China, and Vietnam, are now among the largest buyers of California's farm products, as you saw in Chapter 21.

Key Concepts

Measuring Income: GNI and Purchasing Power

The first and most common measure of development is income per person. In Chapter 16, you learned that GDP counts the value of final goods and services produced inside a country's borders. The World Bank's preferred measure for sorting countries is gross national income (GNI), which counts the income earned by a country's residents, no matter where in the world they earn it. GNI is close to GDP for most countries. To compare countries of different sizes, economists divide by the population to get GNI per capita, the average income per person.

Figure 22.2 shows GNI per capita for the four World Bank income groups in 2024, measured in current U.S. dollars. The numbers are very far apart. The average person in a high-income country had an income of about $49,400, which was more than 64 times the average of about $765 in a low-income country. The world average, about $13,400, is much lower than the high-income average because most of the world's people live in middle-income countries.

Comparing countries in dollars has a problem: a dollar buys different amounts in different places. A haircut, a bus ride, or a bowl of noodles usually costs far fewer dollars in India than in California, because wages and rents are lower there. To adjust for this, economists use purchasing power parity (PPP), a method that converts incomes into a common set of prices so that an international dollar buys about the same basket of goods in every country. Income measured in PPP dollars is a better guide to how well people can actually live. The gaps in PPP terms are smaller than in market exchange rates but are still very large. In 2023, according to the United Nations, GNI per person in PPP terms was about $73,650 in the United States, about $13,000 in Vietnam, about $9,000 in India, and about $1,590 in Niger.

Averages have limits. GNI per capita says nothing about how income is divided. In a country where a small group earns most of the income, the average can be high while most families remain poor. It also leaves out work done at home, such as caring for children or growing food for the family, and it counts activities that harm well-being, such as cleaning up after a disaster, as output.

Key Concepts

Measuring Well-Being: The Human Development Index

Because income does not tell the whole story, the United Nations Development Programme (UNDP) created a broader measure in 1990, the Human Development Index (HDI). The HDI combines three kinds of information into a single number between 0 and 1: life expectancy at birth (a measure of health), years of schooling (a measure of education), and GNI per capita in PPP terms (a measure of the standard of living). A country with a score close to 1 does well on all three. Countries are then ranked from highest to lowest. In the report released in 2025, which uses data from 2023, 193 countries and territories were ranked.

Figure 22.3 shows the HDI for ten countries. Iceland ranked first, with a score of 0.972, followed by Norway and Switzerland. The United States ranked 17th, at 0.938, and South Korea ranked 20th, at 0.937. Mexico, at 0.789, ranked 81st. Vietnam, at 0.766, ranked 93rd, India, at 0.685, ranked 130th, and Kenya, at 0.628, ranked 143rd. At the bottom of the list were countries in conflict or with very low income. Somalia scored 0.404 and ranked 192nd, and South Sudan scored 0.388 and ranked 193rd.

The HDI tells you more than income alone. Compare life expectancy in the 2025 report: the United States had a higher income per person than almost any other country, but Americans' life expectancy was 79.3 years, lower than South Korea's 84.3 years, even though South Korea's income per person was lower. Bangladesh, with an income per person (PPP) of about $8,500, had a life expectancy of 74.7 years, longer than India's 72.0 years at a higher income of about $9,000. These examples show that how a country spends its income, on health, schools, and clean water, can matter as much as how much it earns.

The HDI has critics. It combines three measures into one number, so two countries with the same score can be very different. It does not directly count inequality, safety, or the health of the environment. And it uses averages, which hide differences between rich and poor, men and women, or cities and villages. Most economists use the HDI together with other measures, not by itself.

Key Concepts

Health, Poverty, and Inequality

Two of the most telling signs of development are how long people live and whether children survive their first years. Figure 22.4 shows both for the four World Bank income groups in 2023. A person born in a high-income country could expect to live about 80 years, compared with about 65 years in a low-income country, a gap of about 15 years. The gap in infant mortality, the number of babies who die before their first birthday for every 1,000 born, is even more dramatic: about 4 in a high-income country and about 48 in a low-income country, which is about 11 times higher. Many of those deaths are preventable with clean water, vaccines, and basic health care.

Economists also measure how many people live in extreme poverty, which means having so little income that a person cannot afford basic needs such as food and shelter. The World Bank sets a poverty line, a daily income below which a person is counted as extremely poor. In June 2025, the Bank raised the line from $2.15 to $3.00 per person per day, measured in 2021 PPP dollars, to reflect the changes in prices around the world. By that line, about 838 million people lived in extreme poverty in 2022. The Bank also tracks higher lines used in middle-income countries. About 3.8 billion people lived on less than $8.30 a day (Figure 22.5). The numbers show that, while the number of people in the very worst poverty has fallen over decades, a large share of the world's people still live on very little.

Poverty lines are chosen by people, so they involve judgment. A small change in the line can change the number of people counted as poor by tens of millions, because many people live close to the line. A line set in PPP terms also hides differences in local prices, such as housing, which are not easy to measure. For these reasons, economists look at several lines and at trends over time, not only at one number.

Inequality also matters. Even in countries that have grown quickly, some regions or groups, often rural areas, women, or ethnic minorities, may be left behind. In the United States, too, differences exist between regions: a family in the Central Valley faces different costs and opportunities than a family in Silicon Valley. Development is a process of improving the well-being of everyone, not just the average person.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEBangladesh: Healthier Than Its Income Suggests

Bangladesh is a densely populated country in South Asia. In 2023, its income per person was about $8,500 (PPP), which put it among the lower-middle-income group, yet its life expectancy of 74.7 years was longer than that of India, whose income per person was a little higher. Researchers who study Bangladesh credit several low-cost policies: programs to spread oral rehydration, a simple mixture of water, salt, and sugar that prevents death from diarrhea; widespread vaccination; and the work of community health workers who visit homes.

Bangladesh's experience shows that development is not simply a matter of income. Smart, low-cost investments in health can raise well-being even where incomes are low. It also shows the limits of any single measure: a country can rank in the middle on income and high on health at the same time.

Key Concepts

Productivity: Why Some Workers Produce More

Why does a worker in a rich country earn more than a worker in a poor country? Part of the answer is not that one works harder, but that one produces more in each hour. Economists call this output per worker or per hour productivity. Wages and incomes in the long run follow productivity: when each worker can make more, there is more to share. A Kern County almond grower illustrates the point. Decades ago, a crew would knock almonds from the trees with poles and collect them by hand. Today a machine shakes the tree, another sweeps the nuts into rows, and another picks them up, so a few workers can harvest an orchard that once needed dozens. The workers in the orchard did not become many times harder working. They gained better tools and skills.

Rising productivity does not come from nowhere. Economists point to a small number of ingredients, which they sometimes call the foundations of development. A country's workers need skills and good health. They need tools, buildings, roads, and power. They need better ways of doing things. And they need an environment of rules that rewards work and investment instead of punishing it. The rest of this section looks at each of these four foundations in turn: human capital, physical capital, technology, and institutions. Figure 22.6 shows how they fit together.

None of these ingredients can be built overnight, and each one supports the others. A new factory is useless without trained workers and electricity. A school is less valuable if there are no jobs for graduates. A farmer who learns a better technique has little reason to use it if the land can be taken away. This is why development is a slow process of building many things at once, and why countries that have succeeded have usually made gains on several fronts.

Key Concepts

Human Capital: Investing in People

The skills, knowledge, and health that workers bring to their jobs are called human capital. You are building yours right now: every year of school adds to the knowledge you can use to earn income. The same is true for a nation. Countries with more schooling tend to have higher productivity, and better health lets people work more days and learn more in school.

Figure 22.7 compares schooling in five countries. The data from the UNDP's 2025 report show two measures: mean years of schooling, the average number of years completed by adults aged 25 and older, and expected years of schooling, the number of years that a child entering school today can expect to complete if current enrollment patterns continue. Adults in the United States had completed about 13.9 years of schooling on average, and children today can expect about 15.9. In Mexico, adults had completed 9.3 years, and children can expect 14.5. In India, the numbers were 6.9 and 13.0. In Niger, adults had completed only 1.4 years on average, and children can expect 8.3 years.

Notice that in every country the second bar is taller than the first. This means that younger people are expected to get more schooling than their parents. That is good news for development, because it signals that human capital will grow. But the gap between countries remains large, and a year of schooling in a classroom without books or a trained teacher may teach less than a year in a better school. Economists therefore look at the quality of learning as well as years in school.

Health is also human capital. A child who is sick or malnourished may learn less in school and earn less as an adult. Cheap interventions, such as vaccines, clean water, and school meals, can raise human capital at low cost. At the same time, human capital can leave: when skilled workers such as doctors and engineers emigrate to richer countries, their home countries lose some of what they invested in training them. This is sometimes called the brain drain, although the money those workers send home, which you will study in Section 3, can partly make up for it.

Key Concepts

Physical Capital and Technology

Physical capital is the stock of buildings, machines, tools, roads, ports, and power plants that a country uses to produce goods and services. In Chapter 1, you saw that capital is one of the factors of production. A farmer with a tractor can plow more land than one with a hand tool, and a factory with a reliable electricity supply can run all day. Roads and ports matter as well, because goods that cannot get to market cannot be sold. In many low-income countries, power outages, poor roads, and crowded ports raise the cost of doing business.

Capital must be built by saving and investing: people give up consumption today in order to produce more tomorrow. You will look at this process more closely in Section 3. Economists also point out that capital has diminishing returns. The first tractor on a farm raises output a great deal, but the tenth adds less. This helps explain why poorer countries, which have little capital, can grow rapidly by adding some, and why rich countries must rely more on new ideas to keep growing.

That is where technology comes in. By technology, economists mean the knowledge and methods used to produce goods and services, not only gadgets. A new seed variety, a better way to organize a factory, and a phone app are all technology. A developing country has an advantage here: it does not have to invent everything from scratch. It can adopt methods that richer countries have already developed. The challenge is that adopting technology requires skilled workers, capital, and a business environment that allows firms to compete and change.

A well-known example comes from Kenya. In 2007, a mobile phone company there launched a service called M-Pesa that let people send money using a text message. Millions of Kenyans who had no bank account could suddenly pay bills, send money to relatives, and save. Because Kenya did not have a dense network of bank branches, mobile phones allowed it to "leapfrog" the older technology. Similar services are now used in many developing countries. The story shows that technology can help overcome a shortage of physical capital, but it also needs a supportive set of rules and trusted institutions.

Key Concepts

Institutions: The Rules of the Game

Imagine you are a farmer who wants to plant a pistachio orchard, which takes years before the trees produce a harvest. You will plant only if you are confident that you, and not someone else, will own the harvest. You will also want to be able to borrow against your land and to enforce contracts with the people who will buy your nuts. These conditions depend on institutions, the rules, laws, and organizations that shape how people deal with one another. Economists, including Nobel Prize winner Douglass North, have argued that good institutions are a basic reason why some economies grow and others do not.

Three institutions matter most. The first is property rights, the legal right to own and use resources and to keep the rewards. In Chapter 3 you saw that secure property rights give owners an incentive to conserve and improve what they own. A farmer who owns the land has a reason to protect the soil and to invest in irrigation. If property can be taken at any time by officials or powerful people, owners may not invest. The second is the rule of law: the principle that laws apply equally to everyone and are enforced fairly, including laws against fraud and theft. The third is honest and stable government. Where officials demand bribes, where rules change suddenly, or where conflict makes planning impossible, firms hold back, and talented people may leave.

Some economists argue that the poorest countries lack even the basics: many families in developing countries live on land they do not hold a legal title to, which means that they cannot sell it, pass it on with certainty, or use it to borrow. Others point out that titles alone do not guarantee results, and that culture, history, and geography also play a part. The debate is not settled. Still, the evidence supports a modest conclusion: investment in machines, schools, or roads tends to pay off less where owners cannot count on keeping the results.

Institutions are the hardest foundation to build. A government can build a road in a few years, but a reputation for fair courts and honest officials may take decades. They also involve political choices and trade-offs. For example, a government that is too weak cannot protect property, while one that is too strong can take it. The Chapter 2 discussion of economic freedom and the role of government helps explain why economists pay so much attention to getting this balance right.

Key Concepts

Openness to Trade

The fifth ingredient that many economists add is openness to the world economy. In Chapter 21, you learned that countries gain from specialization and trade. A small economy that makes only for its own market cannot take advantage of large-scale production, and it must produce for itself everything it needs, even things that others make more cheaply. By exporting what it makes well and importing the rest, it can use its resources better. Trade also brings new ideas and technology: a company that sells in foreign markets must keep up with foreign competitors and can learn from them.

Several East Asian economies, including South Korea, Taiwan, and later China and Vietnam, grew quickly after they began selling manufactured goods to the world. In Section 4 and in the Case Study, you will see how Vietnam's exports grew from a small share of its economy in the mid-1980s to more than 90 percent of GDP in 2024. Latin American countries tried another path for decades, as Chapter 21 described: after the Great Depression, many governments used high tariffs to protect their own factories (import substitution). The results were mixed, and many countries lowered their barriers in the 1980s and 1990s.

Economists do not all agree on how fast or how far to open. Critics note that trade can hurt workers and firms that face new competition, that countries which depend on exports of a few raw materials are exposed when prices fall, and that rich countries have sometimes kept their own markets closed to products that poor countries make well, such as farm goods and textiles. Supporters respond that the countries that have grown fastest are mostly those that traded more, and that closing a market usually hurts the poor consumers who pay higher prices. Most economists today take a middle view: that openness helps when it is accompanied by the other foundations, such as schools and strong institutions, which help workers and firms adjust.

Figure 22.8 shows one result of these forces: large differences in income per person. In PPP terms, income per person in 2023 ranged from about $112,700 in Norway and $73,650 in the United States to about $13,000 in Vietnam, about $5,600 in Kenya, and about $1,600 in Niger. Closing a gap that big takes decades of steady progress on all the foundations at once.

Key Concepts

Saving, Investment, and the Poverty Trap

Section 2 explained that development requires building capital: factories, roads, schools, and clinics. But building capital costs money, and money must come from somewhere. In Chapter 14 and Chapter 15 you learned that banks and financial markets connect savers with borrowers. A saver, such as a family that puts $1,000 in a bank, supplies funds. A borrower, such as a farmer who wants to buy a tractor, uses those funds to invest. When a country invests more, it can build more capital, which raises productivity and income, which allows even more saving. This is a cycle of growth.

The same cycle can run the other way. When incomes are very low, families must spend nearly all of their income on food and shelter, leaving little to save. Little saving means little investment, and little investment means low productivity and low income. Economists call this situation a poverty trap: poor countries are poor partly because they cannot afford to invest, and they cannot afford to invest because they are poor. A country can escape the trap in several ways, which are the subject of this section: by drawing on foreign sources of funds, by building a reliable banking system, or by growing quickly enough that saving rises.

Financing also requires a financial system that people trust. If people fear that banks will fail or that their savings will be seized, they may hold cash, buy gold, or send money abroad instead of putting it in a bank where it can be lent. In a country with weak institutions, even a high saving rate may not lead to investment. This is one reason the institutions you studied in Section 2 matter for financing as well.

Key Concepts

Foreign Investment and Foreign Aid

One way to find funds is to attract them from abroad. Foreign direct investment (FDI) occurs when a company in one country builds a factory, buys a company, or sets up a business in another country. A company that opens a plant in Mexico to make auto parts for the U.S. market, or a firm that builds a mobile-phone factory in Vietnam, is making a foreign direct investment. FDI brings capital, but also new technology, training, and links to foreign markets. Supporters say that it creates jobs and helps local firms learn better methods. Critics worry that foreign firms may send profits home, take over local businesses, or move away when conditions change, and that governments may compete by offering low taxes or weak regulations.

Governments and international organizations also provide funds. Foreign aid, which the OECD calls official development assistance (ODA), is money and other help given by governments to developing countries, either as gifts (grants) or as loans with very low interest rates. In 2025, the world's main donor countries gave about $174 billion in ODA, which was about 0.26 percent of their combined national income, according to the OECD's preliminary figures. That was 23 percent less than in 2024, after adjusting for prices, and the largest one-year drop on record. The United States remained the largest single donor at about $29 billion, but its giving fell by almost 57 percent in real terms.

Does aid work? Economists disagree. Some, such as Jeffrey Sachs, argue that a big, well-targeted push of aid for health, farming, and infrastructure can help the poorest countries break out of the poverty trap. Others, such as William Easterly, argue that decades of aid have often failed because it was poorly designed, because money was diverted by corrupt officials, or because aid agencies do not face the accountability that businesses face in a market. Most researchers find that the results depend on the type of aid and the circumstances: aid for specific tasks such as vaccination has a good record, while aid given to governments with weak institutions has a more mixed one. In addition, aid in the form of food or goods can compete with local farmers, and aid that comes with strings, such as a requirement to buy goods from the donor country, is less valuable to the recipient than the headline number suggests.

The debate also involves values. Some people believe that wealthy countries have a moral duty to help; others believe that aid is the proper job of private charity and not government, or that the money would be better spent at home. As a student of economics, you can weigh the evidence while recognizing that the final choice rests on values as well.

Key Concepts

Debt and the International Financial Institutions

Governments and firms in developing countries can also borrow. The total amount a country owes to lenders outside the country is its external debt. Borrowing for a useful project, such as a port or a power plant, can pay for itself if the project raises income enough to cover the interest. But when borrowed money is wasted, or when interest rates rise or the value of the country's currency falls, a country can find itself spending so much on repayment that little is left for schools and clinics. Chapter 18 explained how governments manage debt at home. A country with large foreign debts faces an additional problem, because it must earn foreign currency, usually by exporting, to repay.

Two international organizations play central roles. Both were created at a conference in Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, in 1944, as World War II was ending. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) lends to member countries that face financial crises, such as a shortage of foreign currency, and helps to promote stable exchange rates and international payments. The World Bank makes loans and grants for specific development projects, such as roads, schools, and clinics, and gathers much of the data you have used in this chapter. Wealthy countries provide most of their funds and have the most votes.

Both organizations are controversial. Supporters say that the IMF helps countries avoid collapse and the World Bank funds projects that private lenders would not. Critics say that the IMF often requires countries to cut government spending and raise taxes as a condition of a loan, which can be painful for the poor in the short run, and that international institutions give too little voice to the poorest countries. Defenders reply that without conditions, loans might not be repaid or might be used badly. In some cases, lenders have agreed to cancel or reduce a poor country's debt (called debt relief) so that it can spend on health and schools. Whether that approach is wise, or whether it encourages future borrowing, is part of the debate.

Key Concepts

Remittances: Money from Workers Abroad

One of the largest flows of money to developing countries does not come from governments at all. A remittance is money that a worker sends home to family members in another country. Millions of people leave their home countries to work abroad, many in the United States, the Persian Gulf, and Europe, and send part of their paychecks to relatives. According to the World Bank, remittances to low- and middle-income countries were about $685 billion in 2024, which was larger than the total of foreign direct investment and official development assistance to those countries combined.

Figure 22.10 shows the five largest recipients. India received about $129 billion, the largest amount in the world. Mexico received about $68 billion, China about $48 billion, the Philippines about $40 billion, and Pakistan about $33 billion. In Kern County, many families have relatives in Mexico, Central America, or other countries, and some regularly send money to them. That money pays for food, medicine, school fees, and sometimes a small business or a house.

Remittances help families directly, and they come in even when a country's own economy is weak. But they have limits. They depend on people leaving, which can drain a country of its workers, as Section 2 noted. They are subject to costs such as fees charged by money-transfer companies, and to changes in immigration and labor laws in the countries where migrants work. And some economists note that families may depend on money from abroad instead of building local businesses. The topic is also politically debated in the United States, where people disagree about immigration policy. Economists can describe the flows, but the policy choices depend on values as well as facts.

Key Concepts

Microfinance and the Economics Pacesetter

Most poor families cannot borrow from a bank. A bank wants collateral, such as land or a house, which poor families lack, and it finds that small loans cost too much to make. As a result, poor people may turn to moneylenders who charge very high interest, or they cannot borrow at all and must pass up opportunities such as buying a sewing machine or a few goats. Microfinance is the provision of very small loans, and often savings accounts and insurance, to low-income people who are shut out of the banking system. The loans are often as small as $50 to a few hundred dollars. Because borrowers have few assets to pledge, microfinance organizations often lend to groups, whose members encourage each other to repay.

Microfinance has spread to many countries, and many lenders focus on women, who in many societies have fewer chances to borrow and who often spend extra income on children's food and schooling. The idea is that a small loan can help a person earn income, repay the loan, and start again, building a record of reliability and the habit of saving.

How well does it work? Studies of microfinance have shown mixed results. Several careful experiments in which some communities were given access to microloans and others were not found that borrowers did start or expand businesses, but average incomes did not change dramatically, at least in the first years. Some borrowers have faced heavy debts when businesses failed, and critics have pointed to interest rates that can be high once costs are counted. Supporters respond that microfinance gives poor families more control, that it is one tool among many, and that it works better for some borrowers than others. Economists generally agree that credit helps, but that it is not a cure for poverty by itself.

Muhammad Yunus was born in 1940 in Chittagong, in what is now Bangladesh. He studied economics, earned a doctorate in the United States, and became a professor of economics in Bangladesh. In the 1970s, after a famine hit the country, he became troubled that the theories he taught seemed far removed from the lives of the poor people living near his university.

Yunus began making very small loans to villagers, many of them women who made baskets or other goods, and found that they repaid the loans reliably. He argued that the poor could be good borrowers if lenders designed loans to fit their needs, for example by lending to groups whose members supported one another. In 1983, his project became the Grameen Bank, which means "village bank" in Bengali. Microfinance has also had critics and has shown mixed results in careful studies, as you have read. But Yunus changed the way many economists and lenders think about poor borrowers. Instead of assuming that the poor cannot be lent to, microfinance showed that a market can reach people whom banks had ignored.

Key Concepts

From Plan to Market

In Chapter 2, you learned that in a command economy, government planners decide what to produce, how to produce it, and who gets it, and that many such economies ran into shortages, weak incentives, and slow growth. By the late 1980s, many of these governments had lost faith in central planning. The Soviet Union broke up in 1991, and the countries of Eastern Europe and the former Soviet Union set out to build market economies. China and Vietnam, which stayed under single-party rule, also began to move toward markets, in 1978 and 1986. A country that is moving from central planning toward a market economy is called a transition economy.

What does such a change require? Economists point to three big tasks. First, prices must be freed. Under central planning, prices were set by officials and did not reflect scarcity, so nothing signaled what people wanted. To free prices is to allow supply and demand to set them (Chapter 6). Second, ownership must change. Most factories, shops, and farms belonged to the state. Privatization is the sale or transfer of state-owned businesses to private owners, which gives owners the incentive to cut costs and improve products. Third, market institutions must be built: property rights, contracts, courts, banks, a tax system, and rules that allow new firms to open and failing firms to close. A market economy without these institutions can produce confusion, corruption, and fraud.

The transition is painful. When prices are freed, they often jump, because shortages that had been hidden by price controls are suddenly visible. Many state-owned enterprises (SOEs), factories and firms owned by the government, which had been protected from competition, turn out to be unable to compete, so workers lose jobs before new firms can hire them. Some people become very wealthy by buying state assets cheaply, and many ordinary families feel left behind. These costs help explain why governments disagree about how quickly to change, as the next part shows.

Key Concepts

Two Paths: Shock Therapy and Gradual Reform

Governments choose different speeds. Shock therapy is a strategy of making many market reforms at once: freeing prices, privatizing quickly, and opening to trade, all in a short time. Its supporters argue that half-measures leave the economy stuck between two systems and give officials time to block reform, so it is better to move fast and accept a short period of pain. Gradual reform is a strategy of changing the system step by step, often by testing reforms in a small area before spreading them. Its supporters argue that moving slowly gives people time to adjust and lets the government fix mistakes before they become national disasters.

Poland is the best-known example of shock therapy. In January 1990, shortly after the fall of its communist government, Poland freed most prices, cut government subsidies, allowed its currency to be traded, and began privatizing businesses. Prices jumped and output fell sharply, and many people lost their jobs. But within a few years, Poland's economy began to grow, and it has grown most years since. Poland joined the European Union in 2004. In Figure 22.13, you can see how income per person in Poland, adjusted for prices and measured in 2021 international dollars, rose from about $12,800 in 1990 to about $45,200 in 2024.

Russia also moved quickly in the early 1990s, freeing prices and privatizing many businesses, including by giving citizens vouchers that could be exchanged for shares. But the results were far more painful. Russia's income per person, as measured by the World Bank, fell from about $29,600 in 1990 to about $18,400 in 1995, a drop of about 38 percent, before recovering to about $41,900 by 2024. Many economists argue that Russia moved quickly to privatize without first building the institutions that markets need: courts that enforce contracts, honest regulators, and open competition. A small number of people bought valuable assets cheaply and became very wealthy, and many families lost their savings to inflation. Other economists point out that Russia's situation was very different from Poland's: the Soviet Union had collapsed, supply chains between republics broke down, and the country faced political upheaval. Because so many things were different, Poland and Russia are not a controlled experiment, and economists still disagree about how much the speed of reform mattered compared with institutions and politics.

China chose the gradual path. Beginning in 1978, its leaders first allowed farm families to keep and sell part of what they produced above a required quota, which raised farm output. They then permitted small private businesses and village enterprises, while many large state-owned enterprises, businesses owned by the government, remained under state control. And they tried reforms in limited areas first.

Key Concepts

China's Experiment: Special Economic Zones

China's most famous experiment was the special economic zone (SEZ), a geographic area where the government allows different, more market-friendly rules, such as lower taxes, fewer restrictions on foreign investors, and simpler customs rules, than in the rest of the country. In 1980, China designated several zones on its southern coast, including Shenzhen, a small fishing town next to Hong Kong. Foreign companies and Chinese entrepreneurs built factories there to make clothing, toys, and electronics for export. Workers moved from the countryside to take the jobs, and the experiment's success led China to open other coastal cities and eventually much of the country.

The change was enormous. Today, Shenzhen is a city of more than 17 million people, with skyscrapers, technology companies, and one of the world's busiest ports. Across China, GDP per person (adjusted for prices) rose from about $1,700 in 1990 to about $23,800 in 2024, an increase of about 14 times. According to the World Bank, China's economy grew by more than 9 percent a year on average from 1978 on, and almost 800 million people were lifted out of extreme poverty. In 2024, about 15 percent of Chinese lived on less than $8.30 a day, the line used in upper-middle-income countries.

China's path remains debated. Supporters credit its pragmatic, step-by-step approach, its investments in infrastructure and education, and its willingness to trade with the world. Critics point out that China's government has kept tight control over politics and parts of the economy, that large state-owned firms and government-directed credit sometimes lead to waste and heavy debt, that income inequality and pollution have increased, and that China's export success has been controversial among its trading partners, as the tariff debates in Chapter 21 showed. Chapter 2 noted that China's economic freedom score (48.3) is much lower than the United States' (72.8). The country's experience shows that rapid growth is possible under a system that is neither a pure command economy nor a pure market economy, but many economists caution that its path may be difficult for other countries to copy.

Key Concepts

What Can We Learn?

Economists do not agree about a single recipe, but a few lessons appear in most accounts. First, institutions matter. Reforms that free prices and privatize businesses work best when courts, banks, and fair rules are in place. Second, incentives matter. When people can keep the rewards of their work, as Chinese farmers could after 1978, output tends to rise. Third, openness to trade and investment has helped most of the success stories, including Poland, China, and Vietnam. Fourth, the costs of change are real, and they fall on particular people: workers in old factories, families whose savings were wiped out by inflation, and regions that do not benefit as quickly. Governments that help people adjust, with training, unemployment benefits, and a safety net, may find that reforms last longer.

At the same time, no one should draw a simple conclusion from a handful of cases. Countries differ in their history, geography, population, and politics. Strong growth does not by itself bring political freedom, and some countries with rapid growth have faced new problems such as pollution, inequality, and high debt. A fair student of economics asks how much evidence there is, who gains and loses, and which parts of the debate rest on facts and which rest on values. In the Case Study that follows, you will apply those questions to Vietnam, a country that has changed its economy in ways that combine features of both paths.

CASE STUDY

Vietnam's Doi Moi:
Renovating an Economy

In the 1980s, Vietnam was one of the poorest countries in the world, with a planned economy, food shortages, and few trading partners. Today it is a middle-income country with factories that make electronics and clothing for the world. What changed, and what can other countries learn from it? Studying the evidence also shows why economists are careful about claiming too much.

A Planned Economy Changes Course

After decades of war, Vietnam was reunified under a communist government in 1975, and the new government extended central planning across the whole country. The state controlled most prices, farms were organized into cooperatives, and private business was restricted. By the mid-1980s, the economy was in trouble: output was growing slowly, food was short in some regions, and prices were rising quickly. Vietnam also had little trade with countries outside the Soviet bloc.

In December 1986, at the Sixth Congress of the Communist Party, Vietnam's leaders announced a program of reforms called Doi Moi, which means "renovation." Over the next years, the government allowed farm families to use land more independently and to sell their crops, loosened price controls, permitted private businesses, and welcomed foreign investors. It kept one-party rule and kept many state-owned enterprises. Vietnam's approach looks like what you read about in Section 4 as gradual reform, although it did not follow China's example in every detail.

The results were striking. According to the World Bank, income per person in Vietnam, adjusted for prices, rose from about $2,500 in 1990 to about $14,400 in 2024. The share of the population living on less than $3.00 a day fell from 57.5 percent in 1992 to 1.6 percent in 2022. Vietnam's exports grew from a small part of its economy to more than 90 percent of its GDP. Vietnam became a major supplier of clothing, shoes, furniture, and electronics, and the country's farmers also became leading exporters of coffee, rice, and seafood.

DOCUMENT D · COMPOSITE VIEWPOINTSThree Ways to Judge Vietnam's Path

These viewpoints were written for this textbook. Each combines arguments commonly made by farmers, workers, and economists; they are not quotations from real individuals.

THE DELTA FARMER'S VIEW
"My parents farmed for the cooperative. Today my family decides what to plant and sells to buyers who pay by the weight. We can send our children to school. But when the world price of rice or coffee falls, our income falls too, and we cannot control that."
THE FACTORY WORKER'S VIEW
"My job at a factory pays more than farming did. I send money to my family. But the hours are long, the rent in the city is high, and I worry that if orders slow down, the company will move to another country."
THE ECONOMIST'S VIEW
"The data show a huge fall in poverty after Doi Moi. But several things changed at once: reforms, trade, foreign investment, and a young workforce. We cannot be sure which mattered most, and other countries with similar policies have not done as well. Inequality, pollution, and dependence on exports are real concerns."

CHAPTER22

Chapter 22 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Defining and Measuring Development
Economic development is a sustained improvement in income, health, education, and opportunity, not only growth in output. Economists measure it with GNI per capita (and purchasing power parity), the Human Development Index, life expectancy, infant mortality, and poverty lines, and each measure has limits.
Section 2 · Foundations for Development
Higher productivity raises incomes. It rests on human capital, physical capital, technology, and institutions such as property rights and the rule of law. Openness to trade helps most when the other foundations are present, though economists disagree about how fast to open.
Section 3 · Financing Development
Investment requires saving and a trusted financial system. Developing countries draw on domestic saving, foreign direct investment, foreign aid, loans, and remittances. The IMF and the World Bank lend and advise, and microfinance reaches poor borrowers. Each source has benefits and limits.
Section 4 · Transition to Market Economies
Moving from central planning to markets means freeing prices, changing ownership, and building institutions. Poland used shock therapy, China used gradual reform and special economic zones, and Russia's experience was painful. The evidence does not show a single best path.
