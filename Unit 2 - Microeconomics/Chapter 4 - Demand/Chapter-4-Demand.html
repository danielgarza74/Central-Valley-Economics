Concept Review

In Chapter 1 you learned that scarcity forces choices, and that people respond to incentives. In Chapter 3 you saw that a market economy runs on voluntary exchange and competition. A market has two sides: the people who want to buy and the people who want to sell. This chapter takes up the buying side. Chapter 5 will take up the selling side, and Chapter 6 will put the two together to explain how prices are set.

Chapter 4 Key Concept

Demand is the willingness to buy a good or service and the ability to pay for it. The law of demand says that, other things equal, when the price of a good rises, buyers purchase less of it, and when the price falls, they purchase more. Demand changes when something other than price changes, such as income, tastes, or the price of a related good. Elasticity measures how strongly the quantity people buy responds to a change in price.

Why the Concept Matters

Suppose the taco truck outside Shafter High School raises the price of a carne asada taco from $2.00 to $4.00. What happens? A few loyal customers pay the higher price. Some switch to the bean burrito, which did not go up. Some bring lunch from home. Others walk to a different truck. Now suppose that, instead, the price of gasoline jumps by the same percentage. Most drivers who have a long commute to Bakersfield cannot simply stop driving, so they buy nearly as much as before and complain about it. The same price change produces very different reactions, and the businesses that sell tacos and the businesses that sell gasoline have to understand why. By the end of this chapter you will be able to draw a demand curve, explain what moves along it and what shifts it, and use elasticity to predict how buyers will react.

Key Concepts

Demand: Willingness and Ability

Think about the last thing you bought with your own money. It might have been a burrito, a phone case, or a tank of gas. At some point you looked at the price and decided that the item was worth it. Every one of the millions of purchases made in Kern County each day follows the same pattern, and economists study those decisions as a group. The study of the choices made by individual consumers, workers, and businesses is called microeconomics. In this unit you will learn the microeconomics of markets, beginning with the buying side.

In everyday speech, people say they “demand” a lot of things. A teenager may say, “I demand a new truck.” To an economist, that statement means very little by itself. Demand has a specific meaning: it is the willingness to buy a good or service and the ability to pay for it. A buyer must meet both requirements. A student who wants a $40,000 truck and has $300 in savings is willing but unable. A student who has $300 and would never spend it on a truck is able but unwilling. Neither one adds to the demand for trucks. Only a person who both wants the truck and can pay for it takes part in demand.

The idea becomes clearer if you think about a single price at a single moment. Demand is not a vague wish to have something. It is a plan to buy that depends on the price. Ask a hundred shoppers at a Bakersfield grocery store whether they would buy strawberries at $2 a basket, and many will say yes. Ask the same people at $9 a basket, and most will say no. The people did not change. The price did. Economists want to know exactly how the plans of buyers change as the price changes, and that question leads to the law of demand.

Notice also that demand is always about a specific period of time and a specific market. The demand for tacos in Shafter on a Friday at lunch is different from the demand for tacos in Shafter in the middle of the night or the demand for tacos in all of California for a year. Whenever you describe demand, you should be able to answer the questions for what product, where, and during what time?

Key Concepts

Ability to Pay: Income Sets the Limit

Of the two requirements for demand, ability is the easier one to measure. A buyer's ability to pay depends mostly on income, and the Census Bureau estimates it for every county in the country. Figure 4.2 shows the median household income in Kern County, California, and the United States in 2019 and 2024. The median is the middle value: half of the households in an area earn more than the median, and half earn less.

In 2024, the median household in Kern County had an income of $71,295. That was about 71 percent of the California median ($100,130) and about 87 percent of the national median ($81,604). Kern County's median had grown quickly, from $53,245 in 2019, an increase of about 34 percent. These figures are not adjusted for inflation, so part of the increase only made up for rising prices. Even so, the gap between Kern County and the state as a whole shows why the same product can face a different demand in different places. A restaurant that charges $18 for a lunch plate in a wealthy coastal city may find that far fewer households in the southern San Joaquin Valley are able to buy it at that price.

Income is not the only part of ability. Savings, credit, and the prices of the other things a family must buy, such as rent, utilities, and gasoline, all affect how much is left to spend. Economists use the term purchasing power for what a given amount of money can actually buy. When prices rise faster than incomes, purchasing power falls, and demand falls with it, even if a family's wishes have not changed at all. That is one reason that economists pay so much attention to both prices and incomes at the same time.

Key Concepts

The Law of Demand

When prices go up, people tend to buy less. When prices go down, they tend to buy more. You have seen this at every sale: a store marks shoes down by half, and the line at the register grows. Economists call this pattern the law of demand: when the price of a good rises, the quantity demanded falls, and when the price falls, the quantity demanded rises, other things being equal.

The words “other things being equal” are important enough to have a Latin name, ceteris paribus. Many forces can change what people buy. If you want to know what a change in price does to purchases, you have to imagine that everything else, such as income, tastes, and the prices of other goods, stays the same. Economists use ceteris paribus the way scientists use a controlled experiment: they change one thing at a time.

The law also depends on a careful use of words. The quantity demanded is the amount of a good or service that buyers plan to purchase at one particular price. When the price changes, the quantity demanded changes. That is a movement from one point to another on the same demand curve. The word demand by itself describes the whole relationship between prices and quantities. Keep the two terms apart. In Section 2 you will see that demand itself can increase or decrease, and that is a different event from a change in the quantity demanded.

The Demand Schedule and the Demand Curve

To show the law of demand clearly, economists use two tools. The first is a demand schedule: a table that lists the quantity demanded at each of several prices. The second is a demand curve: a graph that shows the same information, with price on the vertical axis and quantity demanded on the horizontal axis.

Figure 4.3 shows an example. It is a hypothetical demand schedule for carne asada tacos at a taco truck outside a Shafter school at lunchtime. At $1.50 per taco, students and workers would buy 120 tacos in a day. At $2.00, they would buy 100. As the price rises in steps of 50 cents, the quantity falls by 20 tacos each time, until at $4.00 only 20 tacos are sold. Each pair of numbers in the table becomes a point on the graph, and joining the points gives a line that slopes down from left to right. A downward slope is the picture of the law of demand.

Reading a demand curve takes practice. Start with a price on the vertical axis, move across to the curve, and then drop down to read the quantity. At $3.00, for instance, you find the point labeled D and read down to 60 tacos. You can also read the other way: if the truck wants to sell 80 tacos, the demand curve says it must charge $2.50. The curve answers two kinds of questions: how many will buyers purchase at a given price, and what is the highest price at which a given amount can be sold.

Reading the Curve as Willingness to Pay

There is a second way to read a demand curve. Instead of asking how many tacos buyers will purchase at a given price, ask what buyers are willing to pay for a given quantity. The highest price that a buyer will pay for one more unit is the buyer's willingness to pay. In Figure 4.3, 20 tacos are demanded even at $4.00, so at least 20 buyers are willing to pay that much for a taco. By the time the truck sells its 100th taco, the price has to be down to $2.00, which tells you that the 100th buyer values a taco at about $2.00.

This reading connects to Chapter 1. There you learned that a rational decision maker compares marginal benefit with marginal cost. For a buyer, the price is the marginal cost of one more unit, and willingness to pay is a measure of its marginal benefit. A buyer purchases a unit when the willingness to pay is at least as high as the price. That is why a lower price brings in more purchases: at every lower price, there are more units whose benefit to some buyer is above the price.

Each point on the demand curve therefore has two meanings. Read from price to quantity, it says how much will be bought at that price. Read from quantity to price, it says what the last unit bought is worth to buyers. Economists use the second reading to study how much buyers gain from trading, an idea that will return when you study how prices are set in Chapter 6.

Key Concepts

Why the Demand Curve Slopes Downward

Why do buyers behave this way? Economists have found three explanations that work together.

The substitution effect. When the price of a good rises, buyers look for something similar that costs less. Economists call this the substitution effect. If the price of carne asada goes up and the price of chicken stays the same, many buyers switch to chicken tacos. The more substitutes a good has, the more easily buyers can walk away from it.

The income effect. A higher price also makes buyers a little poorer, because the same paycheck now buys less. The income effect is the change in the quantity people demand because a price change alters their purchasing power. If gasoline rises from $4 to $5 a gallon, a family that drives a lot has to spend more of its income on fuel. That leaves less money for other things, and the family may cut back on all of them, including fuel.

Diminishing marginal utility. In Chapter 1 you learned that decision makers compare marginal benefit to marginal cost. Economists use the word utility for the satisfaction a person receives from a good or service. The first slice of pizza at lunch may bring great satisfaction. The second brings less, and the fourth much less. This pattern is called diminishing marginal utility: each additional unit of a good that a person consumes brings less additional satisfaction than the one before. Because extra units are worth less, buyers will pay for them only if the price is lower. That is why people buy more when prices fall.

EXAMPLEThe Price of Carne Asada Rises

Suppose a drought and a shortage of cattle push the price of carne asada up by 30 percent at taquerías across Bakersfield. A taquería's menu looks like this: carne asada taco, $3.00; pollo taco, $2.50; al pastor taco, $2.75. After the increase, the carne asada taco costs $3.90.

Many customers who always ordered carne asada notice that pollo is now $1.40 cheaper. Some switch (the substitution effect). Others still order carne asada but take two tacos instead of three because the lunch now takes a bigger bite out of their pay (the income effect). Others skip the third taco because a third taco is not worth as much to them as the first two (diminishing marginal utility). All three responses reduce the quantity of carne asada tacos demanded.

Diminishing Marginal Utility in Dollars

A table makes the idea of diminishing marginal utility concrete. Figure 4.4 shows, for a hypothetical student, the most she would pay for each additional slice of pizza at lunch. The first slice is worth $5.00 to her, because she is hungry. The second is worth $3.50, the third $2.40, the fourth $1.20, and the fifth only $0.40. Each slice adds less satisfaction than the one before it, so each is worth fewer dollars.

Now suppose a pizzeria near campus sells slices for $2.50. She buys the first slice, which is worth $5.00 to her, and the second, which is worth $3.50. She stops at two, because the third slice is worth only $2.40, which is less than the $2.50 price. If the price falls to $1.50, she buys three slices. At $1.00 she buys four. At $0.50 she still buys only four, because the fifth slice is worth $0.40. Plot those prices and quantities and you get a downward-sloping demand curve. A demand curve is, in this way, built out of diminishing marginal utility.

The same logic applies to every good. The first gallon of water is extremely valuable to someone who is thirsty, and the thousandth gallon, used to wash a car, is worth very little. The price that buyers will pay depends on what the last unit is worth to them, not on what the first unit is worth. That gap helps explain why water is cheap even though life depends on it: because there is so much of it, the last gallon that most people buy is not worth much.

Key Concepts

From Individual Demand to Market Demand

So far we have looked at a group of buyers as if they were one. In real markets, every buyer has an individual demand. Individual demand is the demand of one person or one household. The demand of everyone who buys a product in a market added together is the market demand. To find it, you add the quantities that all buyers demand at each price.

Figure 4.5 shows how this works with three students: Ana, Ben, and Carla. At a price of $4 per taco, Ana would buy 4 tacos, Ben 6, and Carla 8, so the market quantity demanded (for these three people) is 18 tacos. Because we add quantities at each price, we add the curves horizontally. The market demand curve lies to the right of each individual curve, and it also slopes downward. When more buyers enter a market, market demand grows. That helps explain why a taco truck near a school or a big employer can do far more business than the same truck in a quiet neighborhood.

QUICK REFERENCEMarket demand is the total quantity demanded by all buyers in a market at each price. It is the sum of all individual demands. Spanish: demanda del mercado

Key Concepts

Demand for One Seller and Demand for the Whole Market

The market demand curve in Figure 4.5 includes every buyer in the market. A single seller, however, does not face the demand of the whole market. It faces only the buyers who choose to buy from it. Suppose five taco trucks serve the lunch crowd outside a Bakersfield warehouse, and all of them sell nearly identical tacos for $3.00. If one truck raises its price to $4.00, it does not lose just a few customers. It may lose most of them, because the other four trucks are close substitutes. The demand for one truck's tacos is much more sensitive to price than the market demand for tacos.

For that reason, economists are careful about how they define “the market.” The broader the definition, the less sensitive demand tends to be. The demand for all lunch food near the warehouse changes little when one truck changes its price, because workers still need to eat. The demand for one truck's carne asada plate can change a great deal. Section 3 shows how to measure this difference with a number called elasticity.

A second point is about how the curves are added. When you build a market demand curve, you add quantities at each price. You never add prices. If Ana would buy 4 tacos at $4.00 and Ben would buy 6, together they would buy 10 tacos at $4.00, not “$8.00 worth” of anything. A student who adds prices instead of quantities draws a curve that does not describe any real buyers.

Key Concepts

Ceteris Paribus in the Real World

The law of demand describes what happens when only the price changes. In the real world, many things change at once, so data do not always look like a clean downward-sloping line. Figure 4.6 shows real data for gasoline in California. To compare a price in dollars with a quantity in gallons, the figure converts both to an index in which the value for 2019 equals 100. Anything above 100 is above the 2019 level, and anything below 100 is below it.

Look at 2019 and 2020. The price of regular gasoline in California fell from $3.60 to $3.05, a decline of about 15 percent. The law of demand predicts that people would buy more. Instead, the quantity of gasoline sold fell by about 18 percent. Did the law of demand fail? No. In 2020, the COVID-19 pandemic closed schools and offices and canceled trips, so people wanted to drive far less at every price. Something other than price changed, and the effect of that change was larger than the effect of the lower price. Economists describe this as a change in demand, not a change in quantity demanded. You will study such changes in Section 2.

This example teaches an important lesson about reading data. A graph with price and quantity on it cannot, by itself, show the law of demand. To test the law, you must hold the other influences constant, or at least account for them.

Key Concepts

A Change in Quantity Demanded Versus a Change in Demand

Section 1 closed with a puzzle. Why did gasoline purchases in California fall in 2020 when the price of gasoline also fell? The answer is that two different things can change in a market, and economists use two different terms to describe them. Keeping them straight is the most important skill in this chapter.

A change in the price of the product itself causes a change in the quantity demanded. On a graph, it is a movement along one demand curve, from one point to another point on the same line. The curve does not move. If the price of a taco rises from $2.00 to $3.00, you slide up the curve from 100 tacos to 60 tacos.

A change in anything other than the product's own price causes a change in demand. The whole relationship between price and quantity is different. On a graph, the entire curve shifts. An increase in demand shifts the curve to the right, so that buyers demand more at every price. A decrease in demand shifts the curve to the left, so that buyers demand less at every price.

Figure 4.7 shows the idea with the taco truck. D1 is the original demand. When more people want tacos at every price (maybe because a new business opened nearby), demand shifts right to D2. At the old price of $2.70, the quantity demanded rises from 70 to 100 tacos. When fewer people want tacos at every price, demand shifts left to D3. As you read this section, ask yourself each time: did the price change, or did something else change?

Key Concepts

Consumer Income

The first determinant of demand is consumer income. When people earn more, they can afford more, and for most goods, demand shifts right. Economists call a good whose demand increases when income increases a normal good. Restaurant meals, vacations, and new clothes are examples. When income falls, demand for normal goods falls too. A family whose parent is laid off from a packing house in the winter may cut back on dinners out almost immediately.

A few goods behave the opposite way. An inferior good is one whose demand decreases when income increases. Here inferior does not mean poor quality. It means that people buy less of it when they can afford better alternatives. Instant noodles, generic-brand cereals, and used bus passes are common examples. As a household's income rises, it may shift away from instant noodles toward restaurant meals, as in Figure 4.9. For a household with a very low income, the opposite shift happens during a downturn: demand for inferior goods rises because families trade down.

Whether a good is normal or inferior depends on the buyer and the circumstances. A used car is a normal good for a teenager who has no car, but it might be inferior for a family that now earns enough to buy a new one. It is a useful reminder that the labels describe a relationship between income and demand, not a property of the product itself.

Key Concepts

How Income Changes Demand for Different Goods

Not every normal good responds to a change in income in the same way. When a household's income rises by 10 percent, it may raise its spending on vacations and new cars by much more than 10 percent, while its spending on flour and electricity rises by much less. A luxury good is a good that people buy a great deal more of when their incomes rise, such as restaurant meals, travel, and jewelry. Goods that people buy only a little more of when incomes rise, such as basic groceries, are called necessities. The shift in demand that follows a raise therefore differs from product to product.

Look back at Figure 4.2. Kern County's median household income rose by about 34 percent between 2019 and 2024. Other things equal, economists would predict a rightward shift in demand for normal goods in the county and a leftward shift in demand for inferior goods. But other things did not stay equal. Prices rose over the same years, so the gain in what the money could buy was smaller than the gain in dollars. This is why economists look at income and prices together.

Income can also change who is in the market. A family that moves from a low income to a middle income may begin to buy goods that were out of reach before, such as a first car, a first home, or dental work that had been put off. For goods like these, the change in demand can come in steps: below a certain income the family is not able to buy, and above it the family is. The ability requirement from Section 1 is doing the work.

Key Concepts

Tastes and Expectations

The second determinant is tastes and preferences. These are buyers' likes and dislikes, shaped by culture, habits, fashion, advertising, and news. When a product becomes popular, demand shifts right. When it goes out of style, demand shifts left. Companies spend huge sums on advertising and social media to change tastes. News also changes them: after reports of a food-safety scare, demand for the affected product can fall at once, even if the price has not moved.

The third determinant is consumer expectations: what buyers believe will happen to prices, incomes, or availability in the future. If you expect a price to rise next month, you have a reason to buy now, and demand today shifts right. If you expect a price to fall, you have a reason to wait, and demand today shifts left. Expectations help explain a rush on gasoline before a refinery shutdown, the long line at the electronics store the night before a price increase, and the spike in car sales just before a tax credit expires. You will see that last example in this chapter's Case Study.

Both determinants involve a kind of incentive. A nonmonetary incentive, such as the status of owning a popular product, can change tastes. A monetary incentive, such as a discount or a coupon that will end on Sunday, can change expectations. Remember from Chapter 1 that people respond to incentives, and changes in incentives change behavior.

Key Concepts

Where Tastes Come From

Tastes are not random. Several forces shape what buyers like. Advertising tries to make a product more attractive or to remind buyers that it exists. Information changes tastes when buyers learn something new, such as a health study, a safety recall, or a friend's review. Culture and community shape tastes as well: the foods served at family celebrations in the Central Valley, the music played at the Kern County Fair, and the clothes worn at school all reflect traditions and what friends do. Finally, habit matters, because people tend to keep buying what they bought last time.

A change in tastes is hard to measure directly, because no one can see a taste. Economists infer it. If more people buy a product at the same price and with the same incomes, then something about tastes (or about another determinant) has changed. For this reason, economists usually examine the other four determinants first and treat a change in tastes as the explanation that is left over.

Tastes can fade as well as grow. A fashion that is popular one year and out of date the next makes demand shift right and then back to the left. Sellers that depend on trends, such as a shop that sells a popular sneaker, face a more uncertain demand than sellers of staples such as bread or eggs. A business that expects its product to go out of style needs to earn its profit quickly or find a new product.

Key Concepts

Expectations in Action: Stocking Up and Waiting

Expectations explain some of the most dramatic changes in demand. In March 2020, as the COVID-19 pandemic began, shoppers across the country bought cleaning supplies, toilet paper, and canned food in large amounts, and store shelves emptied. The price of those goods had not changed. What changed was what buyers expected: they feared shortages and higher prices, so they bought early and bought extra. Demand shifted right.

The opposite also happens. When a company announces that a new phone will go on sale in three months, some buyers decide to wait, and demand for the current phone shifts left. When a store announces that a sale ends on Sunday, shoppers who were planning to buy later come in now. Expectations about income matter too. A high school senior who expects a better-paying job after graduation may decide to buy a used truck now, while a worker who fears a layoff may cut spending long before any paycheck is lost.

Because expectations are about the future, they can change quickly with news, forecasts, and rumors. That is one reason that stores are crowded just before a storm warning and that buyers line up for a product before a tax change takes effect. A seller who understands this can plan for it, and a buyer who understands it can avoid being rushed into a decision made only because everyone else is rushing.

Key Concepts

Prices of Related Goods

The fourth determinant is the price of related goods, and there are two kinds. Substitutes are goods that can be used in place of each other. Chicken and beef, tortillas and bread, and a ride-share and a bus are substitutes. If the price of beef goes up, buyers shift toward chicken, so the demand for chicken shifts right. In other words, when the price of one good rises, the demand for its substitute increases. This relationship is the one that Standard 12.2.1 means when it refers to “incentives and substitutes.” A higher price gives buyers an incentive to turn to a substitute.

Complements are goods that are used together. Tortilla chips and salsa, printers and ink, and cars and gasoline are examples. If the price of gasoline rises, people drive less and also shift their next car purchase toward more efficient vehicles, so demand for large trucks falls. In other words, when the price of one good rises, demand for its complement decreases.

Key Concepts

The Number of Buyers

The fifth determinant is the number of buyers in the market. Market demand is the sum of individual demands, as you saw in Section 1, so when more people enter a market, demand shifts right. A growing population, an immigration wave, a baby boom, or the opening of a new employer in town all add buyers. When the number of buyers falls, demand shifts left. The number of buyers also changes with age and demographics: a rising number of teenagers raises demand for school supplies, and an aging population raises demand for health care.

In Kern County, the number of buyers can change with the seasons. During the harvest months, thousands of seasonal farmworkers arrive to work in the fields and orchards, and demand shifts right in local grocery stores, laundromats, and food trucks that cater to them. When the harvest ends, many of those workers leave, and demand falls again.

To summarize, you can remember the five determinants with the memory aid I-T-E-R-N: Income, Tastes, Expectations, prices of Related goods, and Number of buyers. If a change in the market does not involve the product's own price, one of these five is almost always the cause.

Key Concepts

Putting It All Together: A Four-Step Method

When a news story describes a change in a market, you can sort it out with four steps. Step 1: Name the good and the market. Be specific about the product, the place, and the time. Step 2: Ask whether the product's own price changed. If the cause is a change in the good's own price, the buyers move along the demand curve and the quantity demanded changes. Step 3: If the cause is anything else, find the determinant. Use I-T-E-R-N: income, tastes, expectations, related goods, number of buyers. Step 4: Decide the direction. If the change makes buyers want more at every price, shift the curve right. If it makes them want less at every price, shift it left.

Try the method on a story. Suppose the price of gasoline in Bakersfield rises sharply, and a reporter asks what will happen to the number of people who ride Golden Empire Transit buses. Step 1: the good is bus rides in Bakersfield. Step 2: the price of bus rides did not change, so this is not a movement along the curve. Step 3: the price of a related good changed, because driving a car (which uses gasoline) is a substitute for riding the bus. Step 4: a higher price of a substitute increases demand, so the demand curve for bus rides shifts right.

Often two determinants change at once, and they may push in opposite directions. Suppose a new school opens in Shafter (more buyers, a rightward push) at the same time that a new restaurant opens next door and sells lunch at a lower price (a cheaper substitute, a leftward push on the first restaurant's demand). The result depends on which push is larger. Economists cannot say which way the net shift goes without more information, and a careful student says so instead of guessing.

Key Concepts

Measuring How Buyers React

The law of demand tells us the direction of buyers' response: higher prices mean lower quantities. A business owner needs more than that. If the owner raises the price of a taco by 10 percent, will sales fall a little or a lot? Will the truck earn more money or less? To answer these questions, economists measure the size of the response. The measure is called elasticity, the degree to which a change in one variable causes a change in another.

The most common measure is the price elasticity of demand, which tells how much the quantity demanded changes when the price changes. The formula is a ratio of two percentages:

Price elasticity of demand = percent change in quantity demanded ÷ percent change in price.

Because price and quantity move in opposite directions along a demand curve, the answer is technically a negative number. Economists ignore the minus sign and use the absolute value, so that elasticity is always reported as a positive number. A value of 2 means that a 1 percent increase in price makes the quantity demanded drop by 2 percent. A value of 0.5 means that a 1 percent increase in price makes the quantity demanded drop by only 0.5 percent.

There is one more detail. If you compute percent change from $2.00 to $2.50 you get a 25 percent increase, but if you compute from $2.50 back to $2.00 you get a 20 percent decrease. To avoid getting two answers for the same pair of points, economists use the midpoint method, which divides each change by the average of the starting and ending values.

QUICK REFERENCEElasticity is a measure of how much one variable changes in response to a change in another variable. Spanish: elasticidad
QUICK REFERENCEPrice elasticity of demand is the percent change in quantity demanded divided by the percent change in price. Spanish: elasticidad precio de la demanda
MATH HANDBOOKMidpoint formula. % change in quantity = (Q2 − Q1) ÷ [(Q1 + Q2) ÷ 2]. % change in price = (P2 − P1) ÷ [(P1 + P2) ÷ 2]. Elasticity = |% change in quantity ÷ % change in price|.

Step 2: Percent change in price. The change is $3.50 − $3.00 = $0.50. The average of $3.00 and $3.50 is $3.25. So 0.50 ÷ 3.25 ≈ 0.154, or about 15.4 percent.

Key Concepts

Elastic, Inelastic, and Unit Elastic Demand

The size of the elasticity number tells you what kind of demand a product has.

When elasticity is greater than 1, the percent change in quantity is larger than the percent change in price. Buyers are very sensitive to price, and economists call the demand elastic. When elasticity is less than 1, the percent change in quantity is smaller than the percent change in price. Buyers are not very sensitive, and the demand is inelastic. When elasticity equals exactly 1, the percent changes are equal, and the demand is unit elastic.

Two extremes are useful in thought experiments. With perfectly inelastic demand (elasticity of 0), buyers purchase the same quantity at any price, and the demand curve is a vertical line. A life-saving medicine with no substitute comes close for people who need it. With perfectly elastic demand, a tiny price increase makes buyers stop buying at all, and the demand curve is a horizontal line. This happens in theory when many identical sellers compete, as you will see in Chapter 7. Real products fall somewhere between the extremes.

Figure 4.14 shows the pattern. A flat demand curve is elastic, since a small change in price covers a large change in quantity. A steep curve is inelastic. An important warning: the slope of a straight-line demand curve is the same everywhere, but its elasticity is not. Using the taco schedule, demand was inelastic at low prices (0.64) and elastic at high prices (2.6 and beyond). Elasticity depends on the percent changes, and a one-dollar change matters much more when the price is $3 than when it is $30.

Key Concepts

What Makes Demand Elastic?

Why is demand for some products elastic and demand for others inelastic? Economists have identified four main factors.

1. Availability of substitutes. The more easily buyers can switch to something else, the more elastic the demand. If one taco truck raises its prices, customers can walk to the next truck, so demand for any one truck's tacos is elastic. If the electric company in your area raises prices, you cannot buy electricity from a different company, so demand is inelastic. The broader the definition of a good, the fewer substitutes it has: demand for food is far less elastic than demand for one brand of cereal.

2. Necessities versus luxuries. A necessity is a good that people consider essential, such as basic food, housing, electricity, or prescription medicine. A luxury is something people want but can do without, such as a cruise or restaurant meal. Demand for necessities is typically inelastic, because people must buy them, while demand for luxuries is typically elastic. The category depends on the person. For a worker who lives 40 miles from the job, gasoline is a necessity, but for someone with a bike and a bus pass, it may not be.

3. Share of the budget. When a good takes a large part of a buyer's income, the buyer notices price changes and responds. A 10 percent increase in the price of rent is a big matter because rent is a large share of the family budget. A 10 percent increase in the price of a box of salt, which costs less than a dollar, is hardly noticed. Demand is more elastic for goods that take a large share of income.

4. Time. Demand tends to be more elastic over a long period than over a short one. When gasoline prices jump, drivers in Kern County cannot change their routines overnight. In the short run, they pay the higher price. Over months and years, they buy more efficient cars, carpool, move closer to work, or buy electric vehicles. That is why economists distinguish between short-run and long-run elasticity, and why the demand for gasoline looks very inelastic after a sudden price spike and more elastic years later.

Time and Elasticity: The Gasoline Example

The fourth factor, time, is easy to state and sometimes surprising in the data. Figure 4.16 shows estimates of the short-run price elasticity of gasoline demand in two periods, from a study by the economists Jonathan Hughes, Christopher Knittel, and Daniel Sperling. For 1975 to 1980, they estimated elasticities between 0.21 and 0.34. For 2001 to 2006, they estimated elasticities between 0.034 and 0.077. In both periods, demand in the short run was inelastic. In the later period, however, it was much less responsive to price. A 10 percent rise in the price lowered the quantity demanded by only about 0.3 to 0.8 percent, compared with about 2.1 to 3.4 percent in the earlier period.

The short run is a period that is too short for buyers to change big things such as the car they drive, the home they rent, or the job they commute to. In the long run they can change all of these, so long-run elasticity is usually larger than short-run elasticity. The study measures only the short run, so it does not show the long run. Economists suggest several possible reasons for the drop in short-run responsiveness between the two periods, such as gasoline taking a smaller share of household income. The main lesson here is that an elasticity is not a permanent fact about a product. It is an estimate for a particular place, time, and group of buyers.

For Kern County drivers, the lesson is practical. A worker with a 40-mile commute who faces a sudden price increase cannot do much about it that week. Over a year or two, though, the same worker can move, change jobs, carpool, or buy a more efficient or electric vehicle. Sellers and governments that expect buyers to stay unresponsive forever may be surprised.

Elasticity in the Real World

Economists have estimated price elasticities for many products. Figure 4.17 shows a selection of estimates drawn from a variety of studies, as reported in Principles of Economics 3e. Keep in mind that each number is an estimate for a particular place, time, and group of buyers. Different studies find different values, and the same good can be more or less elastic depending on how it is defined. Even so, the ranking is useful: it shows why a government that wants to raise money by taxing goods with inelastic demand may collect more than one that taxes goods with elastic demand.

Key Concepts

Two More Elasticities: Income and Cross-Price

Elasticity is a general tool: it measures how much one variable responds to a change in another. Price elasticity of demand compares the change in the quantity demanded with the change in the good's own price. Two other versions of the tool answer different questions.

Income elasticity of demand compares the change in the quantity demanded with the change in income: income elasticity of demand = percent change in quantity demanded ÷ percent change in income. It is positive for normal goods, because buyers purchase more when their income rises, and negative for inferior goods, because they purchase less. Look back at the hypothetical household in Figure 4.9. When its income rises from $30,000 to $50,000, its restaurant meals rise from 3 to 5 per month, a change of 2 ÷ 4 = 50 percent by the midpoint method, while its income rises by $20,000 ÷ $40,000 = 50 percent. The income elasticity is 50 ÷ 50 = 1.0, a positive number, so restaurant meals are a normal good. Its instant noodle meals fall from 8 to 6, a change of −2 ÷ 7 ≈ −28.6 percent, so the income elasticity is −28.6 ÷ 50 ≈ −0.57. The negative sign shows an inferior good.

Cross-price elasticity of demand compares the change in the quantity demanded of one good with the change in the price of a different good: cross-price elasticity of demand = percent change in quantity demanded of good A ÷ percent change in the price of good B. It is positive for substitutes, because a higher price for B sends buyers to A, and negative for complements, because a higher price for B lowers the demand for A. It is close to zero for goods that are unrelated. Suppose, for example (hypothetical numbers), that a 10 percent rise in the price of beef raises the quantity of chicken demanded by 4 percent. The cross-price elasticity is 4 ÷ 10 = +0.4, which tells you that beef and chicken are substitutes. If a 10 percent rise in the price of gasoline lowers the quantity of large trucks demanded by 3 percent, the cross-price elasticity is −0.3, which tells you that gasoline and large trucks are complements.

Unlike price elasticity, these two measures keep their sign, because the sign carries information: it tells you whether a good is normal or inferior, and whether two goods are substitutes or complements.

Key Concepts

Elasticity and Total Revenue

Why do businesses care about elasticity? Because it tells them what happens to their money when they change their price. A seller's total revenue is the price multiplied by the quantity sold: total revenue = price × quantity. When the price goes up, two things happen at once. The seller receives more per unit, which raises revenue, but the seller sells fewer units, which lowers revenue. Which effect is stronger depends on the elasticity.

If demand is inelastic, the quantity falls by a smaller percent than the price rises, so total revenue rises when the price goes up. If demand is elastic, the quantity falls by a larger percent, so total revenue falls when the price goes up. If demand is unit elastic, the two effects offset each other, and total revenue stays the same. These results are called the total revenue test. They work in reverse for a price cut: cutting the price raises revenue if demand is elastic and lowers it if demand is inelastic.

Figure 4.18 shows the test with the taco truck. At $1.50, the truck sells 120 tacos and earns $180. At $2.00, it sells 100 tacos and earns $200, so revenue rose after the price rose because demand was inelastic in this range. Between $2.00 and $2.50, revenue holds at $200 (unit elastic). Above $2.50, revenue falls: at $3.00 it is $180, and at $4.00 it is only $80. The same demand curve contains inelastic, unit elastic, and elastic ranges.

A real caution: revenue is not profit. Profit is revenue minus costs, and the taco truck must pay for meat, tortillas, labor, and fuel, which depend on how many tacos it makes. A seller that wants the highest profit must look at costs too, which you will study in Chapter 5.

Key Concepts

Elasticity in Pricing: Matinees, Discounts, and Peak Prices

Have you noticed that a movie matinee costs less than an evening show, or that many places give discounts to students and seniors? Sellers do this because different groups of buyers have different elasticities. People who can easily choose another time or another activity, such as students with flexible afternoons, have more elastic demand. A seller who charges a lower price to the more elastic group and a higher price to the less elastic group can sometimes earn more total revenue than it could with one price for everyone. Economists call this pricing practice price discrimination. Here the word does not mean unfair treatment. It means only that different buyers pay different prices for essentially the same product.

For the strategy to work, the seller must be able to keep the groups apart and must stop the low-price buyers from reselling to the high-price buyers. A theater checks tickets at the door, which keeps the matinee crowd from selling matinee tickets to evening buyers. Airlines, hotels, and sports teams use the same idea when they charge more for tickets bought at the last minute or during popular times, when buyers have fewer substitutes.

Elasticity also helps explain price differences that have nothing to do with discounts. A restaurant near a busy highway exit, whose customers are travelers with few nearby alternatives, can often charge more for a meal than a restaurant in a neighborhood where customers can easily walk to a competitor. The difference in price reflects a difference in the elasticity of demand that each restaurant faces.

The Farmers' Paradox With Numbers

Earlier you read that a very large harvest can lower farmers' total revenue. Elasticity makes the argument exact. Suppose (hypothetical numbers) that onion growers in the San Joaquin Valley sell 100 million pounds in a normal year at $0.40 a pound, for total revenue of 100 million × $0.40 = $40.0 million. In a year with perfect weather they harvest 120 million pounds. If demand for onions is inelastic, with an elasticity of 0.5, then buyers will take the extra 20 million pounds only if the price falls by a larger percent than the quantity rises.

By the midpoint method, the quantity rises by 20 ÷ 110 ≈ 18.2 percent. With an elasticity of 0.5, the price must fall by 18.2 ÷ 0.5 ≈ 36.4 percent. That drop brings the price to about $0.28 a pound. Total revenue is now 120 million × $0.28 ≈ $33.2 million, which is $6.8 million less than in the normal year, even though growers sold 20 percent more onions. A grower acting alone, with a small share of the crop, still gains by selling more, because the grower's own sales hardly change the market price. When every grower does the same, the price falls for all of them.

This result helps explain why agricultural policy and farm cooperatives have often tried to manage supply, and why growers sometimes welcome weather that reduces everyone's harvest. It is also a reminder that “good news” for one market participant can be bad news for the group, depending on elasticity.

Many ideas in this chapter appear in a single book. In 1890, an English economist named Alfred Marshall published Principles of Economics, which became the standard textbook of economics for a generation. Marshall had trained as a mathematician at the University of Cambridge before turning to the study of human behavior, and he taught at Cambridge from 1885 to 1908.

Before Marshall, economists argued in long paragraphs about how prices were determined. Marshall made demand and supply the center of the subject and drew them as curves that students could read and use. He compared the two forces to the blades of a pair of scissors: you cannot say which blade does the cutting. He also gave economics the idea of elasticity, a way to say not just that quantity falls when price rises but by how much.

Marshall cared about the practical side of the subject too. He believed that economists should study real businesses and real working lives in order to improve them, and he spent time visiting factories to see for himself how work was organized. The tools he popularized, including demand curves and elasticity, are used today by businesses setting prices, governments studying taxes, and every student in this class.

Chapter 4 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · What Is Demand?
Microeconomics studies the choices of individuals, households, and businesses. Demand is the willingness and ability to buy a product. The law of demand says that, ceteris paribus, the quantity demanded falls when price rises, because of the substitution effect, the income effect, and diminishing marginal utility. Demand schedules and curves show the relationship, and market demand adds up individual demands. Willingness to pay, income, and diminishing marginal utility explain why buyers respond as they do.
Section 2 · What Factors Affect Demand?
A change in price moves buyers along a demand curve (a change in quantity demanded). A change in income, tastes, expectations, the price of related goods, or the number of buyers shifts the whole curve (a change in demand). Goods are normal or inferior, and related goods are substitutes or complements. A four-step method helps sort out any change in a market.
Section 3 · What Is Elasticity of Demand?
Price elasticity of demand is the percent change in quantity demanded divided by the percent change in price. Demand is elastic above 1, inelastic below 1, and unit elastic at 1. Substitutes, necessities, share of budget, and time affect elasticity. The total revenue test tells sellers whether a price change raises or lowers revenue. Income elasticity and cross-price elasticity classify goods as normal or inferior and as substitutes or complements.
