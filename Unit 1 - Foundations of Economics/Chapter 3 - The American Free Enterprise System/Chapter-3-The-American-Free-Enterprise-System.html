Concept Review

In Chapter 2 you learned that a market economy answers the three basic economic questions through the voluntary choices of buyers and sellers, and that every real economy is a mixed economy. You also met the features that make markets work: private property rights, voluntary exchange, profit, and competition. This chapter takes a closer look at one mixed economy, the one you live in, and asks what its rules are, why they matter, and what role government should play.

Chapter 3 Key Concept

The American free enterprise system is a mostly market-based economy in which people are free to start businesses, own property, make contracts, and keep profits, within laws that protect everyone's rights. Property rights and the chance to earn a profit give people incentives to work, invest, and take care of resources, and prices guide those choices. Government's job is to set and enforce the rules, to provide what markets cannot, and to protect people from harms the market ignores.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine that you want to start a business this summer: a pressure-washing service for driveways in Shafter. You do not need anyone's permission to decide what to charge or whom to work for. You may buy a pressure washer with money you saved, hire a friend, and keep what is left after paying your costs. Customers are free to hire you or someone else. Notice how many rules are quietly helping you: a law that protects your washer from theft, a court that can enforce your contract if a customer refuses to pay, a city that requires a business license, and a state that expects you to follow safety and tax rules. The freedom is real, and so are the rules that protect it. By the end of this chapter you will be able to explain what makes the American system work, how prices and profits guide decisions, and where government steps in.

Case Study Preview

Nearly 36 million small businesses operate in the United States, and most of them have no employees other than the owner. Some succeed for decades, while many close within a few years. At the end of this chapter you will study the United States as a land of entrepreneurs, weigh the evidence about the risks and rewards of starting a business, and decide what that evidence does and does not show.

Key Concepts

What Is the Free Enterprise System?

Think about everything you did to get ready for school this morning. You may have eaten cereal that was grown, processed, packaged, and shipped by dozens of companies you will never meet. You may have worn shoes designed in one country and sewn in another, and you may have checked a phone built from parts that came from a dozen places. No government office assigned each of those companies its job. Instead, millions of people and businesses chose what to make and what to buy, and the result was a store shelf with exactly the cereal you wanted. This remarkable coordination is what most people mean when they talk about the American economy.

In Chapter 2 you learned that the United States has a mixed economy that leans heavily toward the market end of the range. Economists often call the market-based part of such an economy capitalism, a system in which resources such as land, factories, and equipment are owned mostly by private individuals and businesses rather than by the government. In the United States, the same idea usually carries a second name: the free enterprise system. The word enterprise means a business or a project. The name stresses that, within the law, anyone is free to start an enterprise, to run it as they see fit, and to try to make it succeed.

The central idea of free enterprise is that producers are free to make the goods and services that consumers want, and consumers are free to spend their money on whatever they choose. Producers are driven by the hope of earning a profit. Consumers are driven by the desire to satisfy their wants with the money they have. When the two groups meet in markets, the choices they make answer the three basic economic questions from Chapter 1: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom.

Free enterprise does not mean that there are no rules. It means that the rules aim to protect the freedom of individuals instead of replacing their choices with orders. A restaurant owner may choose the menu but must keep the kitchen clean. A farmer may choose which crop to plant but must follow water and pesticide rules. The key difference from a command economy is who makes the main decisions: owners and customers, rather than central planners. Section 4 of this chapter returns to the question of how much government is the right amount.

Key Concepts

The Four Economic Freedoms

What makes free enterprise possible? Economists and historians point to a set of legal rights and economic freedoms. Four of them are especially important, and each one protects a different part of the process of starting and running a business. Without them, free enterprise would exist in name only.

The first is open opportunity, the freedom to choose your own work, to start a business in almost any field, and to move to a new job or a new place. In a system with open opportunity, you do not need a ruler's permission to become a farmer, a welder, a nurse, or an owner of a car wash. Your talent, effort, and willingness to take risks count for more than the family you were born into. This freedom does not guarantee success, and not everyone starts with the same advantages, but the law does not close the door.

The second is legal equality, the principle that the law applies to everyone in the same way. Under legal equality, no business receives special permission to operate in a way that is forbidden to others, and no citizen is shut out of the marketplace because of race, religion, sex, or national origin. A history lesson shows why this matters. In the years after the Civil War, Congress passed the Civil Rights Act of 1866 to guarantee that all citizens had the same right to make contracts and to own property. The Fourteenth Amendment later promised citizens equal protection of the laws. Equal rights on paper have not always meant equal treatment in practice, and Americans have struggled for generations to close the gap, but the principle gives people a legal standard to appeal to.

The third is free contract, the right to make agreements, buy and sell, and set the terms of those agreements. A contract is a legally enforceable agreement. When a farmer agrees to sell almonds to a processor for a set price, both sides can rely on the agreement, because a court can enforce it if either side backs out. Free contract lets people trade with strangers and plan for the future, which would be much harder if every promise depended on trust alone.

The fourth is the profit motive, the desire to earn a profit, which is revenue minus costs. The freedom to keep the profit you earn gives people a reason to work hard, take risks, and invest in better ways of doing things. If government took all the profit, few people would bother to start a business. If no one could earn a profit, no one would have a reason to supply the goods and services that others want.

Key Concepts

Competition: The Force That Keeps the System Honest

Imagine that there is only one place to buy tacos in your town. The owner could charge $8 for a taco, serve it cold, and still have customers, because nobody else sells tacos. Now imagine that three other taquerías open on the same block. Suddenly the original owner has to worry. If the tacos stay expensive and cold, customers will walk next door. The owner must lower prices, improve quality, offer better service, or lose business. This pressure is called competition.

Economists define competition as the rivalry among sellers to win customers, or among buyers to win scarce goods. Competition is closely tied to the profit motive. Sellers want profits, and the best way to get them is to attract customers by offering better products at lower prices. In a competitive market, a business that wastes resources, charges too much, or ignores its customers will lose them to a rival, and the business that serves its customers well will earn the profit.

Competition benefits consumers in at least three ways. First, it holds prices down. When many stores sell similar products, none of them can raise prices much without losing customers. Second, it improves quality. Businesses that sell a poor product find that their customers leave. Third, it encourages innovation. A business that invents a cheaper way to make something, or a new product that customers love, can capture customers from its rivals. The history of the American economy is full of examples, from the personal computer to smartphones to the rise of online shopping.

Competition also benefits workers who have skills that employers want. When many employers compete to hire the same worker, they must offer higher wages or better working conditions to attract that worker. A welder or an electrician in Kern County who is in demand can choose among several employers. Competition among employers and competition among workers are two sides of the same system.

Of course, competition has costs as well as benefits. Businesses that lose the competition may close, and their workers may lose their jobs. Economists call this process creative destruction, because new businesses and technologies replace older ones. Society as a whole usually gains from the changes, but the people who lose their jobs may not feel that way, which is one reason why Section 4 discusses the safety net. Competition can also weaken if one company becomes so large that it can control prices. Chapter 7 examines these market structures in detail.

Key Concepts

Entrepreneurs and the Spirit of Enterprise

Behind every new business is an entrepreneur, a person who organizes land, labor, and capital to produce a good or a service, and who takes the risk of losing money in the hope of earning a profit. In Chapter 1 you learned that entrepreneurship is the fourth factor of production. Entrepreneurs are the people who spot a need, put resources together to meet it, and accept the risk that they may fail.

Entrepreneurs make three important contributions. First, they create jobs, because every new business that grows beyond its owner needs workers. Second, they bring new ideas to the market, from new foods to new software. Third, they push existing businesses to improve, because a new competitor forces older firms to respond. Many of the best-known American companies began as tiny enterprises, started by one or two people with an idea and not much money.

How many people are trying? Figure 3.2 shows the number of business applications filed in the United States each year, as counted by the Census Bureau. Between 2005 and 2019, applications rose slowly, from about 2.5 million to about 3.5 million a year. Then, in 2020, the number jumped, and in 2021 it reached about 5.4 million. Economists have offered several explanations: millions of people lost jobs during the pandemic and tried self-employment, others had extra time at home to try out a business idea, and the rise of online selling made it cheaper to start. The numbers have stayed high since then. An application is not the same as an operating business, since many applications never lead to a business that hires workers, but the trend shows that the spirit of enterprise remains strong.

Starting a business is risky, and the chapter's Case Study shows that many new businesses do not last. That risk is part of the bargain of free enterprise. The profit that rewards a successful entrepreneur compensates for the possibility of loss. A system without that possibility of loss would also have no possibility of gain.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEWho Is Self-Employed? A World View

Another way to look at entrepreneurship is to count the people who work for themselves. In 2023, only about 6 percent of American workers were self-employed, a lower share than in many other countries. Does this mean the United States has fewer entrepreneurs? Not exactly. In low-income countries, most people who are self-employed run tiny farms or street stalls because few paid jobs are available. Economists call this necessity entrepreneurship, and it differs from the choice of starting a business to pursue a promising idea. In wealthier countries, more people work for employers, and the self-employed are more often professionals and business owners.

Key Concepts

What Are Property Rights?

Think about the last time you borrowed something from a friend, such as a phone charger or a jacket. You probably took good care of it, but you did not think twice if it got a little scuffed. Now think about your own phone or your own jacket. You probably handle your own things differently, because the cost of losing or damaging them falls on you. That simple observation sits at the center of one of the most important ideas in economics.

Property rights are the rules that determine who may own, use, and sell resources, and who may keep the benefits that those resources produce. Ownership is not a single right. It is a bundle of rights that usually includes the right to use an asset, the right to exclude others from using it, the right to sell or transfer it, and the right to keep the income it earns. A farmer who owns an orchard in Kern County can pick the almonds, put up a fence, sell the land to someone else, and keep the money from the harvest. If any of those rights is missing, the property is less valuable and the owner has less reason to invest in it.

Economists describe three main kinds of ownership. With private property, individuals or businesses hold the bundle of rights, as with your phone, a family's home, or a farmer's land. With public property, a government holds the rights on behalf of the public, as with city streets, national forests, and public schools. A third category, which you will study later in this section, is common property, a resource that a whole community shares and that no single person owns. The American free enterprise system relies heavily on private property, but every economy uses all three.

Rights on paper do not mean much unless they are enforced. Courts, police, and public records make property rights real. In California, county governments keep records of who owns each parcel of land. In Kern County, this work is done at the Kern County Hall of Records in Bakersfield, where deeds and other property documents are recorded. A buyer can check that a seller really owns the land before paying for it, and a lender can feel safe lending money against it. When ownership is clear and protected, people can buy, sell, borrow, and invest with confidence. When it is unclear, deals become risky or never happen at all.

Key Concepts

Property Rights as Incentives

In Chapter 1 you learned that an incentive is a reward or a penalty that influences people's choices. Property rights create powerful incentives, and the most important is this: owners keep the benefits of caring for their property, and they pay the costs of neglecting it. A homeowner who paints the house raises its value, and the homeowner is the one who gets that higher value when the house is sold. A homeowner who lets the roof leak pays for the repairs and risks a lower price. Because both the reward and the cost land on the owner, owners have a reason to take good care of what they own.

This incentive is especially strong for investments that pay off slowly. An almond tree takes about three years to produce a meaningful crop and several more to reach full production, and a well-managed orchard keeps producing for a quarter century or longer. Why would anyone invest in trees, a drip irrigation system, and soil improvements that take years to pay back unless that person could count on owning the orchard and keeping the harvest? A grower who could be forced to leave next year would plant nothing, because the benefits might go to somebody else. This is why economists say that secure property rights encourage investment, and investment helps the economy grow.

The Mota family's almond orchard shows how this works in practice. In 2015, the U.S. Department of Agriculture described the family's 45-acre orchard in Livingston, California, in the Central Valley. Facing drought, the family worked with the USDA's Natural Resources Conservation Service on a conservation plan. The plan used a cover crop between the rows to protect soil health and used micro-irrigation to deliver water to the trees more precisely. Neither step was required by the market. The owners chose them because they owned the land, and the improvements would pay off over many years in healthier soil and a more reliable harvest.

Property rights also connect to the law of supply, which you will study in Chapter 5. The law of supply says that producers offer more of a good when they expect a higher price, because higher prices raise the profit they can keep. That is an incentive at work. Property rights make the incentive meaningful, because producers can only respond to a higher price if they are sure they will keep the revenue. In this way, property rights and the profit motive from Section 1 work as a pair.

EXAMPLEOwner or Renter? A Hypothetical Orchard

Suppose a drip irrigation system costs $20,000 and saves $4,000 a year in water and extra harvest. Over 10 years it would save $4,000 × 10 = $40,000, which is twice its cost. An owner who expects to keep the orchard for 10 years or longer will build it, because the net gain is $40,000 − $20,000 = $20,000.

Now suppose a renter has only a one-year lease and no promise of renewal. The renter would spend $20,000 to receive only $4,000 in savings, a net loss of $16,000, and would probably skip the system. The land, the water, and the system are the same in both cases. What differs is who gets the benefits and how long the person can count on them. (All numbers are hypothetical.)

Key Concepts

When No One Owns It: The Tragedy of the Commons

If ownership gives people a reason to protect resources, what happens to resources that nobody owns? Many natural resources, such as ocean fish, underground water, and clean air, do not belong to any single person. Economists call a resource that many people can use, but that no one owns or controls, a common-pool resource. Using it is open to all, but each person's use leaves less for everyone else.

Consider a lake with 100 fish that is open to every fisher. If each fisher catches as many fish as possible today, the fish will not have a chance to reproduce, and the lake will be empty next year. Yet every individual fisher faces the same incentive: any fish I leave in the water today will probably be caught by someone else. So each fisher races to catch more, and everyone ends up worse off. This problem is called the tragedy of the commons. The word tragedy does not mean that anyone is wicked. It describes the way that sensible individual choices can add up to a result that nobody wants.

The ocean fisheries of the United States show both the problem and the solutions. Every year, the federal agency NOAA Fisheries reports on the condition of fish populations. At the end of 2024, 23 fish stocks were on the overfishing list, meaning that they were being caught faster than is sustainable, and 42 stocks were on the overfished list, meaning that their populations had fallen too low. Since 2000, 51 stocks have been rebuilt. Figure 3.5 shows the 2023 and 2024 counts. Part of that progress comes from rules that cap the total catch. In some fisheries, such as the Alaska halibut fishery, which began a share-based system in 1995, regulators give each boat owner a long-term share of the total allowed catch. A boat owner with a secure share has a reason to protect the fish population, since a healthier fishery makes the share more valuable, and the owner no longer needs to race.

Fish are a renewable resource, one that can replenish itself if it is not overused. Oil is different. Oil is a nonrenewable resource, because it took millions of years to form and cannot be replaced on any human timescale. Kern County produces a large share of California's oil. Underground oil often sits in large pools that lie under many owners' land, and in the early twentieth century a landowner could pump oil from the shared pool and keep whatever came out. Each owner had an incentive to pump fast, before the neighbors did. The Nobel Prize committee's summary of Ostrom's work points to the failure to privatize oil pools in Texas and Oklahoma in the 1930s as a case that caused massive waste. Today, rules that organize how oil is leased and produced are meant to reduce that waste.

Property rights are not the only answer to a commons problem. Another solution is government regulation, such as limiting the number of fish that can be caught. A third solution is cooperation among users, as the Economics Pacesetter later in this section explains. The right solution depends on the resource and the community that uses it.

Groundwater in Kern County

A commons problem much closer to home lies beneath the Central Valley. For much of California's history, landowners were free to pump as much groundwater as they could reach under their own land, and nobody tracked the total. In dry years, farms pumped more, and water tables fell. The Kern County Subbasin, a groundwater basin that covers about 1.78 million acres of the southern San Joaquin Valley, has been classified by the state as critically overdrafted, which means that over time more water has been pumped out than flows back in.

In 2014, California passed the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, or SGMA. The law does not simply assign each farmer a share. Instead, it requires local groundwater sustainability agencies to write plans that bring each basin into balance by about 2040. The Kern Subbasin is managed by 20 such agencies. In September 2025, the State Water Resources Control Board voted to return the basin to oversight by the state Department of Water Resources instead of placing it on probation, after local agencies strengthened their plans, although the Board required them to meet several further conditions. The story is still unfolding, and it shows that deciding who may use a shared resource, and how much, is a hard question, even in a state with strong property laws.

CONNECT TO SCIENCEPumping too much groundwater can cause the land above to sink, a process called subsidence. Kern's groundwater plans include goals for reducing subsidence, because sinking land can damage canals, roads, and wells.
GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEHerders on the Mongolian Steppe

Economists once believed that a commons could only be saved by government control or by dividing the land into private plots. Research in the real world has complicated that view. According to the Nobel Prize committee's summary of the work of economist Elinor Ostrom, traditional seasonal herding in Mongolia kept grasslands in better condition than either collectivization in China and Russia or privatization in Inner Mongolia, which encouraged herders to settle in one place and led to land degradation.

The lesson is not that private property is bad or that common management is always better. The lesson is that the right rules depend on the resource and the people who use it, and that communities sometimes find effective rules of their own.

ECONOMICS PACESETTER

Elinor OstromSolving the Tragedy of the Commons

Many economists predicted that shared resources would always be overused. Elinor Ostrom went out and looked for evidence. Her work won the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences, and it began close to where this chapter's story of groundwater is set.

Ostrom grew up in Los Angeles during the Great Depression and attended Beverly Hills High School, where she joined the debate team. She worked her way through the University of California, Los Angeles, finishing her bachelor's degree in three years without going into debt. As a graduate student, she joined a research team studying the water industry in Southern California and was assigned to the West Basin, a groundwater basin under several coastal cities and part of Los Angeles County. She later realized that the problem she had studied was a commons problem, because many pumpers shared one underground supply. She earned her PhD at UCLA in 1965.

Ostrom spent her career at Indiana University, where she and her colleagues studied how real communities manage shared resources such as irrigation systems, forests, fisheries, and pastures. Instead of relying only on theory, she collected data from many places around the world. According to the Nobel committee's summary, she found that common resources are often "surprisingly well managed" when users can make and enforce their own rules and when the people who monitor the rules are the users themselves. Rules imposed from outside, or by powerful insiders, were often less respected. Her 1990 book Governing the Commons brought these findings together.

Ostrom did not claim that communities can solve every commons problem, and the Nobel committee noted that privatization or public regulation sometimes works better. Her contribution was to show that there are more than two choices. Between full private ownership and full government control lie many rules that communities can design for themselves. Today, California's groundwater agencies, which bring together local users to write the rules for each basin, follow a similar idea.

Key Concepts

Property Rights, Liberty, and Limits

Property rights protect more than money. They protect liberty. In a society where people own property, they can take actions without asking a ruler's approval. A person who owns a printing press can publish a newspaper, a person who owns a truck can change jobs, and a person who owns land can grow what she wishes. The philosopher and economist Adam Smith, whom you met in Chapter 2, argued that a system of natural liberty, in which people can use their talents and property as they choose, supports both prosperity and freedom. Spreading ownership across millions of people also spreads power, so that no single government office controls everyone's livelihood. California's standards ask students to analyze this connection between market economies and political and personal liberty, and Section 4 returns to it.

Property rights are strong, but they are not unlimited. Your right to use your property ends where it harms others. A landowner may not dump toxic chemicals on a neighbor's well, and a business owner may not operate a factory in a neighborhood zoned for homes. Cities use zoning to decide which areas may be used for homes, stores, or factories. Government may also take private land for public use, such as a highway or a school, through a process called eminent domain, but the Fifth Amendment requires "just compensation." Debates about where to draw the lines are among the most common conflicts in American politics.

Property rights also extend to ideas. A patent gives an inventor the exclusive right to make and sell an invention for a limited time, and a copyright protects the creator of a song, a book, or a computer program. These rights give inventors and artists an incentive to create, since others cannot simply copy their work and sell it for free. The Constitution authorizes Congress to create such protections "to promote the progress of science and useful arts." But because ideas can be shared at almost no cost, societies also set time limits so that, eventually, anyone may use them.

Key Concepts

Producers and Consumers: Who Decides What Is Made?

In a command economy, a planner decides how many pairs of shoes will be made this year. In a free enterprise system, no one decides. Instead, thousands of shoe companies make guesses about what customers will buy, and customers decide which guesses were right by choosing where to spend their money. A company that guesses well earns a profit and grows. A company that guesses badly loses money and may close. Over time, the products that customers value most are the ones that stay on the shelf.

Economists call this power of the buyer consumer sovereignty. The word sovereign means the one in charge, and in a market economy, consumers are said to be in charge of what is produced because producers can only earn a profit by selling what consumers choose to buy. Every dollar you spend is like a vote for the product you bought. When many consumers vote for the same product, producers see that the product is profitable and make more of it. When consumers stop voting for a product, producers make less of it or stop making it altogether.

Producers are not passive. To earn a profit, a producer must pay attention to two things: the price that customers are willing to pay, and the cost of making the product. Producers compete to find the cheapest way to make what customers want. A tomato grower who finds a way to use less water per box earns more profit at the same price. A taquería that buys ingredients in bigger amounts can lower costs. In this sense, free enterprise answers the question how to produce by rewarding the businesses that use scarce resources most efficiently. The answer to for whom is also determined by the market: goods go to those who are willing and able to pay the price.

Consumers, too, face scarcity. Each household has limited income and must decide how to divide it. Figure 3.6 shows how the average American household divided its spending in 2024 among major categories. Housing, transportation, and food together took up about 63 percent of the $78,535 that the average household spent. The pattern changes with income, age, and location, but the chart shows that necessities take a large share of household budgets. Producers who understand how consumers spend can decide where opportunities for profit may be found.

Key Concepts

Prices as Signals and Incentives

How do producers know what consumers want without a planner to tell them? The answer is prices. In a market economy, a price does more than tell you how much to pay. It acts as a price signal, a piece of information that tells buyers and sellers about how scarce something is. A high price signals that a good is scarce relative to the demand for it. A low price signals that the good is plentiful. And because prices are also incentives, they change what people do in response to the signal.

Consider what happens when a price rises. For sellers, a higher price means a higher profit on every unit sold. That is an incentive to produce more, and it is the idea behind the law of supply: producers generally offer more of a good at a higher price than at a lower price. For buyers, a higher price means that buying the good costs more compared with other things the buyer could purchase. That is an incentive to look for a substitute, a similar good that can take the place of the one that has become expensive. This is the idea behind the law of demand: buyers generally buy less of a good at a higher price. You will study these two laws in detail in Chapters 4 and 5, and California's economics standards ask students to understand them as responses to incentives.

The price of a dozen eggs gives a recent example. Figure 3.7 tracks the average U.S. price of a dozen Grade A large eggs from January 2021 to October 2026. In 2022, a disease called highly pathogenic avian influenza spread through U.S. poultry flocks. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service counted 173 million birds affected from February 2022 through May 2025, and table-egg-laying hens made up about three-quarters of those losses. Fewer hens meant fewer eggs. The price of eggs rose from about $1.47 in January 2021 to $4.82 in January 2023, fell back to about $2.04 in August 2023 as flocks recovered, and then climbed to a record of $6.23 in March 2025.

Watch how people and businesses responded. Many shoppers substituted: they bought fewer eggs, bought other foods, or used egg-free recipes. Some stores limited how many cartons a customer could buy, and some restaurants added a surcharge for eggs. On the supply side, the high price gave egg producers a strong incentive to rebuild flocks and to find eggs from other sources. In the first three months of 2025, the United States imported 218 million shell eggs, compared with a tiny amount a year earlier, according to the Congressional Research Service, and some of those eggs came from countries that had never exported table eggs to the United States. As supply recovered, the price fell back to $2.32 by October 2026. No one ordered these changes. The price signaled scarcity, and the incentive did the rest.

Notice one more point. A high price is unpleasant for buyers, and it is tempting to think that government should simply forbid it. But a price limit would remove the signal and the incentive at the same time. Buyers would have no reason to economize, and sellers would have no reason to rebuild supply, which is how shortages begin. You saw in Chapter 2 how price controls caused shortages in command economies. Chapter 6 examines the question of when governments should and should not interfere with prices.

Key Concepts

Profit, Loss, and the Movement of Resources

Prices send signals to buyers and sellers about what to do now. Profits and losses send longer-term signals about where resources should go. When a business earns a high profit, other people notice. They may open similar businesses, hoping to share in that profit, and resources such as land, labor, and money flow toward that industry. When a business loses money, its owners may shut it down, and those resources move elsewhere. Economists call the movement of businesses into and out of a market entry and exit.

Entry and exit guide resources toward their most valued uses. In recent decades, for example, growers across California's Central Valley have planted more almond orchards because almonds proved profitable, and growers in some places replaced other crops with almonds. When prices fall or a crop becomes less profitable, growers may pull out trees and plant something else. No central office decides how many acres should hold almonds, tomatoes, or cotton. Millions of separate decisions respond to the prices and profits that growers see.

A loss, a situation in which costs exceed revenue, is just as important as profit. A loss tells the owner that the resources used in that business are worth more in some other use. Losses are painful for the people who experience them, and a society should care about the workers who lose jobs when businesses close. But a system without losses would keep wasting resources on products no one wants, which is exactly what happened in many command economies.

Private businesses also invest in infrastructure when they see opportunities. In Shafter, a rail facility was built to connect the BNSF railroad line to the Paramount Logistics Park, an industrial area for storing and shipping products. Such a project is expensive and risky. The people who built it expected that businesses would pay to use it and that the profit would exceed the costs. This is how free enterprise directs scarce resources, such as steel, land, and labor, toward projects that others value.

Key Concepts

The Circular Flow With Government

In Chapter 2 you learned the circular flow model, which shows how households and businesses exchange money, goods, and resources through two kinds of markets. Households sell the use of their labor, land, and savings to businesses in the factor market and receive income. Households spend that income on goods and services in the product market, which gives businesses the revenue to pay for resources. The loop continues, and every step is a voluntary exchange guided by prices.

The model becomes more realistic when it includes government. Federal, state, and local governments collect taxes from households and from businesses, and use the money to provide services such as roads, schools, and national defense. Governments also hire workers and buy goods and services from businesses, such as computers, buses, and construction. Finally, they make transfer payments, payments made to people without receiving a good or service in return, such as Social Security checks and unemployment benefits. Figure 3.8 adds these flows to the model.

Adding government changes the picture in two ways. First, government becomes both a buyer in the product market and an employer in the factor market, which means that government spending creates income for households and revenue for businesses. Second, taxes and transfers move money from some households and businesses to others, which changes who can buy what. Whether these changes make the economy work better or worse is one of the biggest questions in economics and politics. Section 4 examines the roles that government plays in the free enterprise system and the arguments about them.

Notice what the model does not show. It does not show who owns the businesses, how much people earn, or whether anyone lacks access to the markets. Like all models, it simplifies reality so that the main relationships stand out. Chapter 16 will show how economists measure the total flow of spending in the economy.

Key Concepts

A Modified Free Enterprise System

In the first three sections of this chapter, the main characters were individuals and businesses: entrepreneurs who start enterprises, owners who care for their property, and consumers who steer production with their dollars. But look again at the helpers in the background. A court enforces a contract. A county office records who owns a parcel of land. A public road carries a truck full of almonds to a rail line. Government is part of the picture on every page.

Because of this, economists often describe the American economy as a modified free enterprise system: a system based on private ownership and market exchange in which government plays an important but limited role. This is another name for the mixed economy that you learned about in Chapter 2. The question is not whether government has a role. The question is what the role should be, and where the line between private choice and public action should be drawn. Americans have argued over that question since the nation's founding, and they argue about it today.

Adam Smith, the Scottish writer you met in Chapter 2, is often quoted as an advocate for free markets, yet he did not argue that government should do nothing. Smith believed that government has three duties: to defend the country against invasion, to protect every person from injustice and oppression by establishing a system of justice, and to build and maintain certain public works and institutions that would not be profitable for private businesses to provide. A modern American economist would add others. Today, most Americans expect government to perform at least five roles. It protects rights by enforcing property rights and contracts, it promotes competition by stopping businesses that try to eliminate their rivals unfairly, it provides public goods that markets will not supply, it corrects market failures such as pollution, and it provides a safety net for people who cannot support themselves.

These roles also connect to liberty. The California standards ask students to analyze how a market economy helps establish and preserve political and personal liberty. One argument says that when many people own property and run businesses, no single group controls everyone's livelihood, and citizens who are economically independent can speak and vote more freely. A second argument says that markets need strong, honest government to enforce the rules. Both arguments suggest that a free enterprise system and a free society support each other, and that each depends on rules that apply to everyone.

Market Failure

Markets are very good at coordinating decisions, but not at everything. Economists use the term market failure for a situation in which a market, left on its own, does not produce the best result for society. There are several causes: markets may fail to provide goods that cannot be sold one customer at a time, they may ignore costs and benefits that fall on people who are not part of the trade, and they may give rise to businesses that grow so powerful that competition fades.

Market failure does not mean that markets are bad. It means that, in certain cases, the incentives that guide private choices point in a direction that is different from what is best for everyone. In those cases, government may be able to improve the outcome by changing the incentives. Economists also warn that government has failings of its own, such as poor information, political pressure, and mistakes, so a good argument for government action needs to compare what would actually happen, and not merely compare reality with an ideal.

Key Concepts

Public Goods and the Free-Rider Problem

Imagine that the people of Shafter want a fireworks show on the Fourth of July. A company could sell tickets and put up a fence, and only ticket holders could watch. But if the show is high in the sky and everyone in town can see it from their backyards, why would anyone buy a ticket? Most people would wait and watch for free. The company would lose money and cancel the show, even though most residents would gladly have paid a few dollars.

A person who gets the benefits of a good without paying for it is called a free rider. When free riders are common, businesses cannot cover their costs, so private firms do not supply the good. Goods with this problem are called public goods. A public good has two features. First, it is non-excludable, which means that it is very difficult or impossible to keep people who do not pay from using it. Second, it is non-rival, which means that one person's use does not reduce what is left for others. National defense is the classic example. If the military protects the country from invasion, it protects everyone, whether or not they paid taxes, and one person's protection does not reduce yours. Other examples include streetlights, flood-control levees, and public health measures such as mosquito control.

Because private companies cannot easily charge for public goods, governments provide them and use taxes to pay for them. Taxes solve the free-rider problem by making everyone share the cost. The money that governments collect through taxes is called tax revenue. In fiscal year 2025, the federal government spent about $917 billion on national defense, roughly 13 percent of all federal outlays, as Figure 3.10 shows. States and cities also pay for public goods, such as the lighting of streets in Bakersfield and the levees that protect low-lying land.

Not everything that government provides is a pure public good. Public schools, for example, can exclude students who do not live in the district, and a classroom seat is used up when a student sits in it, so schools are not non-excludable or non-rival. Governments provide education because it has large benefits for the whole society, as the section on externalities will show. Economists therefore separate goods into four types, as Figure 3.9 illustrates, depending on whether people can be excluded and whether use by one person reduces what remains for others.

Key Concepts

Externalities: Costs and Benefits Beyond the Deal

Suppose that you sell lemonade at a stand near your home. You and your customers make a deal, and both of you gain. But what if the stand creates a mess of cups and sticky spills on your neighbor's lawn? Your neighbor did not agree to the deal and gets none of the benefit, yet pays the cost. Economists call a cost or a benefit that falls on someone who is not part of a transaction an externality.

A negative externality is a cost imposed on others. Pollution is the best-known example. A factory that makes a product may release smoke or chemicals into the air, and the people who live downwind pay in health problems, even though they never bought the product. In California's San Joaquin Valley, pollution from trucks, farm equipment, and other sources is a long-standing concern, and local, state, and federal agencies have set limits and run programs to reduce emissions. A positive externality is a benefit received by others. When you get an education, you benefit, and so do your future employers, customers, and community. When a homeowner gets a flu vaccination, neighbors are less likely to catch the flu.

Why do externalities cause market failures? Because the people making the decision count only their own costs and benefits. A factory deciding how much to produce adds up the costs of materials, labor, and equipment, but not the cost of the pollution. The price is therefore too low, and the factory produces more than is best for society. When a good creates positive externalities, the opposite happens. People deciding whether to get educated or vaccinated count only their own benefits, so they may choose too little of it.

Figure 3.11 shows the idea with hypothetical numbers. In the market, the quantity is 4 thousand tons at a price of $6, where demand meets the supplier's private cost. But if each ton also creates $2 of pollution cost for the community, the true cost to society is $2 higher at every quantity. At the quantity that is best for society, 3 thousand tons, the price is $7. The market produces too much of the product because the pollution cost is left out.

How Government Responds

Government has several tools for dealing with externalities. It can use regulation, setting rules such as limits on the amount of pollution a factory may release. It can use a tax on the harmful activity, which raises the private cost so that it matches the social cost; in Figure 3.11 a tax of $2 per ton would move the supply line up to the social cost line. It can allow the trading of pollution permits, in which businesses with lower cleanup costs sell unused permits to those with higher costs. And for positive externalities, it can use a subsidy: a payment or tax break that encourages the activity. Public schools, college grants, and tax credits for solar panels are examples.

Each tool has strengths and weaknesses. Regulations are simple to explain but may force every business to cut pollution by the same amount, even when some could cut it more cheaply. Taxes and permits use incentives and can reach the goal at lower cost, but they are harder to design. Subsidies encourage useful activities but require that taxpayers pay. And all of these tools require government to estimate costs and benefits that are difficult to measure.

The southern San Joaquin Valley illustrates how complicated these questions become. Kern County is a leader in agriculture, oil production, and energy from sun and wind, and these industries employ many people, yet each also creates costs for air, water, or land. Residents, businesses, and government agencies disagree about how to balance jobs and income with clean air and water, and the disagreements are part of normal democratic decision-making.

Key Concepts

Transfer Payments, Taxes, and the Safety Net

Markets reward people who have resources that others want to pay for. But some people, including children, older adults, and people with disabilities, cannot earn enough income from working. Others lose jobs when the economy slows or when a business closes. A society that depends only on markets would leave these people without help. Most Americans believe that government should provide a safety net, a set of programs that help people who cannot support themselves.

Many safety-net programs work through transfer payments, payments made without receiving a good or service in return. Social Security, for example, sends monthly payments to retired workers and their families, and unemployment insurance provides temporary income to people who lose their jobs. Public programs also provide goods directly, as when schools serve breakfast and lunch to students at a reduced price or for free. Social Security was the largest category of federal spending in fiscal year 2025, at about 22.6 percent of all federal outlays, as Figure 3.10 showed.

Supporters of the safety net argue that it helps families through hard times, helps children grow into productive adults, and keeps the economy steady by helping people continue to spend. Critics argue that taxes needed to pay for transfers can reduce the incentive to work and invest, and that programs can discourage people from taking jobs if benefits end too quickly when earnings rise. Economists try to measure these effects, but values also matter, because deciding how much help is the right amount depends on beliefs about fairness. Chapter 18 explores government revenue and spending in depth.

CASE STUDY

The United States:
Land of Entrepreneurs

The free enterprise system invites anyone to start a business. Millions have accepted the invitation, and many have not lasted. This case study asks you to weigh the opportunities and the risks, and to decide what the evidence shows.

Background

Working for Yourself

For many Americans, a job means working for an employer who provides a place to work, a paycheck, and benefits such as health insurance. But a large number of people choose a different path. They start their own businesses, as owners of a restaurant, a farm, a landscaping crew, a trucking company, or an online store. Some do it to pursue an idea. Some do it for more control over their time. Others begin because they cannot find a job that suits them.

The free enterprise system protects the freedom to try. The four economic freedoms from Section 1, open opportunity, legal equality, free contract, and the profit motive, give entrepreneurs the right to open, the right to compete on equal terms, the right to make deals, and the chance to keep what is left after costs are paid. What the system does not guarantee is success. Starting a business means investing money, time, and effort without knowing whether customers will come. A new owner may also have a choice of how to start: build an independent business from nothing, buy into a franchise with an established name and system, or work alone as a self-employed contractor.

Your task is to use the four documents below to answer a question. What are the opportunities and the risks of starting a business in the United States, and what choices do entrepreneurs make to manage them? The documents include a chart, a data table, a law, and a set of viewpoints. Remember that evidence rarely tells the whole story, and that you should always ask what a source can and cannot show.

THE INDEPENDENT OWNER
"I wanted to make my own recipes and keep my own name on the sign. Nobody told me how to run the place, and every dollar of profit is mine. The first year, though, I worked seven days a week and paid myself almost nothing. If the restaurant fails, I lose the savings I put into it. I accepted that risk because I wanted the freedom."
THE FRANCHISE OWNER
"I bought into a franchise because customers already knew the brand and the company taught me its system. It cost much more than starting alone, I pay fees every month, and I must follow the franchise rules about the menu and the look of the store. I gave up some freedom to lower my risk. THE SELF-EMPLOYED CONTRACTOR
"I do landscaping jobs by myself with a truck and a trailer. I have no employees and no store, so my costs are low and I decide which jobs to take. But I get no paid vacation or health insurance from an employer, and when work slows down in winter, I earn less. I like being independent, and I plan carefully for the slow months."

CHAPTER3

Chapter 3 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Advantages of the Free Enterprise System
The free enterprise system, or capitalism, rests on four freedoms: open opportunity, legal equality, free contract, and the profit motive. Competition pushes businesses to improve and keeps prices down, and entrepreneurs start businesses, take risks, and create jobs.
Section 2 · Property Rights and Incentives
Property rights are a bundle of rights to use, exclude, sell, and keep the benefits of resources. Owners have incentives to conserve and improve what they own. Resources that nobody owns can be overused, as the tragedy of the commons shows, but communities, rules, and ownership can all help.
Section 3 · How Free Enterprise Allocates Resources
Consumers steer production through consumer sovereignty, and prices act as signals and incentives. Higher prices encourage sellers to supply more and buyers to look for substitutes. Profits and losses move resources, and the circular flow model with government shows how money, goods, and taxes move through the economy.
Section 4 · Government and Free Enterprise
The United States has a modified free enterprise system. Government protects rights, provides public goods, addresses externalities, and funds a safety net. Countries differ in how much they tax and spend, and Americans debate where to draw the line.
