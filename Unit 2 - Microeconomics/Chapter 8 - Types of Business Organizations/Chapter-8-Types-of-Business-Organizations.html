In Chapter 7 you studied how many sellers a market has. In this chapter you will look inside the seller. Every business has to be organized in some legal form, and that form decides who owns the business, who makes decisions, who pays the taxes, and who is responsible if the business fails. Chapter 8 Key Concept

A business organization is a legal form of ownership for a business. The main forms are the sole proprietorship, the partnership, and the corporation. Each form trades off control, risk, taxes, and the ability to raise money. No form is best for every business.

Why the Concept Matters

Suppose you and a friend want to start a lawn-care business in Shafter. Will you each own half? If a customer's window is broken by a rock thrown from a mower, who pays? Key Concepts

Choosing a Form of Business

Every business in the United States has some legal form, and the form shapes its daily life. A business organization is the legal form that a business takes, which determines who owns it, who controls it, how it is taxed, and who is responsible for its debts. When you read about the roles of profit and competition in Chapter 7, you were reading about what businesses do. This chapter explains how they are put together.

Economists describe three main forms. The first is the sole proprietorship, owned by one person. The second is the partnership, owned by two or more people. The third is the corporation, a business that the law treats as a separate legal person. Each form has its own mix of advantages and disadvantages, and each person who starts a business weighs control, risk, taxes, and access to money before deciding.

An entrepreneur is a person who starts a business and takes on its risks in the hope of earning a profit. In Chapter 6 you saw that profit is the reward that draws entrepreneurs to produce what buyers want. Choosing a business form is one of an entrepreneur's first decisions, and standard 12.2.8 asks you to understand how profit works as the incentive that leads people to take such risks.

Four questions help to sort the forms from one another. Who owns the business, and how many owners are there? Who makes the decisions? Who pays the tax on the profit? And if the business fails, who is responsible for what it owes? As you read this chapter, notice that the answers change from one form to the next. The answers also connect to the central ideas of Unit 2. Competition, which you studied in Chapter 7, rewards businesses that serve buyers well, but a business can compete only if it is organized in a way that lets it raise money, hire workers, and survive its early mistakes. The legal form is a tool, and like any tool it fits some jobs better than others.

Key Concepts

One Owner, One Business

A sole proprietorship is a business owned and run by one person. It is the simplest and most common form. The owner decides what to sell, where to sell it, and what prices to charge. Hair stylists, farmers who rent a stall at a farmers market, house cleaners, tutors, rideshare drivers, and the person who runs a taco truck are often sole proprietors. In most places, no special paperwork is needed to start, other than a local business license or permit and any required tax registration.

The advantages are easy to see. A sole proprietor keeps all the profit after paying costs and taxes. The owner makes decisions quickly because no one else has to approve them. Start-up costs are low, because no lawyer is needed to form a separate organization, and the business can be shut down easily. The owner pays income tax on the profit as part of his or her personal tax return. The business itself does not pay a separate income tax.

The disadvantages are just as important. Most of them come from the fact that the owner and the business are, in the eyes of the law, the same. The owner has unlimited liability, which means that the owner is personally responsible for all debts and legal claims of the business. If a taquería owned by one person cannot pay its suppliers, the suppliers can go after the owner's savings, car, and even home. Raising money is also harder. A sole proprietor must rely on personal savings, loans, or help from family, since there are no outside owners to sell shares to. And because the business is one person, it may end when the owner retires, becomes ill, or dies.

Many sole proprietors earn a modest income, and many work alone with no employees. Others grow large enough to hire dozens of workers. At some point, growth may push an owner to switch to another form, because needs for money, protection, and managers outgrow what one person can supply.

Key Concepts

Numbers Versus Size

Figure 8.1 counts businesses, and by that count the sole proprietorship wins easily. The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) received about 31.0 million nonfarm sole proprietorship returns for tax year 2022, compared with about 7.0 million active corporation returns and about 4.6 million partnership returns for tax year 2023. The Small Business Administration's Office of Advocacy reaches a similar picture from a different source. Using Census Bureau data for 2022, it counts 36.2 million small businesses in the United States, which is 99.9 percent of all firms. About 29.8 million of them, or 82.3 percent, have no employees at all. The Census Bureau calls these nonemployer businesses. The owner is the whole workforce: a house cleaner, a rideshare driver, a freelance designer, a person who sells tamales on weekends. Many nonemployer businesses are run as sole proprietorships, because that form takes the least paperwork.

Counting businesses is only half of the story. Figure 8.2 shows the same three forms in two ways: by their share of the returns and by their share of receipts, which is the money that businesses take in from selling goods and services. Sole proprietorships make up about 72.7 percent of the returns but only about 3.4 percent of the receipts. Corporations make up about 16.5 percent of the returns but about 77.1 percent of the receipts. The gap tells us that the typical corporation is far larger than the typical sole proprietorship. If you divide total receipts by the number of returns, the average sole proprietorship took in about $67,000 and the average corporation took in about $6.7 million.

Be careful with those averages. A few giant corporations pull the corporate average up, so most corporations are much smaller than $6.7 million. The measures also differ a little: the sole proprietorship figure is business receipts, while the partnership and corporation figures are total receipts, which can include some income other than sales, such as interest. Treat Figure 8.2 as a rough picture of scale, not an exact accounting. The lesson is still clear. A form can be the most common and still account for a small part of all business activity, and that is why economists look at both the number and the size of businesses.

Key Concepts

Taxes and Paperwork

A sole proprietor does not file a separate income tax return for the business. Instead, the profit is added to the owner's other income and taxed on the owner's personal return, using a form called Schedule C. Economists call a business like this a pass-through business, because its profit passes through to the owner. This is simple, and it avoids the double taxation that you will study in Section 3.

A sole proprietor also pays self-employment tax. An employee pays Social Security and Medicare taxes together with the employer, and each pays half. A sole proprietor is both the worker and the employer, so the owner pays the whole amount. The IRS states that the self-employment tax rate is 15.3 percent, made up of 12.4 percent for Social Security and 2.9 percent for Medicare, and that the tax applies when net earnings from self-employment are $400 or more. Because no employer withholds tax from the owner's pay, sole proprietors usually make estimated tax payments during the year instead of waiting until April.

Paperwork is light but not zero. Many cities and counties require a business license or permit, and some trades, such as food service, require health inspections. A careful owner keeps business records separate from personal spending: a separate bank account, saved receipts, and a record of every sale. Good records make it easier to prepare taxes, to prove what the business earned when applying for a loan, and to show, if a dispute arises, what belonged to the business and what belonged to the owner. Remember that in a sole proprietorship the law does not separate the two, so careful records are the only separation that exists.

Farms, Food Trucks, and the Central Valley

Kern County is one of the most productive farm counties in the nation, and its farms show how varied small businesses can be. The 2022 Census of Agriculture counted 1,691 farms in Kern County on 2,399,512 acres, an average of 1,419 acres per farm, and the Census profile reports that 85 percent of the county's farms were family farms. Gross value counts what the crops sold for before the growers subtract the cost of water, labor, fuel, and fertilizer, so it is not the same as profit.

Figure 8.3 shows how different the farms are from one another. A total of 925 of the 1,691 farms sold $100,000 or more of products in 2022, which was about 55 percent of the farms. Away from the fields, you will find sole proprietors in nearly every neighborhood in Shafter and Bakersfield. A taquería run by one family, a mechanic who works out of a garage, a barber who rents a chair, and a house cleaner who finds customers by word of mouth all face the same choices as Marisol in the example above. Each owner decides what to charge, how many hours to work, and how much risk to accept. Each also keeps the profit that is left, which is exactly the reward that standard 12.2.8 describes as the incentive for entrepreneurs.

Key Concepts

Risk, Reward, and Staying Power

Starting any business is a gamble. The owner commits money and time before knowing whether customers will come. The Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) has tracked what happens to new businesses. Among private-sector business establishments that opened in March 2013, 79.6 percent were still operating a year later, 68.9 percent after two years, 50.6 percent after five years, and 34.7 percent after ten years. Figure 8.4 shows these numbers. In other words, about half of new establishments closed within five years, and roughly two out of three closed within ten.

Those numbers help explain why the form of a business matters. In a sole proprietorship, the owner bears the whole loss when a business closes, because of unlimited liability. They also help explain why profit is so important to the incentives that drive a market economy. Entrepreneurs accept a real chance of losing their savings in exchange for the chance to earn profit. When a business loses money year after year, the loss signals that the owner's time and money could be used better somewhere else, so resources move toward activities that buyers value more.

Read the data carefully, though. The BLS counts establishments, which are individual locations, and not whole companies, so a firm that closes one store and opens another is counted as a closure. A business that closes has not always failed. Some owners retire, sell, or move to better jobs. Still, the numbers show that staying power cannot be taken for granted, and that is one reason new owners think hard about how much of their own wealth they can afford to put at risk.

Madam C. J. WalkerBuilding a Business From One Product

Sarah Breedlove was born in Louisiana in 1867, a few years after the end of slavery. She was orphaned young and worked as a laundress for years. Later, she developed a line of hair-care products and began selling them herself. Like many entrepreneurs, she started small, with one owner, one product, and her own labor. She took the name Madam C. J. Walker for her business.

Walker did not rely only on stores. She trained women as sales agents, who sold her products and gave demonstrations. By the early 1910s her company, headquartered in Indianapolis from 1910, employed several thousand women as agents. The business gave many of them an income at a time when few jobs were open to Black women.

Her story illustrates the choices in this chapter. A business that begins as one person's effort can grow into a larger organization that needs managers, a factory, money to expand, and rules. It also shows the entrepreneur's role in a market economy: taking risks in the hope of profit and creating jobs in the process.

Key Concepts

Two or More Owners

A partnership is a business owned by two or more people who share the profits, the work, and the risks. Many law offices, medical practices, accounting firms, farms, and small shops are partnerships. In the IRS data in Figure 8.1, about 4.6 million partnership returns were filed for tax year 2023, and those returns represented about 30.2 million partners.

In a general partnership, every partner helps run the business and has unlimited liability. This means each partner can be held responsible for the debts of the whole business, including debts created by another partner. Suppose two friends start a food-delivery business and one of them signs a lease for an unneeded warehouse. If the business cannot pay the rent, the landlord can pursue either partner, or both.

In a limited partnership, there are two kinds of partners. At least one general partner runs the business and has unlimited liability. One or more limited partners invest money but do not take part in daily management, and in exchange their liability is limited to the amount they invested. A limited partnership is a way to attract investors who want a share of profits without the risk of unlimited liability.

Because partners can disagree, most partnerships begin with a written partnership agreement, a document that spells out how much each partner contributes, how profits and losses are shared, who makes which decisions, and what happens if a partner leaves. Without such an agreement, state law fills in the rules, and the result may not be what the partners expected.

Key Concepts

Who Is Counted as a Partnership?

When the IRS counts partnerships, it counts more than the traditional kind. For federal tax purposes, a business with two or more owners is generally treated as a partnership unless it chooses to be taxed as a corporation. The IRS says that a domestic LLC with at least two members is classified as a partnership. That means the partnership returns in Figure 8.1 include general partnerships, limited partnerships, and limited liability companies, the form you will study in Section 4.

Figure 8.5 shows how the 4,575,280 partnership returns for tax year 2023 divide among types of entities. About 72.7 percent were filed by LLCs, which is roughly 3.3 million returns. General partnerships were 11.9 percent of the returns (542,540 of them), and limited partnerships were 9.7 percent. The IRS reports that LLCs account for about 14.4 million of the 30.2 million partners, and that limited partnerships account for about 10.3 million partners, which shows that limited partnerships often pool the money of many investors. Together, all partnerships reported total receipts of about $12.0 trillion for 2023.

The lesson is that the old picture of a partnership as two friends who share a shop is only part of the story today. Many businesses that have partners, such as a medical office owned by three doctors or a farm owned by several family members, now choose to organize as LLCs because they want limited liability. General partnerships are fewer, but more than 540,000 of them filed returns for 2023, and some operate with no special papers at all, since two people who run a business together for profit can be treated as a general partnership even without a written agreement.

Key Concepts

How Partnerships Are Taxed

A partnership, like a sole proprietorship, is a pass-through business. The partnership itself does not pay federal income tax on its profit. Instead, it files an information return with the IRS that reports its income, deductions, and each partner's share, and each partner reports his or her share on a personal tax return and pays tax on it. This holds even if the partner never takes the money out of the business. A partner who is allocated $20,000 of profit owes tax on $20,000 even if the partnership keeps the cash to buy equipment.

The scale of this system is large. The IRS reports that partnerships had about $2.1 trillion in net income for tax year 2023 that passed through to their partners. Partners are not always individuals. Of the income allocated to partners, the IRS reports that $635.5 billion went to other partnerships, $583.1 billion to corporations, and $547.4 billion to individuals. That tells us that many partnerships are owned partly by other businesses, and that large pools of investment money use the partnership form.

Pass-through taxation avoids the double taxation of corporations, which you will study in Section 3. But it has a cost: partners owe tax on income they may not have received in cash, and general partners keep unlimited liability. Each owner has to weigh these facts when choosing a form.

Key Concepts

Partners as Agents

Two rules of partnership law surprise many new business owners. The first is that, in most states, each general partner acts as an agent of the partnership. This means that when one partner signs a contract or makes a purchase that fits the ordinary course of the business, the whole partnership is bound by it, even if the other partners never saw the document. If one partner of a landscaping partnership orders $12,000 of sod from a supplier, the supplier can collect from the partnership, and from the personal property of each general partner if the partnership cannot pay.

The second rule is that partners owe duties to one another. A partner is expected to act honestly and in the interest of the partnership instead of secretly competing with it or taking profits for himself or herself. In economic language, the partners trust one another to use shared resources well, and the law supports that trust because it is hard to check every decision.

These rules explain why a written partnership agreement matters. The agreement can say how big a purchase one partner may make alone, which decisions need a vote of all partners, and which can be made by majority. For example, partners might agree that any spending over $5,000 requires everyone's approval. Such rules do not remove the risk of partnership, but they reduce the chance that one partner's choices will surprise the others. When partners can choose partners they know well, a partnership can work smoothly for decades, and when they cannot, disputes can end the business.

Key Concepts

Strengths and Weaknesses

Partnerships have several advantages. Two or more people can combine their savings, which makes it easier to raise money than in a sole proprietorship, and banks may be more willing to lend to a business with several owners. Partners can specialize, with one handling sales and another handling the books. The business can draw on the skills of everyone. Like a sole proprietorship, a partnership usually does not pay a separate income tax; the profits pass through to the partners, who report their shares on their personal tax returns.

The disadvantages are also real. Partners may disagree on how to run the business. The unlimited liability of general partners can put every partner's personal property at risk because of another partner's mistakes. Profit has to be divided, so each partner keeps less than a sole proprietor would. And in many partnerships the business must be reorganized if a partner leaves or dies, since the agreement that created it has changed.

Consider the choice in terms of risk and reward. A partner in a law firm may earn a share of large profits, but also accepts responsibility. In a family farm partnership in Kern County, relatives may combine land, equipment, and labor, which spreads the risk of a bad crop year but also ties several households to one business.

Key Concepts

Family Partnerships in the Central Valley

Partnerships are common in agriculture and in the professions. In Kern County, where the 2022 Census of Agriculture reports that 85 percent of farms are family farms, it is easy to find farms owned by relatives who share land, equipment, and work. A father and two adult children might farm together as partners. One contributes land that the family has owned for decades, another contributes a tractor and sprayers, and a third contributes full-time labor. Their agreement might say how the harvest proceeds are divided and what happens to the land if one partner moves away.

Professional partnerships are different. Lawyers, accountants, architects, and doctors often practice together as partners or as LLCs. Each member brings clients and skills, and the group shares the cost of an office, equipment, and staff. Here the main benefit is specialization, with partners who focus on the tasks that they do best. The main risk is that a partner's mistake can affect the reputation, and sometimes the finances, of the entire group.

In both cases, the partners are betting that they can accomplish together what none of them could accomplish alone. That bet pays off when the partners combine different strengths, trust one another, and plan for the day when a partner wants to leave. Many family partnerships have lasted generations. Others dissolve over arguments that a written agreement could have settled in advance.

Key Concepts

When Partnerships End

A general partnership usually ends, or dissolves, when a partner leaves, retires, or dies, unless the partnership agreement says otherwise. Dissolution does not necessarily mean the business closes. The remaining partners may buy out the departing partner and carry on. When the agreement includes a buy-sell clause, it states in advance how the business will be valued and who has the right to buy the share of a partner who leaves. Without such a clause, partners may have to argue over what the business is worth.

If the business does close, the law has an order of payment. The partnership's assets are used first to pay outsiders who are owed money, such as suppliers and lenders. Next, the partners are repaid what they contributed, and anything left over is divided according to the agreement. If the assets are not enough to cover the debts, the general partners must pay the remainder from their own property, which is why the choice of partners is also a choice about risk.

Partnerships therefore carry a built-in lack of permanence. Compare this with a corporation, which can continue for many years while its owners and managers come and go. That difference is one reason growing businesses sometimes leave the partnership form, and you will see why in Section 3.

Key Concepts

A Business That Is Its Own Person

A corporation is a business organization that is treated by law as a separate legal person, apart from its owners. It can own property, sign contracts, borrow, sue, and be sued in its own name. Corporations are less numerous than sole proprietorships, but they are large. They include the manufacturers, retailers, banks, and technology companies that you know best. Figure 8.1 shows that about 7.0 million active corporate returns were filed for tax year 2023, most of them from small corporations, including about 5.4 million S corporations.

To form a corporation, the founders file papers, called articles of incorporation, with a state government and pay a fee. The corporation then sells ownership shares to raise money. A share of stock is a unit of ownership in a corporation, and a person who owns stock is a stockholder or shareholder. In Chapter 15 you will study how stocks are bought and sold in financial markets, and standard 12.2.9 asks you to understand the functions of those markets. For now, notice that selling stock lets a corporation raise large amounts of money from many people.

Stockholders do not run the corporation day to day. Instead, they elect a board of directors, a group that makes major decisions and hires the managers who handle daily operations. Each share usually carries one vote, so a person with more shares has more say. Stockholders earn money in two ways. A corporation may pay part of its profit to stockholders as a dividend, a payment per share. Or the price of the stock may rise, so a stockholder can sell it later for more than she paid.

Key Concepts

A Short History of the Corporation

Corporations are older than the United States. The Netherlands created the Dutch East India Company in 1602, and it is often described as the first company to issue stock that investors could trade. The idea was powerful: instead of one merchant risking everything on a voyage, many investors could each risk a small amount and share the rewards.

In the early United States, a corporation could be created only by a special act of a state legislature. By the end of the 1700s about 300 companies had been incorporated in the country, and most of them provided public services such as canals, bridges, and banks. Only eight were manufacturers. In 1811, New York passed a law that allowed manufacturers to incorporate with limited liability without a special act, and other states followed. By the middle of the 1800s, states began passing general incorporation laws, so that any group of founders could form a corporation by filing papers and paying a fee.

These changes made it much easier to organize businesses that needed large amounts of money. A railroad that had to lay hundreds of miles of track, or a factory that had to buy expensive machinery, could sell shares to many investors and could keep going as owners came and went. Today, the same legal ideas organize everything from a neighborhood family store that has incorporated to a company with hundreds of thousands of stockholders.

Key Concepts

Limited Liability and Its Costs

The key advantage of the corporation is limited liability: stockholders are not personally responsible for the debts of the corporation. Their loss is limited to the amount they paid for their stock. If a corporation fails, creditors may take its assets, but they may not take a shareholder's home or savings. Figure 8.8 uses a hypothetical example to show why this matters. Limited liability makes people willing to invest in businesses they will not manage.

Corporations have other advantages. They can raise large sums by selling stock, which helps them build factories, develop new products, and expand across the country. They can continue after an owner sells or dies, because ownership can be passed from one person to another by selling stock. And they can hire professional managers with special skills.

The disadvantages include cost and complexity. Forming a corporation takes legal work and fees, and corporations must follow many state and federal rules, including reporting requirements. Stockholders may have little control, since managers run the business. And corporations face double taxation: the corporation pays income tax on its profit, and stockholders pay income tax again on the dividends they receive. The federal corporate income tax rate is 21 percent. Figure 8.9 shows, with a hypothetical dividend tax rate, how a corporation's profit shrinks before it reaches an owner.

Some small corporations avoid double taxation by choosing to be taxed as an S corporation, which passes profits through to its owners the way a partnership does, if they meet limits on the number and type of shareholders. A large corporation with thousands of shareholders usually cannot do this.

Key Concepts

Who Really Controls a Corporation?

In a sole proprietorship, the owner makes the decisions and keeps the profit. In a large corporation, the owners and the decision makers are different people. Economists call this the separation of ownership and control. The stockholders own the corporation, but the managers make the daily decisions. This creates a problem that economists describe with the words principal and agent: the stockholders are the principals, who want the managers, their agents, to act in the stockholders' interest, but the managers may have interests of their own, such as higher pay or a bigger office.

Corporations have several tools to reduce the problem. The board of directors, elected by the stockholders, hires and can fire the top managers. Stockholders vote at an annual meeting, and many vote by proxy, which means they mail or submit their votes without attending. Pay is often linked to results, so that managers earn more when the stock price rises. Large investors, such as pension funds and mutual funds, hold big blocks of shares and can press managers to explain their decisions.

Small stockholders often have little reason to take part. Suppose you own 200 shares of a corporation that has 2 million shares. Your votes are 0.01 percent of the total. Researching every issue would take hours, and your vote would hardly matter, so many small owners simply sell if they are unhappy. Economists call this rational apathy. It is part of the trade-off of the corporate form: limited liability and easy transfer of ownership make it possible to raise huge sums, but they also leave stockholders with little day-to-day control.

Key Concepts

Raising Money: Stock, Bonds, and Retained Earnings

A corporation has three main ways to raise the money it needs to grow. The first is to sell new stock. Selling stock brings in money that does not have to be repaid, but it gives new owners a share of future profits and a vote. The second is to borrow by issuing bonds, which are loans from investors that the corporation promises to repay with interest. Bondholders are lenders, not owners, so they have no vote, but the corporation must make its payments even when profits are low. The third is to use retained earnings, which is profit that the corporation keeps instead of paying out as dividends.

Each choice has a trade-off. A fast-growing company with little profit may sell stock, because it has no profits to retain and does not want fixed interest payments. A mature company with steady earnings may borrow because interest on loans is a cost that lowers taxable profit, while dividends are not. A company that retains earnings avoids fees and gives up nothing, but its owners get no dividends that year and must hope the stock price rises.

These choices are part of what economists call financial capital, the money that a business uses to buy the physical capital it needs. In Chapter 15, you will study how stocks and bonds are bought and sold in financial markets, and why standard 12.2.9 asks you to understand what those markets do. For now, remember the main point: the corporate form is useful because it opens all three doors, while a sole proprietor usually has only savings and loans.

C Corporations, S Corporations, and California

The IRS sorts corporations into two main tax groups. A regular corporation, called a C corporation, pays the corporate income tax and then its stockholders pay tax on dividends, which is the double taxation you have studied. A small corporation may instead elect to be an S corporation, which passes its income to its shareholders to be taxed on their personal returns. The IRS says that to qualify, an S corporation must have no more than 100 shareholders, may not be owned by partnerships or corporations, and may have only one class of stock. For tax year 2023, the IRS counted 5,440,879 S corporation returns (Form 1120-S) and 1,525,514 regular corporation returns (Form 1120). That is about 3.6 S corporation returns for every regular one. The corporations that you see on the stock exchange are C corporations, but most corporations in the country are small ones.

California adds its own requirements. According to the Franchise Tax Board, the California income tax rate for C corporations, other than banks and financial institutions, is 8.84 percent, and every corporation that is incorporated, registered, or doing business in California must pay an $800 minimum franchise tax. The Secretary of State charges $100 to file articles of incorporation for a general stock corporation. These costs are small for a large company, but they matter to a new business with little income, and they are one reason that many small California businesses choose another form.

The overall picture is a set of trade-offs. Incorporating gives limited liability and the ability to raise money, but it costs fees, paperwork, and, for C corporations, a second layer of tax. An entrepreneur who wants to grow quickly and attract many investors may accept those costs. An owner who wants a simple business with a few partners may not.

Key Concepts

Combining the Best Features

Business owners who want limited liability without the double taxation of a corporation often choose a limited liability company (LLC). An LLC is a form of business that gives its owners, called members, limited liability like a corporation, while its profits are generally taxed once, on the members' personal tax returns, like a partnership. LLCs are simpler to run than corporations, with fewer formal rules, and they have become very popular with small businesses and professionals. As Section 2 showed, LLCs filed about 72.7 percent of all partnership returns for tax year 2023.

The IRS explains how an LLC is taxed. A domestic LLC with at least two members is classified as a partnership for federal income tax purposes, and an LLC with one member is treated as an entity that is disregarded as separate from its owner, so its income is reported on the owner's return. An LLC can also elect to be taxed as a corporation by filing a form with the IRS, which some owners do to gain tax advantages. The legal protection of limited liability comes from state law, which is why the rules for forming an LLC differ from state to state.

The LLC is a good example of how business law changes to meet needs. Entrepreneurs wanted a form that avoided the main drawbacks of the older forms, and states created it. Even so, an LLC has costs, as the next pages show for California. The best choice depends on the circumstances.

Franchises

Organizations That Do Not Aim for Profit

A nonprofit organization is an organization that is created to serve a public or member purpose rather than to earn profit for owners. Hospitals, private colleges, museums, churches, charities, food banks, and many youth sports leagues are nonprofits. Nonprofits may earn more money than they spend. But they cannot divide it among owners; the surplus must be used for the organization's mission. Many nonprofits are exempt from federal income tax if they meet IRS requirements, and donors may be able to deduct gifts.

Nonprofits rely on donations, grants, fees for services, and volunteers. A food bank in Kern County, for example, may receive donated food from farms and stores, money from donors, and many hours of volunteer labor. Economists note that nonprofits also face scarcity and must make choices, and that they compete for donors and workers. They respond to incentives, though the incentive is not profit but the mission and the people who support it.

Because nonprofits have no owners, a board of directors usually oversees them. The board's duty is to make sure that the money is spent on the mission, not to earn money for itself.

The biggest categories by net business volume were farm supplies such as fuel, fertilizer, and feed ($70.7 billion), grains and oilseeds ($67.7 billion), and dairy ($60.9 billion). Fruit and vegetable cooperatives had $8.5 billion in business, and nut cooperatives had $1.7 billion. The cooperatives also employed 189,764 people in 2024.

California is an important cooperative state. The USDA reported that 114 cooperatives operated in California in 2024, with net sales of $17.7 billion, which ranked third in the nation. Cooperatives such as Blue Diamond Growers, whom you will meet in the Case Study, give small growers a way to process, brand, and sell crops at a scale that one farm could not reach.

Cooperatives are not only for farmers. A credit union is a financial cooperative that is owned by its members, who are its depositors and borrowers, and any earnings go back to the members through lower loan rates and fees or higher savings rates. According to the National Credit Union Administration, there were 4,287 federally insured credit unions with 144.7 million members and $2.43 trillion in assets at the end of 2025. Many people in Kern County belong to a credit union without thinking of it as a business that they co-own. You will study credit unions further in Chapter 14.

Key Concepts

How a Patronage Refund Works

The key difference between a cooperative and a corporation is who receives the money left over at the end of the year. A corporation pays dividends to stockholders according to how many shares they hold. A cooperative usually returns its net earnings to members according to how much they used the cooperative. These returns are called patronage refunds. A grower who delivered 2 percent of a marketing cooperative's crop would receive 2 percent of the earnings that the cooperative decides to pay out, no matter how much stock he holds.

This rule changes incentives. A member earns more by using the cooperative more, and every member has a reason to want the cooperative to operate efficiently. Cooperatives often keep part of their earnings to build plants and equipment, and they track each member's share of that retained money. In the USDA's national totals, allocated equity of $30.8 billion and retained earnings of $29.2 billion made up the $60 billion of member equity in 2024, which shows how much of a cooperative's capital comes from its own members and from earnings kept in the business.

The patronage rule also has a cost. Because ownership is tied to use, a cooperative cannot easily raise money from outside investors who want a return but do not deliver crops. Members must supply most of the money, and when a poor year arrives, they may receive little or nothing back. That trade-off is exactly the one that the Case Study asks you to weigh.

Key Concepts

Putting It Together: Choosing a Form

How should an entrepreneur choose? Start with five questions. First, how much money is needed, and where will it come from? A sole proprietor draws on personal savings and loans, partners pool their money, and a corporation can sell stock. Second, how much risk is the owner willing to accept? Unlimited liability puts personal property at risk, while limited liability does not. Third, who will make decisions, and does the owner want to share control? Fourth, how will profit be taxed, once or twice? Fifth, what is the goal: profit for owners, service to members, or a public mission?

No form wins every comparison. A teenager who mows lawns on weekends can do very well as a sole proprietor. Two nurses who open a clinic with several investors may want an LLC. A company that needs hundreds of millions of dollars for new plants will probably be a corporation. A group of parents that wants to feed hungry families will form a nonprofit, and almond growers who want to bargain together may form a cooperative. Franchise contracts sit on top of these forms, because a franchisee is organized as one of the other types.

Notice what stays the same across all of these choices. Each form trades off control, risk, taxes, and the ability to raise money, and each owner weighs the trade-offs against the goals of the business. That is the reasoning that economists call a cost-benefit decision, and you will use it again in the chapters ahead.

CASE STUDY

Owned by the Growers:
The Almond Cooperative

California's almond growers farm thousands of orchards, but many sell their crop through a cooperative they own together. Is that a better way to organize a business than a corporation? The documents below let you weigh the evidence.

Background

When the Customers Are the Owners

An almond grower works for months to produce a crop, but selling it can be hard. Buyers are large processors and exporters, and one grower holds little bargaining power. In 1910, California almond growers formed the California Almond Growers Exchange so that they could market their crop together. In 1980 the organization officially became Blue Diamond Growers. Today the cooperative describes itself as 100 percent farmer owned, with nearly 3,000 grower-owners in California's Central Valley and almost 3,000 separate, independently run farms.

A cooperative like this one is owned by its members, the growers who deliver their almonds to it. The cooperative processes and sells the almonds, and the proceeds, after costs, go back to the growers. Because the growers are both suppliers and owners, the organization's goal is to serve them, not to maximize profit for outside stockholders.

A cooperative is not protected from hard times. For its fiscal year that ended August 25, 2023, Blue Diamond reported revenue of $1.33 billion, down about 17 percent from $1.6 billion the year before, and news coverage attributed the drop to weak nut prices and inflation. The same coverage reported that grower prices had stayed above roughly $2 a pound, the level needed to cover growers' expenses, until a bumper crop in 2020 pushed prices below it. In November 2025, at its 115th annual meeting, the cooperative announced plans to close its Sacramento manufacturing plant and to consolidate production at its Turlock and Salida plants, saying the change was aimed at building a stronger and more profitable cooperative.

As you read the documents, consider how a cooperative differs from a corporation in ownership, control, and the way that money is raised, and decide what kind of business each form fits best.

Chapter 8 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Sole Proprietorships
A sole proprietorship is owned and run by one person, who keeps all profit, makes all decisions, and has unlimited liability. It is the most common form (about 31.0 million returns in 2022) but accounts for a small share of receipts, and it is limited in raising money.
Section 2 · Partnerships
A partnership has two or more owners who share profit, work, and risk. General partners have unlimited liability; limited partners risk only their investment. A written partnership agreement spells out the rules.
Section 3 · Corporations
A corporation is a separate legal person owned by stockholders, who elect a board of directors. It offers limited liability and the ability to raise money by selling stock, but faces double taxation and more rules.
Section 4 · Other Business Organizations
Limited liability companies combine limited liability with single taxation. A franchise is a contract to use a company's name and methods. Nonprofits serve a mission rather than profit, and cooperatives are owned by their users.
