Unit Overview

The Big Picture

Every economy, from a family kitchen table in Shafter to the global economy, starts with the same problem. People want more than they can have. There is only so much water in the Kern River and the California Aqueduct, only so much land on the valley floor, only so many hours in a day, and only so many dollars in a paycheck. Economists call this fact scarcity, and it is the first big idea of this unit. Scarcity forces choices, and every choice has a cost. In Chapter 1, The Economic Way of Thinking, you learn to name that cost as an opportunity cost, to see how incentives push people toward one choice or another, and to use tools such as the production possibilities curve, tables, and graphs to study the trade-offs that a farm, a business, or a whole country faces.

Once you see that scarcity forces choices, a larger question appears. Who makes the choices? Chapter 2, Economic Systems, shows that societies have answered this question in very different ways. In a traditional economy, custom decides. In a command economy, a central authority decides what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. In a market economy, millions of buyers and sellers decide through voluntary exchange. In a mixed economy, which is what nearly every country actually has, private choices and government decisions share the work. You will compare these systems using evidence, including a striking case study of the Korean Peninsula, where one people split into two economies that answer the three basic questions in opposite ways.

Chapter 3, The American Free Enterprise System, brings the lens home. Kern County is a good place to study it. The same county contains packing houses and almond orchards, oil fields and wind turbines, taquerías and trucking companies, and a growing downtown in Bakersfield. Each of those businesses exists because someone had the freedom to start it, the right to own its equipment and land, and the hope of earning a profit. Chapter 3 explains how competition, property rights, prices, and profits and losses guide scarce resources to the uses people value most. It also shows why the United States is best described as a modified free enterprise system, in which government protects rights, provides public goods such as roads and public schools, and helps people through a safety net.

The three chapters build on each other. Chapter 1 supplies the problem and the vocabulary of choice. Chapter 2 compares the ways societies can respond. Chapter 3 studies the response the United States has chosen, and asks how well it works and where Americans disagree about it. By the end of the unit you should be able to look at a real Kern County decision, such as a grower deciding whether to replace an orchard, a family deciding whether to open a business, or a city deciding whether to spend on a new park, and explain it with the economic way of thinking.

Think Locally

As you read, look for the three questions in your own community. What does Kern County produce, and who decided? How is it produced, with which workers, land, water, and machines? And for whom: who buys the carrots, the crude oil, and the solar power? Every answer you find is the result of choices made under scarcity.

Big Ideas

  • Scarcity forces choices. Limited resources and unlimited wants mean no person or society can have everything.
  • Every choice has an opportunity cost. The real price of a decision is the next-best option you give up.
  • Societies answer the three basic questions in different ways. Custom, central planning, markets, or a mix: the system shapes freedom and living standards.
  • Property rights, prices, and profits steer resources. They give people information and reasons to conserve, improve, and produce.

Essential Questions

Why This Unit Matters

Imagine this. Your cousin Marisol has saved $9,000 and wants to open a taco truck near a Shafter packing house at shift change. She cannot afford a truck, a permit, ingredients, and a part-time helper all at once, so she must choose (scarcity). Every dollar spent on a bigger grill is a dollar not spent on advertising (opportunity cost). She has to check whether the city will let her park there, who owns the lot, and what the health department requires (property rights and government). She will set her prices by watching what nearby trucks charge and what workers will pay (prices as signals), and if the tacos sell, a rival truck will probably appear (competition).

In one weekend, Marisol meets nearly every idea in this unit. This unit gives you the vocabulary to describe what she is deciding, and the reasoning to decide what you would do in her place. (This scenario is hypothetical and was written for the textbook.)

Making Connections

How the Chapters Fit Together

From Chapter 1 to Chapter 2. Chapter 1 introduces the three basic economic questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. Chapter 2 asks who answers them. The production possibilities curve you learn in Chapter 1 is also a useful picture of the choice every economic system faces, because each system decides where on the curve to operate and whether to grow.

From Chapter 2 to Chapter 3. Chapter 2 introduces the features of a market economy, including private property rights, voluntary exchange, profit, and competition, and ends by showing that every real economy is mixed. Chapter 3 takes those features one at a time in the American setting, and adds the pieces that Chapter 2 only previews: prices as signals, profits and losses as incentives to enter or leave a market, and the government’s part in a modified free enterprise system.

Across all three chapters. Three ideas return again and again. Incentives explain why people act as they do. Trade-offs mean that every benefit has a cost. And evidence, in the form of data, documents, and photographs, is how economists test their claims. Each Case Study asks you to bring those ideas together: water in Kern County, the two Koreas, and American entrepreneurs.

Key Terms Preview

Sixteen Words to Know

These terms appear throughout the unit. Each is defined in plain English as the chapters define it, with the Spanish equivalent. You will meet each one again, highlighted, in its chapter.

CH. 1scarcity
The condition that exists because wants are unlimited and resources are limited.Spanish: escasez
CH. 1factors of production
The resources needed to produce goods and services: land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship.Spanish: factores de producción
CH. 1incentive
A benefit offered to encourage people to act in a certain way, or a cost imposed to discourage them.Spanish: incentivo
CH. 1opportunity cost
The value of the next-best alternative given up when a choice is made.Spanish: costo de oportunidad
CH. 1production possibilities curve
A graph that shows all the combinations of two goods that can be produced with a fixed amount of resources and technology.Spanish: curva de posibilidades de producción
CH. 2economic system
The organized way a society uses its scarce resources to answer the three basic economic questions.Spanish: sistema económico
CH. 2command economy
A system in which a central authority, usually the government, answers the three basic questions.Spanish: economía dirigida
CH. 2market economy
A system in which individual buyers and sellers, trading voluntarily, answer the three basic questions.Spanish: economía de mercado
CH. 2mixed economy
An economic system that combines private decisions by individuals and businesses with decisions made by government.Spanish: economía mixta
CH. 2private property rights
The rights of people and businesses to own, use, and sell resources and what they produce.Spanish: derechos de propiedad privada
CH. 2voluntary exchange
A trade in which both parties freely agree, each expecting to gain.Spanish: intercambio voluntario
CH. 3entrepreneur
A person who organizes resources to produce goods or services and takes the risk of profit or loss.Spanish: empresario
CH. 3tragedy of the commons
The overuse of a shared resource that nobody owns, because each user gains the full benefit while the costs are spread among all users.Spanish: tragedia de los comunes
CH. 3price signal
The information that a price gives buyers and sellers about how scarce a good is.Spanish: señal de precio
CH. 3externality
A cost or benefit of a transaction that falls on someone who is not a party to it.Spanish: externalidad
CH. 3public good
A good that is non-excludable and non-rival: no one can be kept from using it, and one person’s use does not reduce what is available to others.Spanish: bien público

Skills You Will Practice

Tools for Thinking Like an Economist

Reading graphs and modelsInterpret a production possibilities curve, a marginal benefit and marginal cost graph, and a circular flow model, and explain what a point or a shift shows (Chapters 1 to 3).
Reading data tables and chartsUse bar charts, line charts, and the “View data table” option on real data, such as California farm commodities, new business applications, and world exports as a share of world output, and always check the source line (Chapters 1 to 3).
Calculating opportunity costCompare alternatives in a decision-making grid and find the value of the next-best option given up (Chapter 1).
Thinking at the marginCompare the extra benefit and extra cost of one more unit to decide whether to do a little more or a little less (Chapter 1).
Separating positive and normative statementsTell testable statements of fact from value judgments about what should be done, which matters in the debate over government’s role (Chapters 1 to 3).
Comparing systems with evidenceUse a range of economic systems, freedom scores, and living-standard data to compare economies, including North Korea and South Korea (Chapter 2).
Integrating multiple sourcesCombine documents, data, and photographs in each Case Study to answer a question, and explain how the sources support or counter each other (Case Studies; RH.11-12.7).
Evaluating Counter-ArgumentsBuild a claim, state the strongest Counter-Argument, and explain which one the evidence supports (Case Studies and Chapter 3 Section 4).

Connecting Across the Course

Look Back and Look Ahead

LOOK BACK

You already use the ideas in this unit. Every time you chose between working a shift and studying, or between saving and spending, you faced scarcity and paid an opportunity cost. If you have studied United States history, you have also seen economic systems in action: how people in the United States organized farming, factories, and trade, and how the Cold War divided the world between market economies and planned economies. Chapter 2 reconnects with that story through the division of Korea in 1945.

Think back to a time you set a price, bought something because it was on sale, or started a small business such as selling snacks or mowing lawns. You were already taking part in the system you are about to study.

LOOK AHEAD

Unit 2, Microeconomics, opens with Chapter 4, Demand, and Chapter 5, Supply. Chapter 3 introduced prices as signals and substitutes; Unit 2 shows exactly how buyers and sellers respond to price, then turns to Demand, Supply, and Prices (Chapter 6), Market Structures (Chapter 7), Types of Business Organizations (Chapter 8), and The Role of Labor (Chapter 9).

Later units return to the questions raised here. Unit 4 measures the whole economy, including GDP in Chapter 16, and Unit 5 examines how government raises and spends money, the debate you meet at the end of Chapter 3.
