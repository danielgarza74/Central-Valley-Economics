Unit Overview

Every economy you have studied so far has had a border around it. In Unit 1 you learned that scarcity forces choices. In Units 2 through 5 you watched prices coordinate buyers and sellers, followed money through banks and the Federal Reserve, and saw how governments tax, spend, and try to steady the economy. Unit 6 removes the border. When a Kern County grower sells pistachios to a buyer overseas, or a Bakersfield family buys a phone assembled on another continent, the same ideas of opportunity cost, incentives, and prices are at work, but two new things appear: national borders and different currencies.

Chapter 21, International Trade, explains why nations trade at all. The answer is comparative advantage: a country gains by specializing in what it makes at the lowest opportunity cost and trading for the rest, even if another country is better at making everything. The chapter then turns to the tools governments use to shape trade (tariffs, quotas, subsidies, and embargoes), the exchange rates that translate one country’s money into another’s, and the trade agreements and institutions, such as the World Trade Organization, that set the rules. The chapter ends with a Case Study of how tariffs and retaliation affected Kern County’s almond and pistachio growers, using the evidence and the competing views of farmers, consumers, and policymakers.

Kern County is a good place to study this unit. Almonds were California’s leading agricultural export in 2024, at about $4.95 billion, and pistachios were second at about $2.93 billion; both are Kern County crops, and many are packed in places like Shafter and Wasco. Citrus, grapes, and other crops also leave the Central Valley for buyers abroad. Meanwhile, much of what fills local stores comes the other way, through the Port of Oakland to the north and the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach to the south. In 2025, the Port of Los Angeles alone handled about 10.2 million twenty-foot container units. A change in a tariff or in the value of the dollar can therefore show up in a Shafter packing house, a Bakersfield store, or a family budget within weeks.

Chapter 22, Issues of Economic Development, widens the lens from how countries trade to how they grow. Worldwide, average income per person was about $13,433 in 2024, but the high-income group averaged $49,418 and the low-income group only $765. Why such a gap? Chapter 22 shows how economists measure development with income, purchasing power parity, and the Human Development Index; how productivity depends on human capital, physical capital, technology, and institutions such as property rights and the rule of law; how countries finance development through saving, foreign investment, aid, loans, and remittances; and how former command economies moved toward markets. Its Case Study follows Vietnam, where income per person rose from about $2,500 in 1990 to about $14,400 in 2024 (in 2021 dollars). The two chapters fit together: trade is one of the ways poorer countries can grow, and development changes who we trade with. Both chapters ask you to weigh evidence, because the experts do not always agree.

Here is how the two chapters connect. Chapter 21 asks what happens when countries exchange goods, services, and money. Chapter 22 asks what lets a country produce enough to take part in that exchange on good terms. A country with skilled workers, modern equipment, reliable courts, and open markets has more to sell, and it earns the foreign currency it needs to buy what it lacks. A country without those foundations may export only a few raw products and import almost everything else. Notice that Chapter 21 describes how exchange rates, tariffs, and trade agreements affect a country that is already trading, while Chapter 22 explains why the starting points differ so much, and why some governments chose to open their economies, some chose to protect them, and others did both at different times.

As you read, practice three habits of mind. First, ask who gains and who loses from every policy, because the answer is rarely “everyone” or “no one.” Second, ask compared with what?, because a price, a trade deficit, or an income figure means little until you know the alternative. Third, ask what does the evidence show, and what does it not show?, because one case, such as a single country’s reforms or one year of tariff data, can illustrate an idea without proving it. These habits matter well beyond this course: you will vote on trade policy, shop in a global market, and may someday work for a company, farm, or government that depends on it.

Weighing a Contested Question: Should the Government Limit Imports?

Few trade questions are more debated than tariffs and other trade barriers. This unit asks you to understand the best case that each side makes, so that you can judge the evidence for yourself.

THE CASE FOR TRADE BARRIERSSupporters argue that barriers can:
  • protect jobs and communities that depend on industries facing foreign competition;
  • keep production of goods considered vital to national security at home;
  • answer practices they consider unfair, such as dumping;
  • raise revenue for the government.
COUNTER-ARGUMENT: THE CASE FOR FREER TRADESupporters argue that barriers:
  • raise prices for consumers and for businesses that use imported parts;
  • invite retaliation that harms exporters, such as California farmers;
  • shelter inefficient producers and give up the gains from comparative advantage;
  • can be replaced by help for workers, such as job training.

Chapter 21, Section 2 and the Case Study give the evidence on both sides. Economists agree that trade creates winners and losers inside each country; they disagree about how much protection, if any, is worth its cost.

A Second Debate: Should a Developing Country Open Its Economy to Trade?

THE CASE FOR OPENINGSupporters argue that trade lets a country use its resources better, brings new ideas and technology, and that the countries that have grown fastest are mostly those that traded more. Several East Asian economies grew quickly after they began selling manufactured goods to the world.
COUNTER-ARGUMENT: THE CASE FOR CAUTIONCritics note that trade can hurt workers and firms that face new competition, that countries depending on a few raw materials are exposed when prices fall, and that rich countries have sometimes kept their own markets closed to goods poor countries make well. Most economists take a middle view: openness helps when schools and strong institutions help workers and firms adjust.

Chapter 22, Section 2 (“Openness to Trade”) examines this question. You will be asked to write one piece of evidence for a claim and a Counter-Argument against it.

Big Ideas

  1. Trade creates gains from specialization. Countries benefit when each produces what it makes at the lowest opportunity cost, but trade also creates winners and losers.
  2. Trade policy is a set of trade-offs. Tariffs, quotas, and subsidies help some groups and cost others, and they can invite retaliation.
  3. Money links economies. Exchange rates and the trade balance connect the value of the dollar to prices, jobs, and export sales.
  4. Rules and borders shape trade. Agreements, global institutions, and supply chains show both the reach and the limits of national sovereignty.
  5. Development is about people. Countries grow when they build human capital, physical capital, technology, and strong institutions, and economists debate the best path.

Essential Questions

  1. How can two countries both gain from trade, even if one is better at making everything?
  2. What tools do governments use to limit trade, and who gains and loses from each?
  3. How do exchange rates and the trade balance connect the dollar to prices that growers and shoppers face?
  4. How do agreements, borders, and the World Trade Organization shape what a country can do?
  5. Why are some countries rich and others poor, and what can a country do to develop?

You should be able to answer all five by the end of the unit.

WHY THIS UNIT MATTERS Imagine it is July and you are working a summer shift at a packing house in Shafter, boxing pistachios for export. At lunch you scroll through your phone, which was assembled overseas, and see that a foreign government has placed a new tax on California nuts. Your hours are cut the next week. The same news report says the dollar has gotten stronger, so the sneakers you wanted are cheaper but your boss says overseas buyers are ordering less. Your aunt, who sends money every month to relatives in Mexico, asks whether the exchange rate is helping or hurting. Is a tax on shoes good for American shoemakers? Is the foreign tax on nuts fair? Who gains and who loses? By the end of this unit you will be able to answer questions like these with evidence.

Skills You Will Practice

Comparative advantage arithmetic
Opportunity cost = what you give up ÷ what you gain. In Chapter 21 you will find that Valleyland gives up 2 crates per tablet and Harborland gives up 1, then test whether a trade at 1.6 crates per tablet leaves both better off. You will also work with tariff revenue = tariff per unit × quantity imported.
Reading trade and exchange-rate data
Foreign price = domestic price × exchange rate (for example, $10 × 18.57 pesos per dollar). Trade balance = exports − imports, for goods and for services. You will read line charts of pesos per dollar and the dollar index, and tables of trading partners and deficits.
Development indicators
Per capita = total ÷ population. Percent difference and percent change compare countries and years. You will read tables of GNI per person, Human Development Index scores, schooling, and poverty rates, and use evidence from several sources to answer a question.

Look Back

Unit 1 (Chapters 1–3). Scarcity and opportunity cost (Chapter 1) are the engine behind comparative advantage. Economic systems and the South Korea story (Chapter 2) return in the development chapter, and secure property rights (Chapter 3) are one of the institutions development needs.

Unit 4 (Chapters 14–17). Banks and financial markets connect savers and borrowers, which is how countries finance development. Gross domestic product (Chapter 16) is the starting point for measures such as GNI.

Unit 5 (Chapters 18–20). Tariffs are a tax and a source of federal revenue, as in Chapter 18, and the Federal Reserve’s interest-rate decisions (Chapter 20) can affect the value of the dollar.

Course Wrap-Up: Bringing the Six Units Together

Unit 6 completes the course. Each unit answered a different part of one question: how do people, businesses, and governments make choices when resources are scarce?

  1. Unit 1, Foundations of Economics: scarcity, opportunity cost, and economic systems.
  2. Unit 2, Microeconomics: demand, supply, prices, market structures, businesses, and labor.
  3. Unit 3, Consumer and Personal Finance: budgets, credit, big purchases, and living on your own.
  4. Unit 4, Money and the Macroeconomy: money, banking, financial markets, and economic indicators.
  5. Unit 5, Government and Policy: taxes, spending, fiscal policy, and the Federal Reserve.
  6. Unit 6, The Global Economy: trade, exchange rates, and development.
