Concept Review

Chapter 10 taught you to plan with a budget, Chapter 11 showed what credit costs, and Chapter 12 showed how to compare big purchases and protect them with insurance. All of those chapters assumed that you have income. This chapter is about where the income comes from and what happens to it before you spend it. You will study how workers choose careers and find jobs, why some jobs pay more than others, how a pay stub turns gross pay into net pay, how income taxes and payroll taxes work, how to read the main tax forms, and how to prepare for a future that includes retirement. These are the skills that turn a diploma into independence.

Chapter 13 Key Concept

Your net income, the money you actually take home, is what is left after taxes and other deductions are taken from your gross income. Choosing a career, comparing the total compensation of job offers, understanding withholding and taxes, and starting to save for the future are the foundations of financial independence.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine that you are 18 and have just graduated from Shafter High School. You take a full-time job at a warehouse on the edge of Bakersfield for $18.50 an hour. You do the math: 80 hours every two weeks means $1,480. Then the first paycheck arrives, and the deposit in your bank account is $1,202.75. Where did the other $277.25 go? Some went to taxes that pay for roads, schools, and Social Security. Some went to a health insurance premium. Some went to a state disability program that you did not know you were paying into. A worker who understands the pay stub can check that it is correct, plan a budget with the real number, and make smart choices about benefits. A worker who does not may be surprised, and may miss chances to save. This chapter teaches you to read the stub and to plan the road beyond it.

Key Concepts

Why Work? Income, Opportunity Cost, and Choices

For most people, work is the main source of income, which makes it the foundation of every other topic in this unit. The budget you built in Chapter 10 starts with income, the credit you studied in Chapter 11 depends on a record of income and repayment, and the big purchases in Chapter 12 depend on the ability to pay. Work also gives people more than money. It gives a sense of purpose, skills, and connections. But the main economic point is simple: the money you earn is the price that employers pay for your time and skills, and your time is limited. Every hour that you spend at work is an hour that you do not spend on school, family, or rest, which is the opportunity cost of work that you learned about in Chapter 1.

Chapter 9 explained that labor is a factor of production, and that wages are determined in a labor market, where workers supply their time and employers demand it. This chapter takes the worker's point of view. A wage is a payment for labor, usually calculated by the hour, such as $18.50 an hour. A salary is a fixed payment for a period, such as $52,000 a year, no matter how many hours are worked in a given week. A worker who is paid an hourly wage and works 40 hours a week for 52 weeks works about 2,080 hours in a year. So an hourly wage of $18.50 comes to $38,480 a year before taxes, and a salary of $52,000 equals about $25.00 an hour (52,000 ÷ 2,080). Knowing how to convert between the two lets you compare offers.

Not all income is the same as take-home pay. California's personal finance requirement asks students to understand the factors that affect net income, which is the amount left after taxes and deductions. Several things affect it. The wage or salary comes first, and then the number of hours. Overtime pay, tips, and bonuses add to it. Taxes and deductions reduce it, as you will see in Section 2. Finally, benefits that the employer provides, such as health insurance, are part of your pay even though they do not appear in your take-home pay, as you saw in Chapter 12. This section looks at how to choose a career and find a job. Section 2 follows the money from your paycheck to your tax return.

Key Concepts

Why Do Jobs Pay Different Amounts?

A restaurant cook and a registered nurse both work hard, but they do not earn the same wage. The California standards ask students to discuss wage differences among jobs and professions using the laws of demand and supply and the concept of productivity. Economists have four main explanations that fit together. The first is demand and supply. A job that few people are able to do, but that employers need, will pay more, because the supply of qualified workers is small compared with the demand for them. A job that many people can do will pay less, unless the demand for workers is very high. A wage is a price, and like any price it rises when demand grows or supply falls.

The second explanation is productivity, the amount of output that a worker produces in an hour. An employer will not pay a worker more than the value of what the worker adds. If a worker helps a business earn $24 an hour in revenue, an employer who pays $30 an hour will lose money, but an employer who pays $15 will make a profit and may need to raise the wage if other employers compete for the worker. Education, training, experience, and tools make workers more productive, which is why they raise wages. In Chapter 12 you saw that workers with more education earn more, on average, because they have more human capital. A worker who learns to operate a skilled machine can earn more because each hour of work produces more value.

The third explanation is compensating differentials, extra pay for jobs that are more dangerous, more unpleasant, or have an inconvenient schedule. An oil field worker in Kern County who works outdoors in heat or at night may earn more than a worker in a similar job with an easier schedule. The fourth explanation is the location and the local labor market. A job that pays one wage in Bakersfield may pay a different one in the Bay Area, partly because the cost of living and the number of workers and employers differ. In September 2026, the California Employment Development Department reported that the unemployment rate in Kern County was 7.9 percent, compared with 5.1 percent for California as a whole. A higher unemployment rate means that there are more job seekers for each opening, and that can limit wage growth, although the effect varies by industry and season. Agriculture, which hires many seasonal workers, is one reason that Kern's unemployment rate moves through the year.

EXAMPLEWhy a Job Pays What It Pays

Key Concepts

A Changing Labor Market

The jobs that your grandparents had are not the same as the jobs that exist today, and the jobs that you will do in 2040 may not exist yet. The California standards ask students to describe the current economy and labor market, including the types of skills that workers need, the effects of rapid technological change, and the impact of international competition. Technology changes the kinds of work that people do. Automated machines have reduced the number of workers needed for some tasks in factories and warehouses, and software tools now handle parts of work that people once did by hand. At the same time, technology creates new jobs, such as installing and repairing the equipment itself. International competition changes the labor market too. When a product can be made more cheaply abroad, the U.S. may import it, and workers in that industry may need to switch to other jobs. You will study these effects in Chapter 21.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) publishes projections of which occupations will grow fastest. In its projections for 2025 to 2035, the fastest-growing occupation is nurse practitioner, with projected growth of 41 percent, followed by solar photovoltaic installer at 37 percent, data scientist at 35 percent, and wind turbine service technician at 30 percent. These numbers appear in Figure 13.2. Two of the four are energy jobs that connect directly to Kern County, which has large wind farms in the Tehachapi area and a growing solar industry in the Central Valley and the desert. Others are in health care, where an aging population increases demand. The BLS reported 2025 median pay of $64,120 for wind turbine service technicians and $53,140 for solar installers. A worker with a certificate or an associate degree, instead of a bachelor's degree, may be able to qualify for such jobs, which connects to the pathways that you studied in Chapter 12.

A projection is not a guarantee. A fast-growing occupation may start with a small number of workers, so a large percentage growth may still mean few openings. Projections can change with the economy, technology, and government policy. The lesson for a young worker is to stay flexible. Skills that apply to many jobs, such as reading and writing clearly, working with numbers and computers, solving problems, and working on a team, help workers adapt when conditions change. Many employers say that these "soft skills" are among the hardest to find. Since people may change jobs and even careers several times, the ability to keep learning may be the most valuable skill of all.

Key Concepts

Choosing a Career Path

How should a high school student choose a career? Economists suggest a simple framework: match your interests, your strengths, and the demand for the work. Interests keep you motivated. Strengths make you productive. Demand makes sure that employers will pay for your skills. A career that fits all three is more likely to be satisfying and well paid. A student who loves video games but has no interest in programming or design may find that the interest does not lead to a job, while a student who likes working with tools and machines may find strong demand in Kern County's energy, agriculture, and construction industries.

There are many pathways. A four-year degree prepares students for professions such as teaching, engineering, and accounting. A community college certificate or associate degree, which you studied in Chapter 12, prepares students for jobs such as dental hygienist, electrician's assistant, and many technician roles. Apprenticeships combine paid work with classroom training, and they exist in construction trades, manufacturing, and other industries. Military service offers training and benefits. And some students go directly into the workforce and build skills on the job. California's personal finance requirement asks schools to teach about career technical pathways and apprenticeships because they lead to good jobs without requiring a four-year degree. A student can also combine paths, for example by starting in a job and taking classes in the evening.

Researching a career means finding the facts. The BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook lists, for hundreds of occupations, the typical education, the median pay, the number of jobs, and the projected growth. The state's CalJOBS system and your school counselor can help. The best sources of information are people. Ask someone who works in the career what a typical day is like, what they would do differently, and how they got started. Job shadowing, which means following a worker for a day, and internships let you see the work firsthand. Information gathered in this way is a low-cost way to reduce the risk of choosing the wrong career, which is exactly the problem of imperfect information that you saw in Chapter 12.

Key Concepts

Finding a Job

Once you have a goal, the next task is to find openings. Figure 13.1 shows the steps of a job search. The first is to look for openings. Many jobs are posted on employer websites and on job boards such as the state's CalJOBS system. Many others are never posted, and are filled through networking, the practice of building and using personal connections, such as relatives, teachers, coaches, neighbors, and former coworkers. Many workers report that they found their jobs through people they know. Telling the people around you what you are looking for costs nothing. Your school's career center and local organizations such as the America's Job Center of California can also help with openings, résumés, and training.

The second step is to prepare your application materials. A résumé is a one-page summary of your education, work experience, skills, and activities. For a first job, it can include volunteer work, school clubs, sports, and class projects. Use clear headings and action verbs such as "organized," "operated," and "served," and where possible include numbers, such as "served about 60 customers per shift." Check for spelling errors, because one mistake can make an employer doubt your attention to detail. A cover letter or an email introduces you and explains why you want the job. A reference is a person who can speak about your work and character, such as a teacher, coach, or former supervisor. Ask for permission before you list someone, and give them a copy of your résumé.

The third step is the application and the interview. An application asks for information such as your name, address, work history, and availability, and it must be accurate and complete. In an interview, the employer wants to know whether you are reliable, whether you can do the work, and whether you will fit in. Prepare by learning about the business, practicing answers to common questions such as "Tell me about yourself" and "Why do you want this job?", and preparing two or three questions to ask. Arrive early, dress neatly, listen carefully, make eye contact, and thank the interviewer. Afterward, send a short thank-you note or email. When a job is offered, ask for the details in writing: the pay, hours, start date, and benefits. Then you are ready to compare.

Key Concepts

Comparing Offers: Total Compensation

When you have an offer, the hourly wage is the first thing that you notice, but it is not the whole picture. Total compensation is the full value of what an employer gives a worker for his or her labor: wages or salary, plus the value of employee benefits such as health insurance, retirement contributions, paid time off, and training. In Chapter 12 you saw that the average employer paid $8,132 a year toward single health coverage in 2026, according to KFF. For a worker, that is real value, even though it does not appear in the paycheck, because without it the worker would have to buy coverage separately. Other benefits include paid vacation and sick leave, retirement plans with an employer match, tuition help, and scheduling flexibility.

Figure 13.3 compares two hypothetical offers. Job A pays $19.00 an hour and offers no benefits. Job B pays $17.50 an hour, includes the average employer share of health coverage, and offers a 401(k) match of 3 percent of wages, which means that the employer adds $1 for every $1 the worker contributes, up to 3 percent of pay. At 2,080 hours, Job A's wages come to $39,520. Job B's wages are $36,400, but when the health share of $8,132 and the match of $1,092 are added, the total is $45,624, which is $6,104 more than Job A. Job B's offer is equivalent to a wage of $21.93 an hour (45,624 ÷ 2,080). The comparison does not mean that Job B is always better. A worker who already has coverage through a parent, or one who needs more cash now, may value the higher wage. But the example shows why it is wise to ask about benefits.

Other factors also matter. Hours and schedule: Is the schedule predictable, or does it vary from week to week? Are there nights and weekends? Location and commuting costs: A job that is far away adds fuel, vehicle wear, and time; in Chapter 12 you saw how large the total cost of a vehicle can be. Opportunities to advance: Will the employer train you, promote from within, or help pay for school? Job security: Is the work seasonal? Many jobs in Kern County's agricultural industry are seasonal, which means that income may be uneven through the year and a worker needs a plan for the slow months. Finally, count the non-monetary benefits, such as a good supervisor, a safe workplace, and work that you find meaningful. Economists note that people respond to both monetary and nonmonetary incentives, which is an idea from the California standards and from Chapter 1.

Key Concepts

Job Scams and Worker Rights

Not every job offer is real. In Chapter 11 you saw that fraud is a growing problem, and fake job offers are a common type of scam. A scam often promises high pay for little work, asks for money up front for training, equipment, or a background check, offers a job without an interview, or sends a check and asks you to send part of the money back, as in the fake check example that you studied in Chapter 11. A legitimate employer does not ask you to pay to be hired. Be careful about giving out your Social Security number or bank account number before you have a written offer from an employer that you have checked. Search for the company's name and the word "scam," look for a real address and phone number, and ask a trusted adult. If it seems too good to be true, it probably is. You can report scams to the Federal Trade Commission at ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

Workers also have rights. California's minimum wage in 2026 is $16.90 an hour, and some cities and some industries have higher minimums. Most hourly workers are entitled to overtime pay when they work beyond certain hours, and California law requires employers to provide paid sick leave. Employers must pay workers on a regular schedule, provide a written pay statement, and follow safety rules. Workers under 18 must follow special rules on hours and on the kinds of work they can do, and they may need a work permit from the school. A worker who believes that the employer has broken the law can contact the California Labor Commissioner's Office. These rights are part of the history of the labor movement that you studied in Chapter 9. Knowing them helps a young worker spot an employer who is cutting corners.

Finally, keep records. Save your offer letter, your pay stubs, and a record of the hours you worked. If you ever have a dispute about wages, these documents are your evidence. And before your first day, you will complete two forms that are required for every employee in the United States: the W-4, which tells your employer how much income tax to withhold, and the I-9, which shows that you are legally allowed to work. You will learn about the W-4 and other tax forms in Section 2.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEA Year-End Bonus by Law: Mexico's Aguinaldo

Many families in Kern County have ties to Mexico, where the law sets rules about pay that differ from those in the United States. Under Mexico's Federal Labor Law, employers must pay workers an annual bonus called the aguinaldo before December 20 of each year. According to Mexico's federal labor protection agency, PROFEDET, the minimum is 15 days of salary. Workers who have not worked a full year receive a proportional share. Independent contractors are generally not entitled to it unless a relationship of subordination exists.

In the United States, an employer does not have to pay a year-end bonus, although many do. The difference shows that the benefits in total compensation are shaped by law, culture, and bargaining between workers and employers. A worker who moves between countries needs to ask what benefits come with the wage. The aguinaldo is also an example of how a rule that guarantees a benefit can raise the cost of hiring, which employers may weigh when they decide how many workers to hire.

Putting It Together

A job is a trade: you give time and skills, and the employer gives total compensation. Wages differ because of supply, demand, and productivity, and the labor market keeps changing, so the best protection is to keep learning. When you search for a job, prepare carefully, gather information, and compare offers by what you will actually receive. Key Concepts

From Gross Pay to Net Pay

Look at the first paycheck of the worker in this chapter's opening scenario. The job pays $18.50 an hour for 80 hours in a two-week pay period, so the employer owes gross pay of $1,480, the total earned before any deductions. But the amount deposited in the bank is only $1,202.75. That amount is net pay, also called take-home pay: gross pay minus all deductions. The difference of $277.25 is 18.7 percent of the gross pay. Where does it go? The answer is on the pay stub, the statement that comes with every paycheck, which California requires employers to provide. California's personal finance requirement asks students to learn to read a pay stub, because errors happen, and because the stub is the best way to understand your real income.

A pay stub usually lists the pay period, the hours worked, the pay rate, gross pay for the period and for the year to date, each deduction, and net pay. In this hypothetical example, the first deduction is $38.00 for the worker's share of a health insurance premium. Because the plan is a pre-tax plan, this amount is subtracted before payroll and income taxes are calculated, so the worker's taxable wages for the period are $1,442. Then come the taxes. Social Security tax is 6.2 percent of wages, which is $89.40. Medicare tax is 1.45 percent, which is $20.91. Together these two taxes are called FICA taxes, from the Federal Insurance Contributions Act, and they total 7.65 percent. The employer pays a matching 7.65 percent on top of the worker's wages. California State Disability Insurance (SDI) is 1.3 percent in 2026, or $18.75; it pays benefits to California workers who cannot work because of a non-work illness or injury, or who take paid family leave. Federal income tax withholding is $89.19 in this example, and California income tax withholding is estimated at $21.00. The total is $277.25, and the net pay is $1,202.75. Figure 13.5 shows these amounts.

Withholding is the amount that the employer subtracts from each paycheck and sends to the government as an advance payment of the worker's income tax. How much is withheld depends on the information that the worker gives on Form W-4, the Employee's Withholding Certificate, which includes the worker's filing status and other information, and on the IRS withholding tables. Withholding is an estimate. If too much is withheld during the year, the worker receives a refund when filing a tax return. If too little is withheld, the worker owes the difference. The system is called pay as you go, because the government collects tax during the year, not all at once in April.

There are some taxes that you pay but never see on your stub. Employers pay unemployment insurance taxes and workers' compensation premiums on your behalf. In California, the employer pays the unemployment insurance tax on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages each year, at a rate that depends on the employer's history. These costs are part of the true cost of hiring a worker, and they are one reason that a business may hire fewer workers when taxes rise. And you also pay sales tax each time that you buy goods, which does not appear on the stub but affects how far your net pay goes. Net pay is the number to use in your budget from Chapter 10, because it is the money you can actually spend.

Key Concepts

Why Do We Pay Taxes?

Few people like to pay taxes, but most people like what taxes pay for: roads, schools, police and fire departments, national defense, parks, and courts. A tax is a required payment to a government. Economists explain taxes with the idea of public goods, which are goods that benefit everyone and that private businesses have difficulty providing, such as national defense and street lighting. If you could enjoy a public good without paying, you would have little reason to pay, which is called the free-rider problem. By requiring everyone to contribute, taxes solve the problem. Governments also use taxes to provide help to people in need, to protect the environment, and to try to stabilize the economy. You will study government spending and revenue in Chapters 18 and 19.

The main taxes you will encounter are these. Income taxes are taxes on what people earn; the federal government and the State of California both levy them. Payroll taxes, such as FICA, fund Social Security and Medicare. Sales taxes are taxes on purchases and are collected at the register. Property taxes are taxes on the value of land and buildings, and they are the main source of money for local governments and schools. Other taxes include excise taxes on gasoline and tobacco and estate taxes on large inheritances. A person who owns a car pays a registration fee, and a business owner pays business taxes.

Economists classify taxes by how the rate changes with income. In a progressive tax, people with higher incomes pay a higher percentage of their income. The federal income tax is progressive. In a proportional tax, everyone pays the same percentage; a flat tax of 5 percent on all income would be proportional. In a regressive tax, people with lower incomes pay a larger percentage of their income. A sales tax can be regressive because lower-income households spend a larger share of income on taxable goods than higher-income households do. Social Security tax is also regressive at high incomes, because it applies only to wages up to a limit, which is $184,500 in 2026. Whether a tax is fair is a normative question that depends on values, but whether a tax is progressive is a positive question that can be tested with data. Two common principles of fairness are the ability-to-pay principle, which says that those with more income should pay more, and the benefits-received principle, which says that those who benefit from a service should pay for it, as drivers do with the gasoline tax.

How Income Tax Is Calculated

To calculate federal income tax, begin with income: wages, tips, interest, and other taxable income. Subtract adjustments for certain items such as some retirement contributions, and then subtract either the standard deduction or itemized deductions. The standard deduction is a fixed amount that reduces the income that is taxed. For tax year 2026, the standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer, $32,200 for a married couple filing jointly, and $24,150 for a head of household, according to the IRS. What remains is taxable income. Then the tax rates are applied. After that, tax credits, which reduce the tax dollar for dollar, are subtracted. The result is the tax owed. If the amount that was withheld or paid during the year is greater than the tax owed, the filer receives a refund.

The federal income tax uses a system of brackets. A tax bracket is a range of taxable income taxed at a particular rate. For single filers in tax year 2026, the rate is 10 percent on the first $12,400 of taxable income, 12 percent on income from $12,400 to $50,400, 22 percent from $50,400 to $105,700, 24 percent from $105,700 to $201,775, 32 percent from $201,775 to $256,225, 35 percent from $256,225 to $640,600, and 37 percent above that. Figure 13.6 shows the rates. The most common misunderstanding about brackets is the belief that if your income enters a higher bracket, all of your income is taxed at the higher rate. That is not so. Each rate applies only to the part of income inside its bracket. That is what "marginal" means: the marginal tax rate is the rate on the next dollar of income, while the effective tax rate is the total tax divided by total income, which is always lower than the top bracket you reach.

Here is a worked example. A single worker has $50,000 of taxable income in 2026. The first $12,400 is taxed at 10 percent, which is $1,240. The remaining $37,600 is taxed at 12 percent, which is $4,512. The total federal income tax is $5,752. The marginal rate is 12 percent, but the effective rate on taxable income is 5,752 ÷ 50,000 = 11.5 percent. If we compare the tax with the worker's gross wages before the standard deduction, assuming wages of $66,100 (the $50,000 of taxable income plus the $16,100 standard deduction), the effective rate is even lower, 8.7 percent. Notice that the worker does not owe any income tax on the first $16,100 of wages, because of the standard deduction. This is one reason that many part-time student workers owe no federal income tax at all.

California taxes income too, with a separate set of brackets and forms. For tax year 2025, the rates for single filers rose from 1 percent on the first $11,079 of taxable income to 2, 4, 6, and 8 percent in successive brackets, to 9.3 percent for income between $72,724 and $371,479, and to higher rates above that, up to 12.3 percent. California does not tax Social Security benefits, and it has its own deductions and credits. The state tax is also progressive, and the combined effect of federal, state, and payroll taxes is what shapes a worker's net income. Local governments, especially counties and school districts, also collect property taxes.

Tax Forms and Filing a Return

A series of forms connects your paycheck to your tax return. Form W-4, the Employee's Withholding Certificate, is the form that you fill out when you start a job. It tells your employer how much income tax to withhold. Form W-2, the Wage and Tax Statement, is the form that your employer sends you after the end of the year, by the end of January. It reports your wages for the year, the federal, Social Security, and Medicare taxes that were withheld, and the state information. You need the W-2 to file your return. If you work as an independent contractor, a client who pays you more than the minimum amount set by law during the year sends a Form 1099-NEC instead. And Form 1040 is the federal individual income tax return, on which you report your income, subtract deductions, calculate your tax, and compare it with the amount that was withheld. California residents also file a state return, Form 540, with the Franchise Tax Board. Figure 13.7 shows how they fit together.

The deadline for filing a federal return is usually April 15 for the previous calendar year. For tax year 2026, the return is due in April 2027. Filers who need more time can request an extension to file, but an extension does not extend the time to pay. A worker who is single and under a certain level of income may not be required to file, but should file anyway if tax was withheld, because that is the only way to get the money back. You can file electronically, which is faster and less likely to contain errors, or on paper. The IRS offers a Free File program for people with lower incomes and free fillable forms, and the Volunteer Income Tax Assistance program offers free help from trained volunteers for people with low to moderate incomes. Be careful about paid tax preparers who promise a big refund or who charge a fee based on the refund size. A paid preparer must sign the return, and you are still responsible for it.

What about the refund? In the 2026 filing season, the IRS reported that the average refund through the week ending March 13 was $3,623, up 10.8 percent from $3,271 at the same point in 2025. Figure 13.8 shows the change. Many people look forward to a refund, but economists point out that a refund means that the worker lent money to the government without interest during the year. A $3,623 refund is about $302 a month that could have been in the worker's paychecks (3,623 ÷ 12). Some people find the refund useful as a form of forced saving. Others prefer to adjust their W-4 so that withholding is closer to the actual tax. Either way, a refund is not a bonus. It is your own money returned to you.

The Safety Net: Social Security, Medicare, and Unemployment Insurance

Social Security is the federal social insurance program that you pay into with FICA taxes. Its largest part provides monthly benefits to retired workers, and it also pays benefits to disabled workers and to the families of workers who die. A worker earns credits by working and paying the tax. In 2026, a worker earns one credit for each $1,890 of covered earnings, up to four credits a year (which requires $7,560), and needs 40 credits, about ten years of work, to qualify for retirement benefits, according to the Social Security Administration. The monthly benefit depends on a worker's earnings over a career and on the age at which the worker begins to collect. Social Security is a pay-as-you-go system: the taxes paid by today's workers fund today's benefits, and in exchange future workers will fund yours. The program faces long-term financing challenges that Congress will have to address. For a young worker, the safest approach is to treat Social Security as one part of retirement income, not the whole.

Medicare is the federal health insurance program for most people age 65 and older and for some younger people with disabilities, and it is funded in part by the 1.45 percent Medicare tax. Unemployment insurance (UI) provides temporary partial income to workers who lose their jobs through no fault of their own and who are looking for work. It is run by the states, and in California it is administered by the Employment Development Department, which is funded by taxes that employers pay. Benefits are only for workers who earned enough in the past and who were employees; as you will see in the case study, independent contractors are generally not covered. Workers' compensation pays medical costs and part of the lost wages for workers who are injured on the job. California SDI, which you saw on the pay stub, pays benefits for off-the-job disability and for paid family leave. Together, these programs form a safety net that makes it less risky to work for someone else, and they are one of the benefits that come with being an employee.

Why do governments run such programs instead of leaving them to private markets? Economists point to the problems that you saw in Chapter 12. Adverse selection can break down private markets for some risks, because those who are most likely to need the benefit are the most likely to buy it, and moral hazard makes people less careful. Some risks are also very large and affect many people at once, such as a recession. By pooling the whole working population, with mandatory contributions, social insurance spreads the risk widely. Critics respond that mandatory programs reduce choices and may reduce incentives to work and save. The debate about how large these programs should be is a normative question that every generation answers through elections.

Frances PerkinsThe Architect of the Safety Net

When the Great Depression began, millions of Americans lost their jobs, and an older person with no job and no savings had few places to turn. Frances Perkins, who became Secretary of Labor in 1933 and the first woman to serve in a presidential cabinet, believed that the country needed a system that would protect workers against the main risks of industrial life: unemployment, old age, and disability.

The committee studied programs in other countries and in the states, and its work led to the Social Security Act, which became law on August 14, 1935. The act created a federal system of old-age benefits paid for by payroll taxes on workers and employers, a joint federal-state system of unemployment insurance, and aid for needy children and the blind and disabled. The government had to create account numbers and keep records for millions of workers, a huge task shown in the photograph.

The act has been changed many times, and the programs have grown. Supporters say that Social Security has sharply reduced poverty among older Americans, and that pooling risk is cheaper and fairer than leaving each person to face it alone. Critics argue that the program's long-term finances are under strain, and that mandatory taxes reduce personal choice. Both sides use the same idea that you saw in Chapter 12: insurance spreads the cost of risk across many people. Saving for Retirement

Retirement may seem far away when you are 18, but the decision to start saving is one of the most important ones that you will make. Social Security replaces only part of a typical worker's pre-retirement income, so most people also need savings. There are two main kinds of tax-favored retirement accounts. A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored plan in which you choose to have part of each paycheck deposited into an account, usually invested in funds. For 2026, the IRS limit on employee contributions to a 401(k) is $24,500. An IRA, or individual retirement account, is a personal account that you open yourself at a bank or brokerage; the 2026 limit is $7,500. In a traditional account, contributions are made before tax, which lowers your taxable income now, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed. In a Roth account, contributions are made after tax, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are not taxed. Withdrawals before retirement age generally face a tax penalty, with some exceptions.

The best deal in personal finance is often the employer match. Many employers match a part of what the worker contributes, for example by adding 50 cents or $1 for every dollar the worker puts in, up to a limit. A match is part of total compensation, as you saw in Section 1, and not taking it means turning down pay. Suppose a worker with wages of $38,480 contributes 6 percent of pay, which is $2,308.80 a year, and the employer matches the first 3 percent, which is $1,154.40. The worker gets $1,154.40 in extra pay, a 50 percent immediate return on the matched part. Workers often say "I can't afford to save," and sometimes that is true, but even a small contribution that earns the match is hard to beat. Because 401(k) contributions are taken from the paycheck, the worker does not need to remember to save.

How the savings are invested matters too, which is the topic of Chapter 15. In brief, there is a trade-off between risk and return. Stocks have historically earned higher returns over long periods than savings accounts, but they can lose value in the short term. Younger savers who will not need the money for decades can accept more risk than someone close to retirement. Many plans offer a target-date fund, which gradually moves toward safer investments as the retirement date approaches. Fees also matter: a fund that charges 1 percent a year takes a bite out of returns each year for decades. And as always, no investment is guaranteed. This textbook provides general educational information, not individualized investment advice.

Key Concepts

The Power of Starting Early: Compound Interest

Compound interest is interest earned on both the original amount and on the interest already earned. It is the reason that time is a saver's most powerful ally. If you invest $1,000 at 6 percent a year, you earn $60 in the first year. In the second year you earn 6 percent of $1,060, which is $63.60, and so on. Over many years, the growth speeds up, because the interest earns interest. This is the opposite of the debt trap that you saw in Chapter 11, where interest on credit card balances compounds against you.

Figure 13.10 shows the effect with a hypothetical example. Three savers each put $200 a month into an account that earns an average of 6 percent a year, and each stops at age 65. The saver who starts at 20 deposits for 45 years, a total of $108,000, and ends up with about $551,199. The saver who starts at 30 deposits for 35 years, a total of $84,000, and ends with about $284,942. The saver who starts at 40 deposits for 25 years, a total of $60,000, and ends with about $138,599. The saver who started at 20 saved only $24,000 more than the saver who started at 30, but ended with $266,257 more. Most of the difference is interest earned on interest. The 6 percent return is an assumption for teaching, not a promise; real returns vary from year to year and can be negative.

The lesson is practical: start early, even with a small amount, and increase it as your income grows. A worker who cannot save $200 a month at 20 can start with $25 and raise it with each raise. The same math also explains why paying off high-interest debt is so valuable. Paying off a credit card at 22.36 percent is like earning a guaranteed 22.36 percent on that money. A common order for a young worker is: build a small emergency fund, capture any employer match, pay off high-interest debt, and then increase long-term saving. This is a general guideline, not personal advice, and each person's situation is different.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVERequired Retirement Saving: Australia

Some countries require employers to contribute to workers' retirement accounts. In Australia, the "superannuation guarantee" requires employers to pay a percentage of an employee's earnings into a retirement fund. According to the Australian Taxation Office, the rate rose to 12 percent on July 1, 2025, which the agency called the final scheduled increase. Employers must pay the contributions at least quarterly into the worker's chosen fund.

In the United States, retirement saving is largely voluntary: Social Security is mandatory, but 401(k) plans and IRAs are optional. A mandatory system raises the share of workers who have savings, but it also reduces take-home pay and reduces choices. A voluntary system respects choice, but many people do not save enough, and economists have studied ways to nudge people, such as enrolling workers automatically with the right to opt out.

Key Concepts

What Independence Requires

Independence is more than a paycheck. It is a set of decisions that fit together, and the chapters in this unit are the pieces. Your housing should fit the 30 percent guideline from Chapter 12. Your transportation should be chosen by total cost of ownership. Your credit should be built with on-time payments and protected with a freeze, as you learned in Chapter 11. Your insurance should match the risks that you could not afford: renters coverage, auto liability, and health coverage, which young adults can keep on a parent's plan until they turn 26. Your taxes should be filed on time, with the W-2 and the right forms. And your savings should begin with an emergency fund and, if possible, a retirement account.

Independence also requires records. Keep a folder, on paper or online, with your lease, insurance policies, pay stubs, W-2s, tax returns, and loan documents. Keep tax returns and supporting documents for at least a few years, because the IRS may ask questions about a return after you file it. Protect your Social Security number and review your Social Security earnings record at ssa.gov to make sure that your wages were recorded. Watch for identity theft, using the four layers from Chapter 11. And finally, keep learning. Tax laws, interest rates, and benefits change every year. The numbers in this chapter are for 2026. In another year or two, the standard deduction, the brackets, and the contribution limits will be different, so check the IRS and the Social Security Administration for current figures.

A last point is about attitude. Many young adults feel that money is too complicated to understand or too limited to matter. The opposite is true: small steps repeated over time are the main tools of building wealth. Pay yourself first, read the pay stub, take the match, avoid high-interest debt, shop for insurance, file your taxes, and ask for help from trusted sources. None of this requires a high income. It requires information and habits, which you now have the beginnings of. In the case study that follows, you will look at a growing way of working, as an independent contractor, in which many of these decisions become the worker's own responsibility.

CASE STUDY

Employee or Independent Contractor?

A growing number of workers deliver food, drive passengers, or take on freelance jobs through apps and clients, and they are paid and taxed differently from employees. Whether a worker is classified as an employee or an independent contractor decides who pays which taxes, who receives which benefits, and who bears the risk. This case study asks you to weigh the evidence.

Background

Two Kinds of Work, Two Sets of Rules

In Section 2 you learned that an employee has income tax and payroll tax withheld, receives a Form W-2, and is covered by minimum wage, overtime, unemployment insurance, and workers' compensation laws. An independent contractor is in business for himself or herself: the worker decides how and when to do the job, may work for several clients, uses his or her own tools, and receives a Form 1099 instead of a W-2. The contractor pays the whole 15.3 percent self-employment tax, which is both halves of Social Security and Medicare, and makes estimated tax payments during the year.

The question of who is which is not always clear. California uses the "ABC test," which presumes that a worker is an employee unless the hiring company shows that the worker is free from its control, does work outside the usual course of its business, and is customarily engaged in an independent trade. In 2020, California voters passed Proposition 22, which says that app-based drivers are independent contractors if certain conditions are met, and which gives them an earnings guarantee, a health care subsidy for qualifying drivers, and occupational accident insurance. On July 25, 2024, the California Supreme Court upheld the proposition.

This case study asks you to look at the evidence from several angles: national data on alternative work arrangements (Document A), the tax and legal differences (Document B), a hypothetical delivery worker's year (Document C), and three views about how to treat this kind of work (Document D). Use the labor-market ideas from Section 1 and the tax ideas from Section 2.

THE FLEXIBILITY VIEW
"Most contractors say that they prefer the arrangement. People can set their own hours, work for several companies, and earn extra income. Forcing every worker to be an employee would reduce flexibility and might lead companies to hire fewer people, leaving workers with no work at all."
THE WORKER-PROTECTION VIEW
"When a company controls the pay and the tasks, the worker is really an employee. Classifying workers as contractors shifts costs, such as payroll taxes, accident insurance, and unemployment protection, onto the workers, who are the least able to bear them. Minimum wage and benefits should apply to everyone who depends on a company for income."
THE MIDDLE-PATH VIEW
"The old categories do not fit new kinds of work. Rather than choosing between employee and contractor, governments could create a middle category, with a basic set of protections, such as accident insurance and a portable benefit account, while preserving flexibility. The challenge is to design the rules so that costs are shared fairly."

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Planning a Career and Finding Work
Wages differ because of supply and demand, productivity, and compensating differentials. The labor market keeps changing with technology and trade; the BLS projects fast growth for health and clean-energy jobs such as nurse practitioners (41 percent) and wind turbine technicians (30 percent). A job search has five steps: search, prepare, interview, compare, and follow up. Compare offers by total compensation, and watch for job scams. California's minimum wage is $16.90 in 2026.
Section 2 · Paychecks, Taxes, and Tax Forms
Gross pay minus deductions is net pay. FICA is 7.65 percent (Social Security 6.2 and Medicare 1.45) and the employer pays a matching amount. Federal income tax is progressive: the 2026 standard deduction for a single filer is $16,100 and the rates rise from 10 to 37 percent by bracket. The marginal rate applies to the next dollar; the effective rate is the total tax divided by income. The W-4, W-2, 1099-NEC, and 1040 connect paychecks to a tax return. A refund is your own money returned.
Section 3 · Independence and Planning Ahead
Build a budget from net pay, with an emergency fund and savings first. Social Security, Medicare, unemployment insurance, and SDI form a safety net. Take the employer match in a 401(k); an IRA is a personal account. Compound interest rewards starting early: $200 a month at 6 percent becomes about $551,000 by 65 when started at 20 but about $139,000 when started at 40 (hypothetical). About 7.4 percent of U.S. workers were independent contractors in 2023. Contractors pay the full 15.3 percent self-employment tax, receive a 1099, make estimated tax payments, and are generally not covered by minimum wage, overtime, or unemployment insurance.
