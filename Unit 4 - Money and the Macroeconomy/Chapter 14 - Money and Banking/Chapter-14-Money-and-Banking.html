Concept Review

Macroeconomics is the study of the economy as a whole: total output, total employment, the general level of prices, and the money that links them. Units 1 through 3 focused on individual choices, markets, and personal finance. Unit 4 steps back to look at the whole economy. Every part of it depends on a tool that Chapter 1's voluntary trades quietly assumed: money.

Chapter 14 Key Concept

Money is anything that people widely accept in payment for goods, services, and debts. It lowers the cost of trade by serving as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. Banks are financial intermediaries that take in deposits and make loans, and their rules, insurance, and technology determine how safe, convenient, and available money is to households and businesses.

Why the Concept Matters

Think about the last three things you paid for. Maybe you tapped a debit card at a Shafter convenience store, sent a classmate $10 through a phone app for a shared pizza, and watched your paycheck land directly in a checking account. Now imagine none of that existed. To buy lunch at the taquería, you would need to find out whether the owner wanted what you had to offer. A Kern County almond picker would have to be paid in almonds, and then trade the almonds for rent, gas, and shoes. Money removes that burden, but it also creates new questions. Who guarantees that a bank will return your savings? How do banks create new loans? What happens to your money if prices rise? In this chapter you will learn how money works, how U.S. banking grew up, and how technology is changing the way we hold and move value.

Key Concepts

Trading Without Money

Imagine you grow almonds on a small farm near Shafter, and your tractor needs a new hydraulic pump. The repair shop owner does not want almonds. She wants a new set of tires for her pickup. The tire dealer, in turn, wants his roof fixed, and the roofer would happily take almonds, but only if you can find a way to get the tire dealer and the repair shop to accept his roofing work. Before you can fix a tractor, you may need to complete a long chain of trades. That puzzle is what life was like in economies without money.

Direct trade of goods and services for other goods and services, with no money involved, is called barter. Barter works in small communities where people know one another and swap simple goods. But barter has three serious weaknesses. First, it requires what economists call a double coincidence of wants: you must find someone who has what you want and wants what you have. Second, it makes prices confusing. If a pair of boots is worth six chickens, and a chicken is worth two bushels of wheat, how many bushels buy a haircut? Every good would have a separate price in terms of every other good. Third, many goods spoil, are bulky, or cannot be divided. You cannot pay for a small repair with half a cow.

These weaknesses add up to high transaction costs, the time and effort needed to complete a trade. Chapter 1 explained that voluntary trade makes both sides better off. When trade is expensive, fewer trades happen, people produce more of their own needs, and they give up the gains from specialization. Societies discovered a solution many thousands of years ago: pick a commonly accepted item and use it as the go-between in every trade. That item is money, which economists define as anything that people widely accept in payment for goods, services, and debts.

Key Concepts

Three Jobs That Money Does

Economists say that money does three jobs, and each one answers one of barter's problems. Figure 14.1 summarizes them.

First, money is a medium of exchange: something sellers accept in payment so that buyers do not need to find a double coincidence of wants. When you hand a cashier $9 for a burrito and a drink, the cashier does not care what you have to offer except the money, because the cashier knows that other people will accept the same money for rent, tires, and groceries. The medium of exchange splits one hard trade (your skill for burritos) into two easy ones (your skill for money, then money for burritos).

Second, money is a unit of account, a common measure for stating prices and keeping records. In the United States, prices are quoted in dollars, so you can compare a $42 pair of shoes and a $6 haircut on the same scale. Businesses use money to keep books, banks use it to track loans, and governments use it to write budgets. Without a unit of account, you would have to remember thousands of barter prices.

Third, money is a store of value: it holds its worth over time, so you can earn it today and spend it later. A farmworker paid on Friday can hold the pay until Monday's rent is due. Money is not a perfect store of value, because rising prices can shrink what a dollar buys (you will see this later in the section), and some goods, such as land or a house, may hold value better over long periods. But a good money keeps its value well enough that people are willing to hold it, and holding it is far easier than storing a barn full of tomatoes.

QUICK REFERENCEA unit of account is a standard measure used to state prices and record debts. Spanish: unidad de cuenta

Key Concepts

What Makes Good Money?

Not everything works well as money. Throughout history, people have used salt, tobacco, cattle, shells, beads, and metals. Some worked better than others. Economists point to six properties that a good money must have. Figure 14.2 shows how each one works.

Durability. Money gets handed from person to person thousands of times, so it must hold up. Ice cream or fresh strawberries would be poor money because they melt or rot. Coins last for decades. A paper U.S. $1 bill lasts only a few years in circulation, and the paper itself is a blend of cotton and linen that resists wear better than ordinary paper.

Portability. Money must be easy to carry and move. A shopper cannot haul a bag of rice to the checkout every day, but a card or a phone can carry thousands of dollars. Today digital money is the most portable form of all.

Divisibility. Money must come in units small enough for cheap items and large enough for expensive ones. The dollar divides into 100 cents, and larger bills and electronic transfers handle bigger purchases. A live cow fails this test, because you cannot pay for a $20 purchase with a fifth of a cow.

Uniformity. Every unit of the same denomination must be the same. One $10 bill is worth exactly the same as any other $10 bill. If units varied in size or quality, buyers and sellers would have to inspect every payment, which raises transaction costs.

Limited supply. Money must be scarce enough to keep its value. If anyone could make it easily, it would soon be worthless. This is why governments protect currency with special paper, ink, and security threads, and why counterfeiting is a serious crime. If a government prints too much money, each unit loses value, as you will see in the Global Perspective in this section.

Acceptability. Finally, and most important, people have to accept it. Money has value largely because everyone expects everyone else to accept it. That expectation depends on trust in the government, the banks, or the community that stands behind the money.

QUICK REFERENCEDivisibility means that money can be divided into smaller units to make payments of any size. Spanish: divisibilidad

EXAMPLEWhich Would You Pick as Money?

Suppose the Shafter High School student store decided to stop using dollars and to use only one of these four items as money: (1) live goldfish, (2) one-ounce silver coins, (3) paper tickets printed on the school's copy machine, or (4) bags of frozen tacos. Rate each on the six properties.

Goldfish fail durability and uniformity: they die, and they come in different sizes. Frozen tacos melt if the freezer fails, and they cannot be divided into small units without ruining them. Paper tickets that anyone can copy fail the test of limited supply, because the copy machine would make them worthless. Silver coins do reasonably well: they are durable, uniform, divisible into fractions of an ounce, and scarce. Their main weakness is that they are heavy to carry in large amounts, and their market price rises and falls, which makes them a less stable measure of value.

Key Concepts

From Gold to Fiat Money

Over time, societies moved through three broad kinds of money. The earliest was commodity money: money that is also a useful good in its own right, such as gold, silver, salt, or cowrie shells. Its value comes partly from the material itself. If a gold coin is melted into jewelry, it still has value. The United States long used gold and silver coins, and in the 1800s many people carried pieces of precious metal.

The second kind is representative money, paper that can be exchanged for a fixed amount of a commodity such as gold or silver. Under the gold standard, a certificate stated that the holder could trade it for a set quantity of gold at a bank or the Treasury. The paper was lighter and easier to carry than the gold itself, but its value came from the promise that backed it. Banks and governments that issued more certificates than they held in gold could not keep the promise, and when people lost faith, they rushed to exchange paper for gold.

Today almost every country uses the third kind, fiat money. Fiat money has no value as a commodity, and no one can exchange it for gold. Its value rests on a government's order and on public trust. The word fiat comes from Latin and means "let it be done." U.S. currency is fiat money. The United States ended the domestic gold standard in the 1930s and, in 1971, stopped letting foreign governments exchange dollars for gold. Since then, a dollar has been valuable because people can use it to pay taxes and debts and because sellers will accept it.

Federal Reserve notes carry a statement that they are legal tender for all debts, public and private. Legal tender is money that a government says must be accepted when it is offered to settle a debt. That does not mean every store must take cash for a purchase (a business may set its own payment rules in many situations), but it does mean that a debt can be settled with it. The real reason fiat money works, though, is acceptability. A banknote is just paper unless people trust that others will take it.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEWhen Money Loses Its Value: Zimbabwe

In the late 2000s, the government of Zimbabwe paid its bills by printing huge amounts of new Zimbabwean dollars. Prices rose so fast that stores changed their price tags several times a day, and the government printed notes with larger and larger face values. Money failed all three of its jobs. People did not want to hold it as a store of value, sellers did not want to accept it as a medium of exchange, and the unit of account changed so fast that prices were nearly meaningless. In 2009, the government stopped requiring the use of its own currency and allowed businesses to price and trade in foreign currencies, mainly the U.S. dollar. Zimbabwe's experience shows that fiat money is only as strong as the trust and limits behind it. Chapter 17 explains inflation in more detail.

Key Concepts

Measuring the Money Supply

How much money is in the U.S. economy? The answer depends on what you count. Economists group money by how quickly it can be spent, an idea called liquidity. An asset is more liquid if you can turn it into spending power quickly and without losing value. Cash in your pocket is perfectly liquid. A checking account is nearly as liquid. A savings account takes a bit more effort to use, and a house is very illiquid, because selling one takes months and costs thousands of dollars.

The Federal Reserve tracks two main measures of the money supply. M1 is the narrowest. It includes currency in circulation, checkable deposits at banks (accounts you can write checks or use a debit card on), and, since a change in the definition in May 2020, savings deposits. In September 2026, M1 was about $20.1 trillion. M2 is broader. It includes everything in M1 plus small-denomination time deposits (such as certificates of deposit under $100,000) and balances in retail money market funds. In September 2026, M2 was about $23.4 trillion, which works out to more than $60,000 for every person in the United States.

Figure 14.3 shows how M2 has changed since 2000. M2 grew steadily for two decades, but it jumped in 2020 and 2021. Between January 2020 and January 2021, M2 grew from about $15.4 trillion to about $19.4 trillion, an increase of about 26 percent. That rise reflected the response to the COVID-19 pandemic, when the federal government sent out large payments and the Federal Reserve bought large amounts of financial assets, as you will see in Chapters 19 and 20. After 2022, M2 flattened and dipped slightly, then began to climb again.

Key Concepts

How Americans Pay

Cash used to be the main way to pay in stores. The Federal Reserve's 2025 Diary of Consumer Payment Choice, which asks a sample of consumers to record every payment they make over several days, found that cash made up about 14 percent of consumer payments in 2025. Credit cards were used for about a third (34 percent) and debit cards for another 31 percent. The remaining payments, about 21 percent, were made with checks, mobile apps, bank transfers, and other methods.

Cash is still important. The same survey found that about four in five consumers used cash in the previous 30 days and that cash made up roughly 20 percent of in-person transactions. For many people, cash is easy to budget with, requires no bank account, and cannot be hacked. People who do not use banks at all have fewer choices. The Federal Reserve's survey of household well-being found that 6 percent of adults were unbanked in 2025, meaning that neither they nor a spouse or partner had a checking, savings, or money market account. Section 3 returns to the people who are left out of the banking system.

The mix of payment methods is itself a sign of how the money supply works. Most spending today moves money between bank accounts and never touches a bill or a coin. When you swipe a debit card, the bank subtracts the amount from your account and adds it to the seller's account. A figure on a screen has done the job of a paper bill. For this reason, economists count deposits as money, and it is why M1 and M2 include them.

Key Concepts

What Money Can Buy: Nominal and Real Values

A dollar is a unit of account, but that unit does not keep a fixed size. If prices rise, a dollar buys less than it did. Economists therefore separate nominal value, which is measured in current dollars with no adjustment for price changes, from real value, which has been adjusted for changes in prices. Nominal figures tell you the dollar amount. Real figures tell you what the money can buy. The California standards ask students to distinguish nominal from real data, and the distinction runs through the rest of Unit 4.

Consider a worker who earns $15 an hour in 2000 and $30 an hour in 2026. Her nominal wage has doubled. But if prices have also nearly doubled, she can buy little more than she could in 2000, and her real wage has barely moved. To compare across years, economists use a price index, a number that tracks the cost of a fixed basket of goods and services over time. The most common one is the Consumer Price Index (CPI), published by the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Chapter 16 and Chapter 17 explain how it is built.

Figure 14.6 uses the CPI to show the declining purchasing power of a dollar. The CPI for all urban consumers stood at 169.3 in January 2000 and 326.6 in January 2026, about 93 percent higher. That means that goods and services that cost $100 in January 2000 cost about $193 in January 2026. Put another way, a dollar in 2026 buys only about 52 cents' worth of what it bought in January 2000. A dollar kept in a jar for 26 years has lost almost half of its purchasing power. This is why the store of value function is only as good as the stability of prices, and one reason that people put money in accounts that pay interest.

Key Concepts

Money in the Kern County Economy

Money's three jobs show up everywhere in the Central Valley. Consider a harvest season in Kern County. A grower contracts with a labor contractor, who pays crews every week. Each worker's paycheck, whether it is deposited electronically or cashed at a store, is a medium of exchange that the worker will use for rent in Shafter, groceries, and fuel. The grower prices a box of oranges or a pound of almonds in dollars, which makes the unit of account the common language between the packing house, the trucking company, and the grocery store in another state. A family that sets aside some of the season's earnings to pay for the slow winter months is using money as a store of value.

The same chain of trades would be nearly impossible under barter. A packing house could not pay a crew in boxes of fruit and expect each worker to find someone who wants citrus in exchange for a month of rent. Money lets thousands of specialized workers and businesses cooperate without ever meeting. This is one reason that the economies of agricultural regions grew as money and banking spread: farmers could sell for money, borrow against a coming harvest, and buy machinery, seed, and water from people they did not know.

Money also links Kern County to the rest of the world. Almonds, pistachios, grapes, and oil leave the county and are sold in global markets, where payments are made in dollars or converted from other currencies. A change in the value of the dollar, or in prices across the economy, can change what a grower earns and what a family pays at the register. Section 1 gave you the basic tools to explain these links. Sections 2 and 3 show how banks keep the system running.

Key Concepts

What Banks Do

A bank is a business that accepts money from people who want to keep it safe and lends part of that money to people who want to use it. Economists call a bank a financial intermediary, an institution that stands between savers and borrowers. A family in Shafter that saves $2,000 may not know a farmer in Wasco who needs a loan for new irrigation equipment, and the farmer may not know the family. The bank brings them together. It collects the savings of thousands of people and lends them to hundreds of borrowers, which would be difficult for the savers and borrowers to arrange on their own.

When you put money in a bank, you make a deposit. The bank promises to return it on demand or at an agreed time and typically pays interest, a payment for the use of money, expressed as a percentage of the amount. When the bank lends money, the borrower pays interest to the bank. A bank earns its profit mainly from the spread: the interest rate it charges borrowers minus the interest rate it pays depositors, after covering the cost of staff, buildings, and loans that are not repaid.

Banks provide services beyond loans. They keep deposits safe from theft and fire, which a mattress cannot do. They run the payment system, honoring checks, processing card transactions, and moving money between accounts. They help customers judge whether a borrower is likely to repay, which is difficult and costly for individuals to do. Finally, they allow savers to withdraw money when they need it, even though the bank has lent most of it out. That promise is what makes banking both useful and risky, as the rest of this section shows.

EXAMPLEHow a Bank Earns a Profit (Hypothetical)

Suppose a small bank holds $10 million in deposits and pays depositors an average of 2 percent interest. It lends $8 million at an average of 7 percent and keeps the rest as cash and safe assets. The bank collects 0.07 × $8,000,000 = $560,000 in interest on loans and pays 0.02 × $10,000,000 = $200,000 in interest to depositors. The difference is $360,000. From that the bank must pay its employees, rent, computers, and any loans that go unpaid. If those costs are $300,000, the bank's profit is $60,000.

The numbers are made up for teaching. They show why a bank must make loans to survive, and why one wave of defaults can turn a profit into a loss: if borrowers fail to repay $500,000 in loans, the bank loses money even if every depositor behaves.

Key Concepts

From the First Bank to the Civil War

Banking began in the United States soon after independence. In 1791, Congress chartered the First Bank of the United States in Philadelphia, a bank that was partly owned by private investors and partly by the government. Secretary of the Treasury Alexander Hamilton, whom you will meet in this section's Economics Pacesetter, had proposed it. The bank held the government's deposits, made loans, and issued notes that could be used as money. Its charter lasted 20 years. Supporters said that it brought order to a young country's finances, and opponents said that it gave too much power to a small group of wealthy investors and that the Constitution did not authorize it. Congress let the charter expire in 1811.

After a wartime scramble for money, Congress created the Second Bank of the United States in 1816, also with a 20-year charter. The Second Bank also became a political battleground. Supporters pointed to the stable national currency it helped create. Critics said that it favored the wealthy and Eastern merchants over farmers and small borrowers. The Supreme Court ruled in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819) that Congress had the power to create it. But President Andrew Jackson vetoed a bill to renew the charter in 1832, and the Second Bank ended its federal role in 1836. For the next quarter century, the United States had no central bank at all.

In its place came a system of state banks, banks chartered by state governments. In the decades before the Civil War, hundreds of state banks issued their own paper notes, each promising to pay in gold or silver. Notes from a bank in a distant state were hard to evaluate and could trade at a discount. Counterfeiters had thousands of designs to copy. Some banks, nicknamed "wildcat" banks, were located in remote places so that holders would have a hard time demanding payment, and some issued more notes than they could redeem. Many failed, and their note holders lost money.

Key Concepts

A National System and a Central Bank

The Civil War forced a change. In 1863 and 1864, Congress passed the National Bank Acts, which let banks obtain a federal charter and become national banks. National banks had to hold federal government bonds as backing for their notes, and the notes were printed in a uniform design. A 10 percent tax on state bank notes, enacted in 1865, made state notes unprofitable, so the country gradually adopted a single national currency. State banks did not disappear. They found they could attract customers through deposits and checking accounts instead of notes, and today the nation still has a dual banking system: banks may choose a state charter or a national charter. In California, state-chartered banks are supervised by the Department of Financial Protection and Innovation, and national banks are supervised by the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency.

The national banking system had a weakness. The supply of money and credit did not adjust to the needs of the economy, and banks that ran short of cash in a crisis could not borrow from a lender of last resort. Financial panics hit repeatedly, and in 1907 a panic in New York spread across the country, closing banks and businesses. Congress responded with the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, which created the Federal Reserve System, the nation's central bank. The Fed has twelve regional Reserve Banks, including the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco shown at the start of this chapter, and a Board of Governors in Washington, D.C. Its original purposes were to make the currency more flexible, to supervise banks, and to act as a lender to banks in trouble. Over the next century, its job expanded to include steering interest rates and the economy, which Chapter 20 examines closely.

Key Concepts

Panic, Depression, and Deposit Insurance

A bank holds only a fraction of its deposits as cash, and it lends out the rest. If all depositors tried to withdraw their money at the same time, the bank could not pay, even if every loan it made were sound. A sudden rush by depositors to pull out their money is called a bank run. A run can begin with a rumor. Once it starts, each person has a reason to run: those who get to the teller first get paid, and those who wait may get nothing. In that way, a fear that a bank is in trouble can make the bank fail, and one failure can frighten customers of other banks. Economists call this spread of fear contagion.

Bank runs were common in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. They reached their worst in the Great Depression. After the stock market crash of 1929, farm and business failures left banks with loans that could not be repaid, and depositors began withdrawing cash. Between 1930 and 1933, roughly 9,000 banks closed, and depositors lost billions of dollars in savings. In early March 1933, states and then the federal government declared bank holidays, closing banks temporarily so that panic could cool and sound banks could reopen.

Congress's long-term answer was the Banking Act of 1933, which created the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC). The FDIC insures deposits, which means that if an insured bank fails, the FDIC pays depositors up to a set limit. When the system began on January 1, 1934, the limit was $2,500 per depositor; it rose to $5,000 later that year. Congress has raised it many times since, and it has been $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category since 2008 (made permanent in 2010). The FDIC is funded by fees that banks pay, not by taxpayers' general funds. It reports that since it began operating in 1934, no depositor has lost a penny of insured deposits. Credit unions have similar protection through the National Credit Union Administration (NCUA), with the same $250,000 limit.

Deposit insurance changed the incentives of depositors. If your deposit is insured, you have little reason to join a run, because you will be paid either way. As a result, runs on insured deposits became rare. But insurance also creates a problem that economists call moral hazard. A bank whose depositors are protected might take bigger risks because it knows that depositors will not punish it. Regulators therefore examine banks, set minimum levels of capital (the owners' own money that absorbs losses), and can close a bank that is unsafe. The system balances protection and risk.

Key Concepts

Two Crises: Savings and Loans, and 2008

Deposit insurance did not end bank failures. In the 1980s, hundreds of savings and loan associations failed. Many of them had made long-term fixed-rate mortgage loans in the 1960s and 1970s, when interest rates were low. When inflation and interest rates rose in the late 1970s and early 1980s, they had to pay depositors high rates while still collecting low rates on old mortgages. Deregulation in the early 1980s then let some of them make riskier loans, and because deposits were insured, depositors had little reason to worry. When real estate prices fell in several regions, many institutions failed. The insurance fund that protected savings and loan deposits ran out of money, and Congress passed a major rescue and reform law in 1989. As Figure 14.8 shows, failures reached 530 that year, the highest of any year in the data.

A second wave followed after the housing boom ended. Between 2007 and 2009, home prices fell, many mortgage borrowers could not pay, and banks that held mortgage loans and securities backed by them suffered large losses. Failures of FDIC-insured institutions rose from 3 in 2007 to 25 in 2008, 137 in 2009, and 155 in 2010. Several of the largest financial firms failed or were rescued, and the federal government and the Federal Reserve took emergency steps to prevent the collapse of the system, including a temporary rise in the deposit insurance limit from $100,000 to $250,000. Several Central Valley cities, including Bakersfield, were among the metropolitan areas with the highest foreclosure rates in the country during these years.

Economists point to several lessons. Insurance protects depositors, but it can weaken the discipline that depositors place on banks, so regulators must supervise. Rapid growth in lending and in housing prices can hide risk. And the failure of a large institution can spread losses to others. These lessons shaped the rules that Congress and regulators adopted afterward and are still debated today.

Key Concepts

Banks, Thrifts, and Credit Unions

Today, Americans can choose among several kinds of depository institutions. A commercial bank is a for-profit institution that accepts deposits and makes a wide range of loans, from car loans and mortgages to business loans. Commercial banks range from the largest national banks, with branches in every state, to community banks with a handful of offices. A savings institution (also called a thrift) includes savings and loan associations and savings banks. They began as institutions that specialized in taking savings deposits and making home mortgage loans, and they now offer many of the same services as commercial banks.

A credit union is a not-for-profit cooperative that is owned by its members. Members share a common tie, such as working for the same employer, belonging to the same community, or attending the same school. Because credit unions do not have outside stockholders and are largely exempt from federal income tax, they often pay somewhat higher rates on savings and charge lower rates on loans. In the second quarter of 2026, the NCUA reported 4,214 federally insured credit unions with 146.1 million members and total assets of about $2.5 trillion. Kern County has several credit unions, including some that began as employee groups at schools and oil companies.

Figure 14.9 compares the three types. All three accept deposits that are federally insured, all make loans, and all are regulated. Which is best for a particular household depends on the products, rates, fees, branches, and online tools they offer. Comparison shopping is worthwhile, and Chapters 10 and 11 gave you tools for it.

Alexander HamiltonBuilding a Financial System for a New Nation

When the United States won its independence, it was nearly bankrupt. The national government and several states owed large sums from the Revolutionary War. Paper money issued during the war had lost almost all its value, and the phrase "not worth a continental" had become a joke. In 1789, President George Washington named Alexander Hamilton, a New York lawyer and former Army officer, as the first Secretary of the Treasury.

Hamilton believed that a country that could not borrow and repay would not prosper. In his Report on Public Credit (January 1790), he proposed that the federal government take over and pay the war debts of the states and honor the debts to its creditors in full. In his Report on a National Bank (December 1790), he proposed a national bank that would hold federal deposits, lend to the government and to businesses, and provide a sound paper currency. Congress passed the bill, and President Washington signed it in February 1791.

Hamilton's plans were controversial. Thomas Jefferson and James Madison argued that the Constitution did not give Congress power to charter a bank and that the bank would serve Northern financiers. Hamilton answered that the Constitution's "necessary and proper" clause allowed Congress to use any appropriate means to carry out its powers over taxes, borrowing, and coin. The Supreme Court later took Hamilton's side in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819). Debates about the proper size of the federal role in finance have continued ever since, and thoughtful people still disagree about how much power over money and credit should rest with the national government.

Hamilton also wrote about manufacturing and trade and helped shape the country's early tax system. He died in 1804 after a duel with Aaron Burr. His ideas about public credit and a national bank form part of the foundation of the American financial system.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVECanada's Branch Banking and the 1930s

While about 9,000 U.S. banks closed in the early 1930s, Canada did not experience a single bank failure during the Great Depression. One reason economists cite is the structure of the Canadian system. A handful of large banks operated hundreds of branches across the whole country, so a bank could balance losses in one region against gains in another. The United States, by contrast, had thousands of small banks, many limited to one town or one state by law, and a farm-price collapse in a county like Kern could sink the only bank in it. Canada created its own central bank in 1935 and its deposit insurance corporation in 1967. Different structures involve different trade-offs: a few large banks may be more stable, but they may also offer less competition and have fewer local owners.

Key Concepts

What Your Bank Offers

Walk into a bank or open its app, and you will find four kinds of services. The first is a place to spend. A checking account lets you deposit money and then pay by check, debit card, or electronic transfer. Many checking accounts pay little or no interest, but they offer convenience and can come with fees. The second is a place to save. Savings accounts, money market accounts, and certificates of deposit (CDs) pay interest. The more flexible the account, the lower the rate usually is, because the bank cannot count on keeping your money for long.

The third service is borrowing. Banks make car loans, home mortgages, student loans, small business loans, and credit cards. In a loan, the bank gives you money now, and you repay it with interest over time. The rate depends on how risky the bank believes the loan to be, how long you will take to repay, and the general level of interest rates in the economy. The fourth service is protection and convenience: safe deposit boxes, wire transfers, cashier's checks, and advice. A farmer who needs a line of credit to buy seed in spring and repay it at harvest depends on this kind of service from a local bank or a farm credit lender.

Chapters 10 and 11 showed you how to manage a bank account and how to use credit wisely. This section looks instead at how the banking system as a whole works and how it is changing. Banks are among the most regulated businesses in the country, and for good reason: their deposits are everyone's savings, and their loans help set the pace of the whole economy.

Key Concepts

Short-Term and Long-Term Interest Rates

The interest rate is the most important price in banking, but there is no single rate. Economists distinguish short-term and long-term interest rates. A short-term rate applies to money lent or borrowed for a year or less, such as a credit card balance, a checking-account overdraft, an overnight loan between banks, or a three-month Treasury bill. A long-term rate applies to lending over many years, such as a 30-year home mortgage or a 10-year U.S. Treasury note. The California standards ask students to explain the significance of the two.

Short-term rates are closely tied to the policy of the Federal Reserve. The Fed influences the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge one another for overnight loans, and that rate quickly affects what banks charge on credit cards, business credit lines, and adjustable-rate loans, and what they pay on savings. Long-term rates depend more on what lenders expect about inflation, risk, and the supply of savings over many years. If lenders expect prices to rise, they ask for a higher long-term rate to protect the purchasing power of what they will be repaid.

Figure 14.12 compares the two. In January 2007, the federal funds rate was 5.25 percent and the 10-year Treasury yield was 4.76 percent, so the short-term rate was higher. After the 2007–2009 financial crisis, the Fed lowered short-term rates to nearly zero, and kept them there for years. In January 2021, the federal funds rate was 0.09 percent and the 10-year yield was 1.08 percent. In 2022 and 2023, the Fed raised rates sharply to fight inflation, and short-term rates reached 5.33 percent in January 2024, above the 10-year yield of 4.06 percent. By October 2026, the federal funds rate had come down to 3.88 percent, while the 10-year yield had climbed to 5.13 percent.

Why does the gap between them matter? A bank typically borrows short (from depositors, whose money can be withdrawn on demand) and lends long (in mortgages and business loans). When long-term rates are higher than short-term rates, as they are most of the time, the spread is wide, and lending is profitable. When short-term rates rise above long-term rates (an "inverted" pattern, as in 2007 and 2024), banks earn less on new loans, and many economists treat it as a warning sign of a slowdown. Short-term rates matter most for people with credit card balances and savings accounts; long-term rates matter most for people buying a house or a farm and for governments and companies that borrow for many years. Chapter 20 explains how the Fed uses these rates as a tool.

Key Concepts

How Banks Create Money

Banks do something that surprises most people: they help create money. A bank keeps only part of its deposits as reserves, which are cash in the vault and deposits at the Federal Reserve. It lends out the rest. This practice is called fractional reserve banking. It works because on a normal day only a small share of depositors want their money all at once.

Follow the money in an example. Suppose you deposit $1,000 in cash at a bank, and the bank must keep 10 percent of deposits as reserves. The bank keeps $100 and lends $900 to a borrower, who spends it. The seller deposits the $900 in another bank. That bank keeps $90 as reserves and lends $810, and so on. At each step, a new deposit is created, and new deposits are part of the money supply. Figure 14.13 follows the first ten rounds. The original $1,000 has become more than $6,500 in deposits after ten rounds, and it would approach $10,000 if the chain continued.

The ratio that predicts the final result is the money multiplier: 1 divided by the reserve ratio. With a 10 percent reserve ratio, the multiplier is 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10, so a $1,000 deposit could support up to $10,000 in total deposits. The real banking system is more complicated. Banks do not lend out every dollar they can, some borrowers keep cash instead of depositing it, and the demand for loans has limits. In March 2020, the Federal Reserve reduced its reserve requirement ratios to zero, which means that banks are no longer required to hold a minimum fraction of deposits as reserves. Banks still hold reserves for safety and to settle payments, and regulators limit how much a bank can lend by requiring minimum capital. The lesson of the example is that lending creates deposits, and deposits are money.

Key Concepts

Deregulation and Consolidation

For decades after the Great Depression, banking was tightly regulated. Rules capped the interest rates that banks could pay on deposits, limited where a bank could open branches, and kept commercial banking (taking deposits and making loans) separate from investment banking (selling stocks and bonds). Beginning in 1980, Congress began to remove some of these limits, a process called deregulation. The Depository Institutions Deregulation and Monetary Control Act of 1980 began phasing out the interest-rate caps and gave all depository institutions access to Federal Reserve services. The Riegle-Neal Act of 1994 allowed banks to open branches across state lines. The Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act of 1999 removed the wall between commercial and investment banking.

Supporters of deregulation argued that it would increase competition, give savers better rates, allow banks to spread risk across regions, and make U.S. banks more competitive around the world. Critics argued that it allowed banks to take on more risk and to grow so large that their failure would threaten the entire economy, a problem known as "too big to fail." Both claims have some support. Banking did consolidate. As Figure 14.10 shows, the number of insured institutions fell from nearly 18,000 in 1984 to about 4,300 in 2025, while the largest banks grew much larger. Economists still debate how much deregulation contributed to the financial crisis of 2007 to 2009, which also involved falling home prices, risky mortgage lending, and a global flow of savings into the United States.

After the crisis, Congress passed the Dodd-Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010. It created new rules on capital, required large banks to pass stress tests, and created the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau. Some people believe these rules made the system safer, and others believe they burden community banks and restrict credit. Lawmakers and regulators continue to adjust them. Whatever your view, the lesson is that banking regulation involves trade-offs among safety, competition, and access to credit.

Key Concepts

From Passbooks to Phones

Technology has changed banking at least as much as regulation. In the late 1960s, the first automated teller machines (ATMs) began dispensing cash outside banking hours. Today ATMs are found in grocery stores, gas stations, airports, and school campuses, and they let customers deposit, withdraw, and check balances. The debit card spread in the 1990s. It looks like a credit card, but it draws money directly from the customer's checking account, so a purchase is paid for right away. Stored-value cards, such as prepaid cards and transit cards, hold a fixed amount that the user loads in advance.

In the 1990s and 2000s, banks added online banking, and then phone apps with mobile check deposit. In 2023, according to the FDIC's national survey of households, 48.3 percent of banked households said that mobile banking was their main way to get into their account, up from 43.5 percent in 2021. Bank tellers were the main method for 15.1 percent, and ATMs or kiosks for 13.8 percent. Payment apps now let people send money to a friend in seconds, and contactless cards and phones let customers pay with a tap. Some new financial companies, often called fintechs, offer banking-like services through an app while relying on a partner bank to hold the deposits. If you use one, check whether the money is actually held at an FDIC-insured bank.

More recently, digital tokens such as cryptocurrencies and so-called stablecoins have entered the conversation. Supporters say they could make payments cheaper and faster. Critics point to price swings, scams, and the lack of deposit insurance for most of these assets. In 2025, Congress passed a law setting federal rules for payment stablecoins. Whether these tools will become widely used money, rather than speculative investments, is a question that economists, regulators, and consumers are still answering.

Technology has also made banking riskier in some ways. In March 2023, depositors at Silicon Valley Bank, a California bank that served many technology firms, withdrew about $42 billion in a single day, largely through online and mobile banking, after worries about the bank's losses spread on social media. Regulators closed the bank on March 10, 2023, and in the days that followed, other banks failed. A crisis that would once have taken days now moved in hours. Regulators took the unusual step of protecting all depositors at the failed banks, including those above the $250,000 limit, to prevent wider panic.

Key Concepts

Who Is Left Out?

Not everyone has joined the banking system. People who have no checking or savings account are called unbanked. The FDIC estimated that 4.2 percent of U.S. households, about 5.6 million, were unbanked in 2023, a record low. The Federal Reserve's 2025 survey of adults found that 6 percent were unbanked, a figure that uses a different measure (adults rather than households) and cannot be compared directly. People without accounts may depend on check-cashing stores and prepaid cards, which often charge fees, and they may find it harder to save or build credit. Reasons for not having an account include a lack of trust in banks, minimum balance requirements and fees, and not having enough money to need an account.

Banks and credit unions are experimenting with accounts that have no overdraft fees and low minimum deposits, and some have created ways for people to open accounts using a phone. Employers, including some farms and packing houses, now offer direct deposit and pay cards. Some governments and charities have built free tax-preparation and savings programs. Whether the number of unbanked households keeps falling will depend on both incentives and trust.

Key Concepts

Protecting Your Money

Faster payments and more apps create new opportunities for fraud. The most common problems are phishing (a message that looks like it comes from a bank but is designed to steal passwords), imposter scams (a caller who claims to be from a bank or government agency), and scams in which a victim is persuaded to send money to a stranger. Once a payment is sent through some quick-transfer services, it may be impossible to get back. Federal rules give consumers some protections against unauthorized card and electronic transfers, and banks generally must investigate errors that are reported promptly. But a payment that you were tricked into sending yourself is treated differently from one that a thief made without your permission, and protections vary.

A few habits help. Use strong, unique passwords and turn on two-factor authentication. Never give a verification code to anyone who contacts you. If a message claims to be from your bank, hang up or close it and contact the bank using the phone number on your card or the official app. Check your accounts often, set up alerts, and report problems right away. Before you send money to someone you do not know, remember that real banks and agencies do not ask customers to move money to a safe account. Finally, keep your deposits within the insurance limits: $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. A household with more than that can use accounts at more than one insured bank or different ownership categories. The FDIC's online tool, EDIE, can help estimate coverage.

CASE STUDY

Borrowing for College:
Student Loans in the United States

A college education is one of the largest purchases many families ever make, and many pay for it by borrowing. Student loans are a form of the credit that banks and governments create, and they show how interest rates, deposits, and decisions about the future all connect.

Background

How Students Pay for Higher Education

Families pay for college from several sources: savings, current income, scholarships and grants (which do not have to be repaid), earnings from work, and loans. Most students who borrow use federal student loans, which the U.S. Department of Education makes directly. Banks, credit unions, and other lenders also make private student loans. Federal loans carry a fixed interest rate that is set each year. By law, the rate is based on the high yield at the May auction of 10-year U.S. Treasury notes plus a set amount that depends on the type of loan. For loans first paid out between July 1, 2026, and June 30, 2027, the 10-year Treasury yield at the May 12, 2026, auction was 4.468 percent. The resulting interest rate is 6.52 percent for undergraduate loans, 8.07 percent for graduate and professional unsubsidized loans, and 9.07 percent for PLUS loans. Once a loan is made, its rate does not change.

Student borrowing grew rapidly in the 2000s and 2010s as college costs rose. According to the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, student loan balances stood at about $1.656 trillion in the third quarter of 2026, among the largest categories of household debt after mortgages ($13.4 trillion), auto loans ($1.73 trillion), and credit cards ($1.29 trillion). Because borrowers can wait years to see whether the education pays off, student loans carry risks that other loans do not. Borrowers may leave college without finishing a degree, may find work that pays less than they expected, or may face a downturn.

In July 2025, Congress changed the rules for new federal loans. Starting July 1, 2026, new borrowers can no longer take out Grad PLUS loans, annual and total limits apply to graduate and parent borrowing, and a new Repayment Assistance Plan joined the Tiered Standard Plan as options for new borrowers. Supporters of the changes say that they will limit rising prices and protect taxpayers from lending that may not be repaid. Critics say that they could limit access for students who cannot borrow elsewhere. The questions below do not ask you to decide which side is right. They ask you to read the evidence carefully.

"Easy borrowing can push up prices, because colleges know that students can pay with loans. Taxpayers end up covering loans that are not repaid, including by people who never went to college. Chapter 14 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Money: Its Functions and Properties
Money is anything widely accepted in payment. It serves as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value, and good money is durable, portable, divisible, uniform, limited in supply, and acceptable. Fiat money has value because of trust and legal tender status. M1 and M2 measure the money supply, and nominal values must be adjusted for price changes to find real values.
Section 2 · The Development of U.S. Banking
Banks are intermediaries that earn a profit on the spread between loan and deposit rates. U.S. banking moved from the First and Second Banks through state and national banks to the Federal Reserve, and bank runs led to deposit insurance, now $250,000 per depositor. Commercial banks, savings institutions, and credit unions all offer insured deposits.
Section 3 · Innovations in Modern Banking
Banks offer accounts, loans, and services. Short-term and long-term interest rates can move differently, and the yield spread is a signal. In fractional reserve banking, lending creates deposits, and the money multiplier equals 1 divided by the reserve ratio. Deregulation and technology brought benefits and risks, and some households remain unbanked.
