Concept Review

In Chapter 4 through Chapter 6 you studied the markets for goods. In this chapter you will study the market for labor, in which workers supply their time and skills and employers demand them. You will learn why wages differ among jobs, how the minimum wage works, how the American labor force has changed, how workers and money move across borders, how workers have organized into unions to bargain with employers, and how unemployment insurance protects workers who lose their jobs.

Chapter 9 Key Concept

The labor market is the market in which workers sell their time and skills and employers buy them. The price in this market is the wage. Wages are set by the demand for workers and the supply of workers, and they reflect the productivity of workers, which is the amount they produce in an hour.

Why the Concept Matters

Why does an electrician earn more than a cashier? Why do some Kern County jobs pay twice as much as others? You will soon enter the labor market yourself, and the choices you make about education and training will affect your income for decades. Understanding the market for labor helps you make those choices, and it helps you understand the minimum wage, unemployment insurance, and union debates you will hear as a voter.

Key Concepts

Demand and Supply of Workers

In Chapter 6 you saw that a price is set where the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. The same model applies to workers. In the labor market, employers demand labor because workers help them produce goods and services, and workers supply labor because they want income. The price of labor is the wage, the payment a worker receives for his or her work, usually measured per hour or per year. Every job you have ever heard of, from picking almonds in Wasco to teaching at Shafter High School, has a wage set in some labor market.

The demand for labor slopes downward: the lower the wage, the more workers an employer is willing to hire, since cheaper labor makes more projects worthwhile. The supply of labor slopes upward: the higher the wage, the more people are willing to work, because a higher wage makes working more attractive compared with staying home, going to school, or taking another job. Where the two cross is the equilibrium wage. When the wage is above equilibrium, more people want jobs than there are jobs, and wages tend to fall. When the wage is below equilibrium, employers cannot fill all openings, and wages tend to rise.

Employers demand labor because of what labor produces. Economists call this derived demand: the demand for an hour of a worker's time comes from the demand for the goods that the worker helps to make. If more people buy solar panels, installers are in higher demand. If fewer people buy newspapers, the demand for printing workers falls. Because wages come from both sides of the market, no single employer and no single worker decides the wage alone. Standard 12.4.3 asks you to use the laws of demand and supply and the concept of productivity to explain wage differences, and this section does exactly that.

Figure 9.1 shows a hypothetical labor market for warehouse workers in a Central Valley town. At $14 an hour, employers want 80 workers but only 40 people are willing to work for that wage, so there is a shortage of 40 workers, and employers must raise their offers to fill jobs. At $22 an hour, only 40 workers are wanted but 80 people are looking for the work, so there is a surplus of 40 workers. The market settles at $18 an hour, where 60 workers are wanted and 60 are willing, and the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied.

Now suppose that online shopping grows and a new distribution center opens nearby. The demand for warehouse workers rises, because employers want 20 more workers at every wage. The demand curve shifts to the right, from D1 to D2. At the old wage of $18, employers now want 80 workers but only 60 are willing, so a shortage appears. Competition among employers pushes the wage up to $20, and the number of workers rises to 70. A change in demand has raised both the wage and the number of jobs. You can reverse the process. If a distribution center closed, the demand curve would shift left, and the equilibrium wage and employment would both fall.

Real labor markets are messier than this graph. Workers differ in skill, jobs differ in location and hours, and employers and workers do not always have full information. Wages do not always change quickly, because contracts, habits, and laws can hold them in place for a time. Even so, the model helps you predict the direction of change. When more employers compete for the same workers, wages tend to rise. When more workers compete for the same jobs, wages tend to fall.

What causes the demand for labor to shift? The most important cause is a change in the demand for the product the workers make. When a late freeze reduces the citrus harvest, the demand for pickers and packers falls. When new housing is built in Bakersfield, the demand for electricians, roofers, and framers rises. A second cause is productivity. A worker who produces more in an hour is worth more to an employer, so a rise in productivity increases the demand for that worker. A third cause is the price of other inputs. If machines become cheaper and better, an employer may substitute machines for some workers, and the demand for those workers falls. If a machine makes workers more productive without replacing them, the demand for them can rise.

The supply of labor shifts, too. A larger population or a rise in immigration increases the number of people looking for work, which shifts supply to the right. A new community college program that trains welders increases the supply of welders in the county. An increase in the pay of alternative jobs reduces the supply of workers for a particular job, because some workers move to the better offer. Preferences matter as well. If more people want flexible schedules, they may accept lower pay for jobs that offer them, or they may refuse jobs that do not. Laws also shift supply, such as the rules that govern who may work and at what age.

In Kern County, the seasons create regular shifts. In the fall and spring harvest weeks, the demand for farm labor rises sharply, and growers compete to hire crews. After the harvest ends, the demand falls and many workers must find other work or move on. The result is that wages and employment in farm labor change through the year. Economists call this seasonal change, and it is one reason why local unemployment rates can swing from month to month even when the economy is steady.

Key Concepts

Why Wages Differ

Wages differ widely. A surgeon earns far more than a cashier, and a welder earns more than a person with no training. Three ideas explain much of the difference. The first is productivity: the amount of goods or services a worker produces in a given amount of time. A worker who produces more in an hour is worth more to an employer, who is willing to pay a higher wage. An employer will not pay a worker more than the worker adds to the business, or the business would lose money on that worker.

The second is human capital: the knowledge, skills, and training that a worker has acquired. Education and experience raise a worker's productivity, so they usually raise wages. Human capital is a kind of capital because, like a tractor or a warehouse, it takes time and money to create and it produces income for years. A student who attends a two-year technical program pays tuition and gives up wages while in school, in the hope of earning more later. Economists call this an investment in human capital.

The third is scarcity. A job that few people can do, or that few people want to do, tends to pay more. Many people can sort boxes with a few hours of instruction, so the supply of workers for that job is large and the wage is low. Few people can repair a wind turbine, so the supply is small and the wage is higher. Scarcity is not the same as importance. A job can matter greatly to society and still pay modestly if many people are willing and able to do it.

In 2025, workers age 25 and over with a bachelor's degree had median weekly earnings of $1,578, compared with $966 for workers with only a high school diploma and $770 for those without a high school diploma. The graph describes a relationship, but it does not prove that a degree by itself causes higher pay. Students who finish college may differ from other students in ways that also affect earnings, such as family support, health, and the neighborhoods where they grew up. Degrees also differ widely in value. A graduate in one field may earn far more than a graduate in another, and a skilled tradesperson with an apprenticeship may out-earn a worker with a degree in a different field. Cost matters too. Tuition, books, and years without full-time pay must be weighed against higher earnings later. Careful economists therefore say that education is associated with higher earnings and likely raises productivity, while warning against treating the graph as a guarantee for any one student.

For Kern County students, the choices are real and varied. Bakersfield College, California State University, Bakersfield, apprenticeship programs run by trade groups, and employers' own training programs all offer paths to human capital. Each path has costs and benefits, which you will compare in the Your Economic Choices feature of Section 2. The key idea is that skills are valued in the labor market, and acquiring them is one of the few ways that a worker can raise the productivity, and therefore the wage, that he or she brings to an employer.

Some jobs pay more because they are dangerous, unpleasant, or inconvenient. Economists call the extra pay a compensating differential, because it compensates the worker for a disadvantage of the job. A roofer who works at heights in the summer sun, an oil-field worker who works long shifts far from home, and a nurse who works overnight may all earn more than they would in similar jobs without those drawbacks. If a job pays no extra, fewer people are willing to take it, the supply of workers falls, and the wage rises until the job can be filled. The wage differences are the market's way of rewarding people who accept hardship.

Other factors also affect wages. The supply of workers in an area matters: a town with many people and few jobs has lower wages than a town with the same jobs and fewer people. The strength of the demand for the product matters, as you saw in the market for warehouse workers. Unions can raise wages for their members, and Section 3 examines how. Laws such as the minimum wage set a floor under wages, as you will read below. Employers' choices matter as well: some pay more than the market requires to keep experienced workers and reduce the costs of hiring and training replacements.

Discrimination is a different kind of wage difference. It occurs when workers are paid or hired differently for reasons unrelated to their productivity, such as race, sex, or national origin. Federal laws, including the Equal Pay Act of 1963 and Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, prohibit pay discrimination on those grounds. Economists and policymakers debate how much of the gaps in average earnings among groups is due to discrimination and how much is due to differences in hours, occupations, and experience, and the answer varies by case. For an economist, the question is always whether a pay difference reflects productivity and choices or reflects unequal treatment.

Key Concepts

The Minimum Wage

A minimum wage is the lowest hourly wage that an employer may legally pay. It is a price floor in the labor market. Congress set the first federal minimum wage in the Fair Labor Standards Act, which President Franklin Roosevelt signed on June 25, 1938. The first rate was 25 cents an hour. Congress has raised the federal minimum many times since then, to $1.60 in 1968, $3.35 in 1981, and $7.25, the rate in effect since July 24, 2009. Farm workers were not covered at first. Amendments passed in 1966 extended the minimum wage to some farm workers, and a rate of $1.00 an hour took effect for them on February 1, 1967, two years after the Delano strike began.

States may set a higher minimum wage, and when state and federal rules differ, an employer must follow the stricter one. California is one of the states with a higher minimum. The statewide minimum was $16.50 an hour in 2025 and rose to $16.90 on January 1, 2026, according to the California Department of Industrial Relations. Some cities and some industries have still higher minimums. Figure 9.5 compares the federal minimum at several dates with the California statewide minimum. The bars are not adjusted for inflation, so you cannot use them to compare purchasing power across years, but they show how much higher California's floor is than the federal floor.

To see the model at work, return to Figure 9.1. If the equilibrium wage for warehouse workers is $18 and the law set a minimum of $16, the minimum would have no effect, because employers already pay more than the floor. If the law set a minimum of $20, the floor would be above equilibrium. At $20, 70 workers would want jobs but employers would want only 50, a surplus of 20 workers. In the model, the workers who keep their jobs gain, and the workers who lose jobs or cannot find them are worse off. The size of the effect in real life depends on how far the floor is above the wage that the market would pay.

Economists and the public disagree about the minimum wage, and it helps to separate what the evidence shows from what is a matter of values. Supporters, including many labor groups and advocates for low-income families, argue that a higher minimum raises the incomes of the lowest-paid workers, reduces poverty, and may reduce turnover and increase the money that workers spend in their communities. Critics, including many business groups and some economists, argue that a minimum wage that is too high makes some workers more expensive than the value they produce, so employers may cut hours, hire fewer workers, or replace workers with machines, and that the people who lose jobs are often the youngest and least experienced.

The best-known recent evidence comes from the Congressional Budget Office, a nonpartisan agency of Congress. In a 2019 report, the CBO estimated that raising the federal minimum wage to $15 an hour by 2025 would raise pay for at least 17 million workers and lift about 1.3 million people out of poverty, but would also reduce employment by a median estimate of 1.3 million jobs in an average week, with a range from about zero to 3.7 million. The CBO estimated that a $10 minimum would have little effect on jobs, and that a $12 minimum would cost about 300,000 jobs. It noted that many studies have found little or no effect of minimum wages on employment while many others have found substantial job losses, and that there is considerable uncertainty.

Because the effects depend on the local labor market, a minimum wage that is modest in a high-wage city may be large in a low-wage region. This is one reason that some people argue the minimum wage should be set by states or cities, while others argue for one national floor. A voter who weighs these arguments has to decide how much weight to give to higher pay for workers who keep their jobs, how much to give to the risk of fewer jobs, and whether other policies, such as tax credits for low-income workers, might reach the same goal. Those are questions about values as well as economics, and this textbook does not answer them for you.

Key Concepts

Productivity and the Standard of Living

Productivity is also the main source of rising living standards. A nation's standard of living is the quality of life measured by the goods and services that people can buy with their incomes. When workers produce more per hour, the economy produces more goods and services for each person, and wages can rise without causing prices to rise too. This is why economists pay so much attention to productivity. It is the way that a society can have more of everything without taking from someone else.

Workers become more productive in several ways: through more education and training (human capital), through better tools and machines (capital), and through better methods of organizing work (technology). A Kern County farm worker who uses a mechanical harvester, or an oil-field technician who uses computer-controlled equipment, produces more in an hour than a worker who uses only hand tools. Over time, the rise in productivity explains why most Americans can afford goods that were once luxuries.

The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics measures productivity as output per hour worked. According to the BLS, productivity in the nonfarm business sector rose 2.3 percent in 2025 and has grown at an average of about 2.2 percent a year since 1947. That may sound small, but small yearly gains add up. A useful shortcut, called the rule of 70, says that a quantity growing at a steady rate doubles in about 70 divided by that rate in years. At 2.2 percent a year, output per hour doubles in about 32 years. A worker in the next generation can produce about twice as much in an hour as a worker today.

MATH HANDBOOKMeasuring productivity. Productivity = output ÷ hours worked. If a crew of 10 workers picks 2,000 pounds of produce in 4 hours, the crew's output per worker-hour is 2,000 ÷ (10 × 4) = 50 pounds per worker-hour. Rule of 70: doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate. At 2.2% a year, 70 ÷ 2.2 ≈ 32 years.

Increases in productivity do not guarantee that every worker gains. If a new machine allows one worker to do the work of three, an employer may need fewer workers, and the workers who lose their jobs must find new ones. Economists note that new technology also creates new kinds of jobs, but the change can be hard on those who must retrain. Section 2 looks at these changes in more detail.

Economists also disagree about how the gains from higher productivity are shared. In theory, when productivity rises, firms can pay workers more, because each hour of work produces more value. Some economists and labor advocates argue that in recent decades the pay of typical workers has not kept pace with productivity because of changes in unions, globalization, and the power of employers. Other economists argue that when total pay and benefits, such as health insurance, are counted and prices are measured carefully, workers' compensation has followed productivity more closely. The measurement is difficult and the debate is not settled, so you should be careful about any claim that states the answer with certainty.

What is not in dispute is that productivity matters for wages over the long run. A worker who produces little cannot be paid much by any employer that wants to stay in business, and a country whose workers produce more can afford a higher standard of living. That is the reason that governments, schools, and companies invest in education and equipment, and the reason that individual workers invest in skills.

Key Concepts

Who Is in the Labor Force?

Economists count the people who are working or looking for work to measure the supply of labor. The civilian labor force consists of all people age 16 and over who are not in the military or in institutions such as prisons and who either have a job or are actively looking for one. People who are in school full time, retired, caring for family, or not looking for work are not counted in the labor force. A person who has a job is employed. A person who has no job but is actively looking is unemployed. A person who is neither is not in the labor force at all.

The labor force participation rate is the percentage of the civilian population age 16 and over that is in the labor force. Because it counts people who have jobs and people who are looking, it shows how much of the population is offering its time and skills to the labor market. A high rate means that most adults are working or looking for work. A low rate means that many adults are students, retirees, caregivers, or people who have stopped looking. Chapter 17 explains how the government measures unemployment and why it is not the same as the participation rate.

Local numbers show how the ideas work. In August 2026, the BLS estimated that the Bakersfield–Delano area had a civilian labor force of 414,000 people. Of these, 379,300 were employed and 34,800 were unemployed, for an unemployment rate of 8.4 percent (34,800 ÷ 414,000). These are preliminary figures that are not adjusted for the seasons. Because farm work in Kern County is seasonal, local rates can swing through the year, so economists compare an area with the same month in other years instead of comparing it with the national seasonally adjusted rate.

Key Concepts

A Changing Mix of Workers

The mix of workers has changed greatly. The biggest change in the second half of the twentieth century was the rise in the number of women who work for pay. Figure 9.6 shows the labor force participation rate of men and women. In January 1948, 32.0 percent of women age 16 and over were in the labor force, compared with 86.7 percent of men. By January 2000, the rate for women had climbed to 60.1 percent. During World War II, millions of women had taken jobs in factories and shipyards while many men served in the military, and many kept working in later decades.

Economists point to several reasons for the long rise. More women completed high school and college. Laws and attitudes changed, so that jobs and training that had been closed to women opened. Family sizes fell, and household technology such as washing machines and refrigerators reduced time spent on housework. Rising wages for women raised the opportunity cost of staying at home. Because many of these changes happened together, economists debate how much weight to give each reason, and values and personal choices matter as well as incentives.

The graph also shows a second trend. The participation rate for men has fallen steadily, from 86.7 percent in 1948 to 67.3 percent in October 2026. Part of this fall reflects longer schooling, earlier retirement than in the past, and changes in the kinds of jobs available. The rate for women peaked in 2000 and has eased to 56.9 percent. The gap between men and women has narrowed from 54.7 percentage points in 1948 to 10.4 percentage points in October 2026, but it has not closed. The labor force has also grown more diverse in age, background, and education, and workers are on average better educated than in the past.

Key Concepts

Why Participation Has Fallen

Figure 9.7 shows the participation rate for all workers. It was 66.8 percent in January 1990, rose to 67.3 percent in January 2000, and then fell to 64.8 percent in 2010, 63.3 percent in January 2020, and 61.9 percent in October 2026. Because men and women together make up the whole population, the fall since 2000 reflects both the decline for men and the slight easing for women.

The most important cause of the long decline is the aging of the population. A growing share of Americans are in their sixties and seventies, and most people in those ages are retired. Even if every person at each age kept the same behavior, the overall rate would fall as the share of older people rose. A second cause is that more young people have stayed in school longer. A third is that some workers left the labor force during and after recessions and did not return. Each cause has a different meaning for the economy, which is why economists look at participation by age group and not only the overall rate.

A falling participation rate is not always a sign of a weak economy. If it falls because more people are retired, the economy may be healthy. If it falls because discouraged workers have given up looking for jobs, the economy may be weak. The rate also affects growth. With a smaller share of the population working, fewer workers support the same number of retirees, which is one reason that Chapter 18 discusses the cost of programs for older Americans.

Key Concepts

From Factories to Services

The kinds of goods and services that the economy produces have changed as much as the workers. Standard 12.4.2 asks you to describe the current economy and labor market, including the types of goods and services produced. The American economy produces far more services than goods. Health care, education, retail, restaurants, and business services employ a large share of workers, while manufacturing employs a smaller share than it did decades ago. Figure 9.8 shows the change. In January 1950, manufacturing provided 13.2 million of the 43.5 million nonfarm jobs, or 30.2 percent. In October 2026, it provided 12.7 million of 159.3 million, or 8.0 percent.

Notice that the number of manufacturing jobs did not fall steadily. It rose until a peak of about 19.6 million in June 1979, and then fell, with sharp drops between 2000 and 2010. Over the same years the total number of nonfarm jobs grew very quickly, so the manufacturing share fell much faster than the number of jobs. Economists give several reasons. Factories became more productive, so they could produce more with fewer workers. Consumers spent a larger share of their rising incomes on services. And competition from imports affected some industries, which you will read about below.

Key Concepts

Technology, Automation, and Skills

Two forces have changed the kinds of jobs available: technology and international competition. Automation is the use of machines and computers to do work that people once did. Machines have replaced many jobs in factories, on farms, and in offices, and in Kern County, machines now help harvest and process crops that were once picked and sorted entirely by hand. Standard 12.4.2 asks you to describe the effects of rapid technological change on the economy and the labor market, and the key idea is that technology changes the demand for different kinds of workers.

Technology can replace workers who do routine tasks, as when a machine sorts fruit by size and color. It can also raise the demand for workers whose skills work together with the machines, such as technicians who install and repair equipment, programmers who write the software that runs it, and managers who plan how to use it. Because the new jobs often require more education or training than the old ones, the workers who lose jobs may not be the same people who gain them. This is why economists say that technology raises productivity and incomes for the economy as a whole but can create hardship for some workers during the change.

The skills that workers need have risen with the change. Employers say that they value reading, math, communication, problem solving, teamwork, and the ability to learn new tools. Technical skills matter, but they can become outdated quickly, so the ability to keep learning matters as much as any single skill. Many workers will change jobs, and even careers, several times. This is the reason that schools, community colleges, apprenticeship programs, and employers all offer ways to build new skills, and that policymakers debate how best to help workers who must retrain.

Key Concepts

Trade and International Competition

International trade has also reshaped the labor market. Standard 12.4.2 asks you to describe the impact of international competition. When a country imports a good, workers in that country who make the same good face competition from foreign producers, and when a country exports a good, workers who make it gain a larger market. Competition from imports has hurt workers in some industries, such as textile and furniture manufacturing, while trade has created jobs in other industries, such as agriculture and aircraft. California's farms sell large amounts of almonds, pistachios, grapes, and other crops to buyers around the world, which supports farm jobs and packing-house jobs in the Central Valley.

Some companies practice outsourcing, which means hiring other firms, often in other countries, to make parts or perform services. A company that outsources can lower its costs, which may lower prices for consumers and help the company compete. But the workers whose jobs move away can lose income, and they may find it hard to move to a new town or learn new skills. Economists generally agree that trade raises the total output of the countries involved, and they also agree that the gains and costs fall on different groups of people. Whether and how to help those who lose is a policy question on which people disagree.

The two effects are not always easy to separate from technology. When a factory closes, the cause may be a foreign competitor, a new machine, a change in consumer taste, or all three. Careful studies try to separate the effects, and their results differ by industry and time period. For students, the useful lesson is to ask what share of a job loss is due to trade and what share to other causes before accepting a simple explanation. Chapter 21 explains the gains from trade and the policies that governments use to respond to its costs.

Key Concepts

Labor and Capital Across Borders

Workers and money now move across national borders more than ever before. Standard 12.4.4 asks you to explain the effects of the international mobility of capital and labor on the U.S. economy. Mobility means the ability to move. Labor is mobile when workers move to another country for jobs, and capital is mobile when money or equipment is invested in another country. Both affect the labor market in the United States. We begin with labor.

Immigration is the movement of people into a country to live there. Immigrants add to the supply of labor and also to the demand for goods and services, because they buy things. In 2025, the BLS reports, foreign-born workers made up 19.1 percent of the U.S. civilian labor force. Their participation rate was 66.3 percent, higher than the 61.6 percent for native-born workers, and their unemployment rate was 4.2 percent compared with 4.3 percent. Foreign-born workers were more likely than native-born workers to work in service occupations (21.2 percent compared with 15.6 percent) and in natural resources, construction, and maintenance (13.3 percent compared with 7.9 percent). How does immigration affect the U.S. economy? Economists agree on some points and disagree about others. Capital is mobile too. Foreign direct investment is investment in which a business in one country builds, buys, or expands a business in another country. When a foreign company builds a factory in the United States, buys an American firm, or opens a store here, that is foreign direct investment into the United States. When an American company builds a plant abroad, that is foreign direct investment out of the United States. Money that flows into the country adds to its capital, which can raise the productivity and wages of workers who use it.

The size of the flow is large. The Bureau of Economic Analysis reports that U.S. affiliates of foreign multinational companies employed 8.57 million workers in the United States in 2024, a 0.2 percent increase from 8.56 million in 2023. These are jobs in the United States that exist because foreign-owned firms chose to invest here. Those jobs often involve new machinery and technology, and they can bring new methods to American workers. At the same time, American firms invest abroad, and some of that investment replaces production that might have been located here and some of it serves foreign customers that cannot be reached by exports alone.

What are the effects on the U.S. economy? Supporters of open capital flows say that foreign investment brings capital, technology, and jobs, lowers prices, and lets American firms reach large markets. Critics say that capital that moves abroad can mean the loss of jobs at home and that foreign ownership can give outsiders control of key businesses. Most economists see net gains from capital mobility for the economy as a whole, and they also stress that some workers and communities bear costs. As with trade and immigration, whether and how to respond are policy questions. Chapter 21 and Chapter 22 return to these ideas in the global economy.

Key Concepts

Workers Join Together

A single worker usually has little bargaining power against a large employer. If one worker asks for a raise and the employer says no, the worker may have to quit, while the employer can hire someone else. A labor union is an organization of workers who join together to improve their wages, benefits, and working conditions. The main tool of a union is collective bargaining: negotiations between union representatives and an employer over wages, hours, benefits, and conditions of work. The result is a written contract that covers all of the workers whom the union represents. In the language of this chapter, a union is a way for the sellers of labor to act together instead of one by one.

Unions grew in the United States in the 1800s as factories replaced workshops and farms. Early unions were often organized by craft, such as carpenters or printers. The American Federation of Labor (AFL), founded in December 1886 in Columbus, Ohio, was a federation of craft unions led by Samuel Gompers. It began with delegates from 13 national unions. In the 1930s, leaders such as John L. Lewis argued that workers in mass-production industries such as steel and autos should organize all workers in a plant, regardless of skill, and the Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) formed as a rival to the AFL. The two groups merged in 1955 to form the AFL-CIO, which remains the largest federation of unions in the country.

Early unions faced fierce opposition. Employers sometimes fired union members, used court orders against strikes, and in some cases hired private guards. Strikes, which you will read about below, were sometimes met with violence. Over time, public opinion and new laws changed the balance. California has its own union history. In October 1938, cotton pickers in Kern County held a strike, and the photographer Dorothea Lange made portraits of workers and strike leaders there that November. Twenty-seven years later, farm workers in Delano would begin the strike you will study in the Case Study.

Key Concepts

How Unions Gain Benefits for Their Members

Standard 12.4.1 asks you to understand the procedures that unions use to gain benefits for their members. The first step is organizing. Under federal law, workers who want a union in most private-sector workplaces can ask the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to hold a secret-ballot election. If a majority of the workers who vote choose the union, the union becomes their representative, and the employer must bargain with it in good faith. Workers and unions may also win recognition in other ways, but the election is the best-known path. The rules are different for public employees, who are covered mostly by state law, and different again for farm workers in California, as you will see in the Case Study.

The second step is bargaining. Representatives of the union and the employer meet to negotiate a contract. Contracts commonly cover wages, overtime, health and retirement benefits, work schedules, how workers are promoted or laid off, and safety rules. They usually include a grievance procedure, a series of steps that a worker can follow if he or she believes that the contract has been broken, and they often last for several years. A union pays for its staff and its lawyers with dues paid by the workers it represents, and contracts may include rules about who must pay dues, which you will read about under right-to-work laws.

Unions use other tools as well. They may help train workers through apprenticeship programs, which combine paid on-the-job training with classroom study. They lobby lawmakers on issues such as the minimum wage and workplace safety. They may ask the public to support a boycott, a refusal to buy a company's products, as the farm workers did in the Case Study. And in the last resort, they may call a strike, a work stoppage by employees to put pressure on an employer. Each tool is a way to raise the bargaining power of workers relative to the employer, and each has costs and risks for the workers who use it.

Key Concepts

Settling Disputes

When an employer and a union cannot agree, there are several ways to settle the dispute. The first step is negotiation: the two sides bargain directly. If bargaining fails, they may turn to mediation, in which a neutral third party helps the two sides reach an agreement but cannot force one. If mediation does not work, they may use arbitration, in which a neutral arbitrator hears both sides and makes a decision, which is often binding. Federal agencies, such as the Federal Mediation and Conciliation Service, help with mediation.

If bargaining breaks down, workers may stage a strike: a work stoppage by employees to put pressure on an employer. In a strike, workers lose wages while they are off the job, and the employer loses production, so both sides have an incentive to settle. Strikers often form a picket line outside the workplace to publicize the dispute. Employers may respond by hiring replacement workers, or they may lock out workers, which means keeping them out of the workplace. Under the Taft-Hartley Act, the president may seek a court order to delay a strike or lockout that threatens national health or safety for an 80-day cooling-off period.

Most contracts are settled without a strike, and most strikes end in agreements. Both sides weigh costs and benefits, as in any economic choice. A union weighs the raise it may win against the wages its members lose while on strike. An employer weighs the cost of higher wages against the loss of sales and customers during a strike. When each side has a good idea of the other's position, a settlement is more likely, which is why economists say that information and bargaining power matter as much as the size of the offers.

Key Concepts

Laws That Shaped Labor Relations

Federal law has defined the rights of workers and employers. The National Labor Relations Act of 1935, often called the Wagner Act, was signed by President Franklin Roosevelt on July 5, 1935. It protected the right of most private-sector workers to organize, to join unions, and to bargain collectively. It created the National Labor Relations Board (NLRB) to supervise union elections and to stop unfair labor practices. The act did not cover agricultural workers or domestic workers, a fact that matters in this chapter's Case Study.

The Fair Labor Standards Act of 1938 (FLSA), signed on June 25, 1938, set the first federal minimum wage at 25 cents an hour, required overtime pay of time and a half after a weekly limit of hours (44 hours at first, 40 hours today) for most covered workers, and limited child labor. The federal minimum wage is $7.25 an hour today, though many states, including California, set a higher minimum. Standard 12.4.1 asks you to understand the effects of the minimum wage, which Section 1 examined.

The Taft-Hartley Act of 1947 placed limits on unions. It became law on June 23, 1947, when Congress overrode President Harry Truman's veto. It outlawed the closed shop, in which an employer may hire only workers who are already union members, though it still allowed a union shop, in which new hires must join the union after a set period. It allowed the president to seek an 80-day cooling-off period in strikes that endanger national health or safety. And through a provision called Section 14(b), it let states pass right-to-work laws: state laws that prohibit requiring workers to join a union or pay union dues as a condition of keeping a job. Public-sector workers, such as teachers and firefighters, are covered by different laws, mostly state laws.

Key Concepts

The Effects of Unionization

Standard 12.4.1 also asks you to understand the effects of unionization. Economists study the effects on wages first. According to the BLS, in 2025 full-time wage and salary workers who were union members had median usual weekly earnings of $1,404, compared with $1,174 for nonunion workers. Nonunion earnings were 84 percent of union earnings. The raw gap, however, does not prove that unions cause the difference. Union members and nonunion workers differ in age, experience, occupation, and industry, and unions are more common in some jobs than in others. Economists who compare similar workers generally find that union workers earn more, but the gap is smaller than the raw numbers suggest.

Unions can affect more than wages. Contracts may include health insurance, retirement plans, paid leave, rules about layoffs and promotions, and safety procedures. Supporters of unions argue that these provisions improve working conditions, give workers a voice in decisions, and reduce turnover, so employers also gain from more stable and experienced workforces. Unions may also narrow pay differences within a workplace, because contracts often set wage scales for all workers in a job. Some studies find that workers' safety and training improve in unionized workplaces, but results vary by industry and time.

Critics make different arguments. They say that when a union raises wages above the level that employers would pay in a competitive market, the higher cost may lead employers to hire fewer workers, raise prices, or move work to nonunion firms or other countries. They also argue that union rules about seniority and job duties can reduce flexibility, and that unions benefit their members at the expense of workers who are not covered. Union supporters answer that unions also raise productivity and wages for the whole economy, and that the higher wages are fair. The debate involves economics and values, and careful studies reach mixed results, so this textbook does not decide the question for you.

Key Concepts

Unemployment Insurance

Standard 12.4.1 also lists unemployment insurance: a program that pays temporary benefits to workers who lose their jobs through no fault of their own. Congress created unemployment insurance in the Social Security Act, which President Roosevelt signed on August 14, 1935. It is a joint federal-state program: the federal government sets guidelines and each state runs its own program. In California, the Employment Development Department (EDD) runs it. According to the EDD, unemployment benefits are paid for by employers, who pay a payroll tax on their workers' wages. Workers do not pay for the benefits directly.

To collect benefits in California, a person must be totally or partially unemployed through no fault of his or her own, have earned enough wages in a past period, be physically able and available to work, look for work each week, and be ready to accept work right away. Someone who is fired for serious misconduct or quits without good reason may not qualify. There is a one-week unpaid waiting period before payments begin. The weekly benefit amount ranges from $40 to $450, depending on past earnings. The most that a person can collect in a claim is 26 times the weekly benefit amount, or one-half of the wages earned in the base period, whichever is less, so most claims last up to about six months.

Unemployment insurance has two main purposes. For workers, it replaces part of lost income so that a family can pay rent and buy food while looking for a new job. For the economy, it keeps spending going during a downturn, because the money is spent in local stores. Economists call such a program an automatic stabilizer, which Chapter 19 explains. The benefit does not replace a worker's whole pay. The weekly maximum of $450 is far below a full-time paycheck at the median wage in Figure 9.4, and workers who need more help may turn to other programs.

People disagree about how generous unemployment insurance should be. Supporters say that a stronger program protects families, helps workers search for a good job match instead of taking the first job, and supports spending in a recession. Critics say that benefits may reduce the incentive to look for work quickly, so that people stay unemployed longer than they otherwise would, and that taxes on employers raise the cost of hiring. Economic studies find that benefits do have some effect on the length of unemployment, and they also find that benefits help families, so the question is how to balance the two.

Unemployment insurance does not cover everyone. A worker must have earned enough wages and must have lost the job through no fault of his or her own, and self-employed people and some others are often not covered. A worker whose benefits run out may have to find other support. The rules also change over time. During some recessions, Congress has temporarily raised benefits or extended their length, and states have changed their own rules, so the information in this section describes the rules in the sources named in the figure notes and not necessarily the rules at the time you read it. Always check the current rules at the state's website.

Larry ItliongThe Walkout That Began a Movement

Larry Itliong was born in the Philippines in 1913 and came to the United States in 1929 at the age of fifteen, according to the National Park Service. Like many Filipino men of his generation, he worked in fields and canneries across the West Coast and Alaska. He became active in organizing farm workers. In 1959 he was asked to organize for the Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee (AWOC), a union group chartered by the AFL-CIO, and by 1965 he was one of its leading Filipino organizers.

In September 1965, AWOC members met at the Filipino Community Hall in Delano and voted to strike the grape growers around Delano, in Kern County. On September 8, 1965, they walked off the farms. Sources differ on how many struck, with estimates from about 800 to about 2,000 workers, and on the wage in dispute: the strikers asked for $1.40 an hour, and sources report the existing wage as $1.20 or $1.25. Itliong concluded that only a united strike could pressure the growers and asked the mostly Mexican American National Farm Workers Association to join. The NFWA voted on September 16 to do so. In 1966, the two groups merged, and Itliong served as assistant director of the new union.

Itliong's role illustrates two ideas from this chapter: that workers have more bargaining power when they act together, and that the law shaped who could organize. Because the 1935 National Labor Relations Act excluded farm workers, the strikers had to rely on pressure tactics such as strikes and boycotts rather than the legal protection that most other workers had. GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEUnions Around the World

Workers organize in unions in almost every country, but the rules differ. In some European countries, a large share of workers are covered by union contracts, and unions sit on company boards. CASE STUDY

Delano:
The Strike That Became a Union

In September 1965, farm workers in Delano, in Kern County, walked off the grape fields. The strike lasted five years and led to the first union contracts for a large group of California grape workers. The documents below let you weigh the evidence.

Background

Organizing Where the Law Did Not Reach

In the 1960s, many farm workers in California's Central Valley worked long hours for low pay, and few had any way to bargain with growers. Federal labor law, which had guaranteed most other workers the right to organize since 1935, did not cover agricultural workers. If farm workers wanted a union, they had to organize without the protection of the National Labor Relations Board. The federal minimum wage did not cover most farm workers either until amendments in 1966 took effect in 1967.

Many farm workers in these years had come to the United States under the Bracero Program, which brought Mexican workers to U.S. farms on short-term contracts from 1942 to 1964. Congress ended the program on December 31, 1964, less than nine months before the Delano strike. On September 8, 1965, members of the mostly Filipino Agricultural Workers Organizing Committee walked off the grape farms around Delano. On September 16, members of the mostly Mexican American National Farm Workers Association voted at Our Lady of Guadalupe Church in Delano to join them. The strikers also asked consumers across the country to stop buying table grapes. In 1966, the two groups merged to form the United Farm Workers Organizing Committee.

On July 29, 1970, grape growers began signing labor contracts with the union, ending the strike and boycott after five years. In 1975, California passed the Agricultural Labor Relations Act, which gave farm workers a legal process for union elections. As you read the documents, think about how the labor market, the law, and collective action interacted. Remember that economists can measure outcomes such as wages and membership, but questions about fairness involve values as well. Different sources report some numbers differently, such as how many workers first struck, and Document A notes where they differ.

Chapter 9 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Wages and the Labor Market
The labor market sets wages by demand and supply. Wages differ because of productivity, human capital, scarcity of skills, and the conditions of work. The minimum wage is a price floor that raises pay for workers who keep their jobs, and its effects on employment depend on how high it is. Higher productivity raises the standard of living.
Section 2 · The Changing Labor Force
The civilian labor force is people 16 and over who have or seek jobs. Women's participation rose sharply after 1948 while men's fell, and the overall rate has fallen since 2000 as the population ages. Automation, trade, and outsourcing change the kinds of jobs and the skills workers need, and the movement of workers and capital across borders affects wages, prices, and jobs.
Section 3 · Labor Unions
Unions bargain collectively for workers and use organizing, strikes, boycotts, and lobbying to gain benefits. Key laws are the National Labor Relations Act (1935), the Fair Labor Standards Act (1938), and the Taft-Hartley Act (1947). Disputes may go to mediation, arbitration, or a strike. Union membership has fallen to 10.0% of workers, and unemployment insurance cushions the loss of a job.
