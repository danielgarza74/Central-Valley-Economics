Concept Review

The business cycle is the series of growing and shrinking periods of economic activity, measured by increases and decreases in real gross domestic product (GDP). Chapter 16 showed how economists measure GDP, jobs, and prices. This chapter applies those measures to the problems that appear when the economy slows down, speeds up, or leaves some people behind.

Chapter 17 Key Concept

Unemployment has several different causes, and some unemployment is expected even when the economy is healthy. Poverty is a shortfall of income measured against a standard, and income distribution describes how the nation's income is divided among households. Inflation is a sustained rise in the general level of prices. Economists measure all three with data, but people disagree about their causes and about the best responses.

Why the Concept Matters

Suppose you are a Kern County worker who has driven a forklift at a Shafter packing house for two years. In January the warehouse cuts back for the slow season, and your hours disappear. You spend days applying for jobs, your savings shrink, and you notice that groceries cost more than they did last spring. You may have to choose among rent, a car payment, and a medical bill. Unemployment, low income, and rising prices are not abstractions. They shape what a household can buy this month and what it can plan for next year. You may also be the person who has a steady job but sees a paycheck buy less than it used to. Learning how these problems are measured will help you separate what the numbers say from what people argue about them.

Key Concepts

Measuring Unemployment

In January, a Shafter packing house trims its warehouse crew. A forklift driver who had worked there for two years goes home on a Friday with her last paycheck and a question: how long will it take to find another job? Millions of Americans ask that question every year. Some find the answer in a few weeks. Others take months. To understand how well the whole economy is doing, economists need a way to count the people who want work but cannot find it. Chapter 16 introduced the main indicators. This section looks at unemployment in depth.

Every month, the Census Bureau surveys about 60,000 households for the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS). Interviewers ask each adult whether he or she had a job during the week that included the 12th of the month, whether he or she looked for work during the previous four weeks, and whether he or she was available to start. The answers sort the civilian population age 16 and older into three groups. People who worked for pay are employed. People who did not have a job but actively looked for one and could start work are unemployed. Everyone else, including full-time students, retirees, and people caring for family members, is not in the labor force. The labor force is the number of people who are employed plus the number who are unemployed.

The unemployment rate is the percentage of the labor force that is unemployed. The formula is the number of unemployed people divided by the labor force, multiplied by 100. Suppose a small town has 10,000 people in its labor force, and 9,400 of them have jobs. The other 600 are unemployed, so the unemployment rate is 600 ÷ 10,000 × 100 = 6.0 percent. (This town is hypothetical, written for this textbook.) The BLS reported that the U.S. unemployment rate was 4.1 percent in October 2026, which meant that about 7.1 million people were unemployed. The unemployment rate does not count people who are not in the labor force. A student who is not looking for work is not unemployed, and neither is a retiree.

Economists also track the number of jobs. A second survey, which asks about 120,000 businesses and government agencies how many people are on their payrolls, produces the number of new jobs created each month. According to the BLS, total nonfarm payroll employment rose by 165,000 in October 2026 to about 159.3 million jobs. The two surveys can send different signals in the same month, because they ask different questions of different samples. Together they give a more complete picture of the labor market than either one alone.

The unemployment rate has real limits. It misses some people who are struggling and counts some who are not in serious distress. A discouraged worker is a person who wants a job and is available but has stopped looking because he or she believes that no jobs are available. Because the person is not actively looking, the BLS does not count him or her as unemployed, so the official rate looks better than the job market feels. In the same way, a person who works part time because full-time hours are not available is counted as employed, but may be underemployed. In October 2026, 4.8 million Americans were working part time for economic reasons, and 6.0 million people who were not in the labor force said that they currently wanted a job.

To capture these groups, the BLS publishes several alternative measures. The one that is quoted in news reports is called U-3, and it is the official rate. The broadest, called U-6, adds discouraged workers, other people who want a job but have not searched recently, and people working part time for economic reasons. In October 2026, U-6 was 7.9 percent, compared with the official rate of 4.1 percent. The gap tells you that the headline number leaves out a lot of people who want more work than they have.

There is a second limit. The rate says how many are unemployed, not how long they have been without work. People who have been jobless for 27 weeks or more are the long-term unemployed. In October 2026 there were about 2.0 million of them, or 27.6 percent of all unemployed people. Long spells of unemployment are harder on families than short ones, because savings run out and skills can fade. The BLS uses the labor force participation rate, the percentage of the population age 16 and older that is in the labor force (61.9 percent in October 2026), and the employment-population ratio (59.3 percent) to describe whether people are working, not only whether the unemployed are finding jobs.

Key Concepts

Four Types of Unemployment

Not all unemployment has the same cause, and the cause matters, because different causes call for different responses. Economists sort unemployment into four types. Figure 17.1 summarizes them.

Frictional unemployment is the short-term joblessness of people who are between jobs or just entering the labor market. A worker who quits to look for a better job, a new graduate searching for a first job, and a person returning to work after raising children are all frictionally unemployed. This kind of unemployment is a normal part of a healthy economy. Workers need time to find jobs that fit their skills, and employers need time to find the right workers. Better information, such as online job boards and job fairs, can shorten the search.

Seasonal unemployment results from predictable changes in the demand for workers during the year. Harvest crews, tourism and ski workers, holiday retail employees, and school bus drivers work heavily in some months and little in others. Kern County is a clear example, and you will see the data below. Because the pattern repeats every year, government statisticians publish both the raw numbers, which are called not seasonally adjusted, and a version that removes the usual seasonal swings so that a month can be compared fairly with other months.

Structural unemployment results when the skills that workers have do not match the skills that employers want, or when workers live far from the jobs. Structural unemployment can come from technology, from changes in consumer taste, from international competition, or from a decline in a region's main industry. When a machine replaces a job, or an oil field cuts back, the workers who lose those jobs may need retraining or a move before they can find new work. This kind of unemployment lasts longer than frictional or seasonal unemployment.

Cyclical unemployment is the joblessness that results from a downturn in the business cycle. When total spending in the economy falls, businesses sell less, cut production, and lay off workers. In a recession, cyclical unemployment can rise quickly, as it did in 2008 and 2009 and again in the spring of 2020. When the economy recovers, many of these jobs come back.

People who study labor markets often ask whether an economy ever reaches zero unemployment. It does not, and economists would not want it to. Because some workers will always be between jobs, and some industries will always be changing, a healthy economy always has some frictional and structural unemployment. The level of unemployment that remains when the economy is producing at its capacity is sometimes called full employment. At full employment, the only unemployment is frictional, seasonal, and structural; cyclical unemployment is close to zero. Economists estimate that full employment corresponds to an unemployment rate somewhere in the range of about 4 to 5 percent. They disagree about the exact figure, and it changes over time as the workforce, technology, and the economy change.

Kern County's seasonal swings help to explain why it often reports higher unemployment than the rest of California. In the Bakersfield metropolitan area, the BLS reported that the unemployment rate was 7.9 percent in September 2026 (preliminary, not seasonally adjusted), compared with 5.0 percent for California in October 2026 (seasonally adjusted). The two numbers are not perfectly comparable, since one is adjusted for the calendar and the other is not, but the gap persists in nearly every month of nearly every year. The reason is not only the calendar. The county's economy depends on agriculture, oil and gas, and related industries, which have many seasonal and structural features.

That does not mean that Kern County is in a permanent slump. Its job totals, by BLS estimates, were about 307,000 nonfarm jobs in September 2026, with education and health services adding jobs and several other categories holding steady or shrinking. It does mean that a student who plans to work in the county should expect cycles of busy and slow periods, and should understand that skills that apply in more than one industry make it easier to shift when the calendar or the economy changes.

Key Concepts

The Business Cycle and Jobs

The economy does not grow in a straight line. Over many decades, the output of goods and services in the United States has tended to rise, but it rises faster in some years than in others and sometimes falls. The business cycle is the series of expansions and contractions in economic activity, usually measured by real gross domestic product (GDP). Figure 17.3 shows a simple, hypothetical business cycle. In an expansion, output and employment rise. The expansion ends at a peak, the high point of activity. In a contraction, output and employment fall. The contraction ends at a trough, the low point, and recovery begins.

A contraction that is large enough and long enough is called a recession. Many people have heard that a recession means two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. That is a rule of thumb, not the official definition. In the United States, the dates of recessions are announced by a private research group, the Business Cycle Dating Committee of the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER). The committee defines a recession as a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months. It looks at real GDP, but also at jobs, income, and sales.

Recessions and unemployment move together, but not at the same moment. Businesses often wait to lay off workers until they are sure that sales will stay low, and they wait to hire back until they are sure that sales will stay high. As a result, the unemployment rate usually keeps rising for some time after a recession starts, and it can keep falling slowly for years after a recession ends. Figure 17.4 shows the annual average unemployment rate since 1948, with the recession years shaded. In nearly every shaded period, the rate rises, and in the longer expansions it falls.

Who Is Unemployed, and What Does It Cost?

Unemployment does not fall evenly across the population. Figure 17.7 shows that unemployment rates differ widely among groups. In October 2026, the rate was 13.9 percent for teenagers, who are new to the labor market and often have little experience, compared with 3.9 percent for adult men and 3.6 percent for adult women. By race and ethnicity, the BLS reported rates of 6.6 percent for Black workers, 4.7 percent for Hispanic workers, 3.7 percent for White workers, and 2.8 percent for Asian workers. Economists point to several things that can lie behind gaps like these, including differences in age and experience, in education, in the industries and regions where people work, and, in some cases, discrimination by employers. They disagree about how much weight each factor deserves, and a single month's numbers cannot settle that question.

Education shows a clearer pattern, which Figure 17.8 shows. Among adults age 25 and over in October 2026, the unemployment rate for people with less than a high school diploma was 5.8 percent, while the rate for people with a bachelor's degree or higher was 2.6 percent. A diploma or degree does not guarantee a job, but on average it lowers the risk of being without one. This is part of the reason that education shows up so often in discussions of wage and income differences, which Section 2 takes up.

Another way to look at unemployment is to ask why people are out of work. Figure 17.6 shows the BLS breakdown for October 2026. The largest group was job losers and people whose temporary jobs had ended, about 3.2 million. A second large group, about 2.3 million, was reentrants: people who had worked before, left the labor force for a time, and were now looking again. Smaller groups were new entrants, who had never worked, and job leavers, who quit voluntarily. The mix of reasons changes through the business cycle. In a recession, job losers become a much larger share of the unemployed.

Unemployment has costs for the people who lose jobs and for everyone else. For individuals, the biggest cost is lost income, which can lead to missed payments on rent or loans, drawn-down savings, and stress. A long spell of joblessness can also reduce a worker's skills and make it harder to find a job at the old wage. For the economy as a whole, unemployed workers are labor that is not producing goods and services. Output that is lost cannot be recovered. A downturn also reduces the taxes that governments collect while it increases the amount they spend on assistance. Unemployed workers buy less, which reduces sales for other businesses and can cause more layoffs.

The United States and California respond to some of these costs through unemployment insurance (UI), a program that pays temporary benefits to workers who lose a job through no fault of their own. UI was created by the Social Security Act of 1935 and is run by the states under federal rules. In California, the Employment Development Department (EDD) pays the benefits, and the cost is paid by employers. According to the EDD, weekly benefits range from $40 to a maximum of $450, and the total a person can receive is limited to the smaller of 26 times the weekly amount or one half of the wages earned in the base period. Workers must be able and available to work and must look for jobs. (Benefit rules change, so check the EDD for current details.)

Supporters of unemployment insurance argue that it helps families keep paying rent and buying groceries during a spell of joblessness, which also supports local businesses, and that it gives workers time to find a good job match rather than taking the first offer. Critics argue that benefits that are too generous or last too long can lessen the incentive to search, and that the employer-paid taxes can raise the cost of hiring. Economists who have studied the question find that benefits can lengthen the time that some people spend unemployed, but also that they allow some workers to find better matches. The size of each effect remains a subject of debate, and it can differ between a recession and an expansion.

Key Concepts

Defining and Measuring Poverty

Imagine two families in Shafter. Each has four members, and each earns about the same amount of money. One family owns its home and has no rent. The other pays a large share of its income to a landlord, and a medical emergency uses up what was left. Are both families poor? Is neither? Economists and governments need a clear rule to count the people who do not have enough money to meet their basic needs, because a rule makes it possible to compare years, regions, and groups, and to decide who qualifies for help. No rule is perfect, and people disagree about how to draw the line.

In the United States, the official measure is the poverty line, also called the poverty threshold: a dollar amount that depends on the size and makeup of a family. A family whose pre-tax money income is below its threshold is counted as poor. The poverty rate is the percentage of people whose family income is below the line. In 2025, the Census Bureau reported that the official poverty rate was 10.2 percent, which meant that 34.5 million people were living below the line. That was 0.5 percentage points lower than in 2024. The weighted average threshold for a family of four was $32,970 in 2025, and the threshold for a family of two adults and two children was $32,649. (The Census Bureau's thresholds are updated each year using the Consumer Price Index.)

Poverty is not spread evenly among age groups. In 2025, the official poverty rate for children under 18 was 13.4 percent, a historic low according to the Census Bureau, and the rate for Hispanic people of any race was 13.9 percent, also a historic low. The same data show that the share of people in poverty has fallen over the long run. In 1959, the first year of the official series, the rate was 22.4 percent, more than twice today's rate. It fell to 11.1 percent in 1973 and has stayed between about 10 and 16 percent ever since (Census Bureau historical series, as compiled by the Economic Policy Institute, a left-leaning research group).

The official measure has critics from several directions. Some argue that it counts too few people as poor, because it was built to measure a minimum diet and was set using spending patterns from the middle of the twentieth century. Others argue that it counts too many, because it counts only cash income before taxes and ignores benefits such as food assistance or housing subsidies that raise a family's living standard. Still others note that the same dollar threshold is used in every part of the country, even though housing in Los Angeles costs much more than housing in rural Kansas.

Responding to these concerns, the Census Bureau and the BLS publish a second measure, the Supplemental Poverty Measure (SPM). The SPM counts income after taxes and counts the value of non-cash benefits such as the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), school meals, and housing help. It then subtracts necessary expenses such as taxes, child care, work expenses, and medical costs, and it adjusts the thresholds for local housing costs. In 2025, the SPM rate was 13.1 percent, which was not statistically different from the 2024 rate. It is higher than the official rate, mainly because the SPM counts rising costs of living and subtracts necessary expenses, and it is useful for showing the effect of government programs. For example, the Census Bureau estimates that Social Security moved 28.8 million people out of SPM poverty in 2025, the largest effect of any program.

Which measure is better? Neither is perfect. The official measure has the advantage of a long and consistent record that goes back to 1959, so that it can show trends. The SPM is more complete, but its definitions have been revised and it began only in 2011. Many economists use both.

Mollie OrshanskyThe economist who drew the poverty line

Before the 1960s, the government had no single, official count of how many Americans were poor. Mollie Orshansky, an economist at the Social Security Administration, set out to make one. She had studied mathematics and statistics at Hunter College, started her federal career in 1936 at the Children's Bureau, and joined the Social Security Administration in 1958, where she studied family income and whether families had enough to live on.

In 1963 and 1964, she created a set of poverty thresholds based on the cost of a minimum food diet, which she calculated for different sizes and kinds of families. According to the Census Bureau, the original thresholds multiplied the cost of the diet by three "to account for other family expenses." Because the thresholds were simple, consistent, and based on data, the government adopted them as the basis for its official poverty statistics, and the Office of Management and Budget later made them the official measure for government statistics. Colleagues called her "Miss Poverty." She retired from the Social Security Administration in 1982.

Her method is still debated. Critics say that food now takes a much smaller share of a family's budget than in the 1950s, so the multiplier no longer fits, and that the line ignores taxes and benefits. Defenders say that a stable measure is valuable because it allows comparison across decades. Either way, the line that she drew still decides how the government counts poverty.

Poverty in Kern County and California

National numbers hide large differences from one place to another. Poverty rates differ by state, by county, and even by neighborhood. The Census Bureau's Small Area Income and Poverty Estimates (SAIPE) combine survey data and administrative records to estimate poverty for every county each year. According to SAIPE, an estimated 18.6 percent of people of all ages in Kern County lived in poverty in 2024, compared with 11.8 percent for California and 12.1 percent for the United States. (SAIPE rates are model-based estimates of the official measure for people of all ages from the American Community Survey, so they are not the same as the Current Population Survey rate of 10.6 percent in 2024.)

Kern County also has a lower typical income than the state or nation. The SAIPE estimate of median household income for 2024 was $71,295 in Kern County, compared with $100,130 for California and $81,604 for the United States. The Census Bureau's QuickFacts shows a similar picture: for 2020 to 2024, median household income in Kern County was $70,210 in 2024 dollars, per capita income was $29,699, and 18.8 percent of adults age 25 and over had a bachelor's degree or higher. Key Concepts

Causes of Poverty and Ways to Respond

People in poverty are not one group. Some are children, whose families' incomes are low, some are older adults living on a small fixed income, some are workers whose jobs do not pay enough to keep a family above the line, and some are people who cannot find work or cannot work because of illness or disability. Economists identify several factors that are associated with poverty. These include low wages, part-time or irregular work, unemployment, limited education or training, illness or disability, family circumstances such as a single parent with young children, living in a region where few jobs exist, and discrimination in hiring, housing, or lending. People disagree about how large each factor is, and the factors reinforce one another.

People also disagree about what to do. One group emphasizes economic growth and work. They argue that the best antipoverty tool is a job, and that policy should make work pay and make it easier for employers to hire, for instance by keeping taxes and regulations low, and by supporting education and training. Another group emphasizes direct help. They argue that low-wage jobs are not enough for families to meet basic needs, and that programs such as SNAP, health coverage through Medicaid (called Medi-Cal in California), and tax credits are needed to reduce hardship, particularly for children. Many economists take a middle position: work and assistance can support each other, and the question is how to design programs so that people are encouraged to work and are not left without help. Policy research groups differ in their emphasis. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities, a left-leaning group, often stresses the antipoverty effects of benefit programs. The American Enterprise Institute, a right-leaning think tank, often stresses work, family stability, and the risk that benefits can reduce the incentive to work. Both groups cite data, and a careful reader should check each group's evidence and assumptions.

Several tools are widely used. Government payments to people who do not give a good or service in return, such as Social Security or SNAP benefits, are called transfer payments, and the programs as a group are often called the safety net. A minimum wage sets the lowest hourly pay that employers may legally offer. The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since 2009, and California's was $16.90 an hour in 2026. Supporters say that a higher minimum raises the earnings of the lowest-paid workers. Critics say that it can reduce hiring of some workers, particularly where wages are low and employers have thin profits. Studies reach different conclusions depending on the size of the increase and the local economy. The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) is a federal tax credit for working people with low or moderate earnings, and California has its own credit. SNAP helps low-income households buy food. Social Security provides retirement and disability benefits and is the single largest program in reducing measured poverty. Education and training are a longer-term tool, since more education tends to bring higher earnings and lower unemployment.

How Income Is Distributed

Poverty is about the bottom of the income scale. Income distribution describes how the income of the whole nation is divided among households. Economists often sort households from lowest income to highest and divide them into five equal groups called quintiles. Figure 17.13 shows the share of the nation's money income that each group received in 2025. The lowest fifth of households received 3.0 percent of all income. The highest fifth received 52.4 percent, which is about 17 times as much (52.4 ÷ 3.0 ≈ 17.5). The top 5 percent of households alone received 23.5 percent. These figures describe money income before taxes and do not count benefits such as food assistance or health coverage.

A Lorenz curve shows the same information in another way. It plots the cumulative share of income on the vertical axis against the cumulative share of households on the horizontal axis. If income were divided equally, the curve would be a straight diagonal line (the line of perfect equality), since the lowest 20 percent of households would receive 20 percent of income, the lowest 40 percent would receive 40 percent, and so on. In Figure 17.14, the actual curve for 2025 bows below the line. The more it bows, the more unequal the distribution.

The Gini index summarizes the bow of the Lorenz curve in one number between 0 and 1. A Gini index of 0 means that everyone has exactly the same income, and an index of 1 means that one household receives all the income. The Census Bureau reported that the Gini index of money income was 0.490 in 2025, which was not significantly different from 2024. It is also a useful way to compare countries, with the caution that different countries measure income differently. The Census Bureau also reported that the household at the 90th percentile had an income of $261,300, while the household at the 10th percentile had $20,010, a ratio of about 13 to 1.

Income and wealth are not the same thing. Income is a flow of money that a household receives over a period, such as wages, interest, and benefits. Wealth is the value of what a household owns minus what it owes, such as a home, savings, and investments, minus debts. Wealth is generally distributed more unequally than income.

Key Concepts

Why Wages Differ: Human Capital and Productivity

People often ask why a surgeon is paid more than a cashier. The laws of supply and demand give part of the answer. A wage is a price, the price of labor. Where the demand for a type of work is strong and the supply of workers who can do it is small, the wage tends to be high. A surgeon's skills are scarce, because the training takes many years and few people complete it, and the demand for surgeons is high because patients value the service. Cashiers, by contrast, need fewer specialized skills, so more people can do the work, and wages tend to be lower.

Economists use the term human capital to describe the knowledge, training, and skills that people acquire through education and experience. Like a factory or a truck, human capital is an investment that can raise a worker's productivity, the output that a worker produces in an hour. Employers will pay more for workers who produce more, so wages tend to rise with productivity. This is why education and experience are linked to higher earnings. Other factors affect wages too, including the risk or difficulty of a job, the cost of the training required, and, in some cases, labor unions or government rules such as the minimum wage. Wages can also be affected by discrimination, which is illegal but still a concern.

Another source of income differences is income mobility, the ability of people to move from one place in the income distribution to another over time or across generations. Economists who study mobility find that many Americans move up and down the income ladder over a career. They also find that children's chances of moving up differ by place and by the income of their parents. Whether mobility is high enough is another question on which people disagree. Some stress that mobility shows that today's low earners are not stuck, while others stress that opportunity is not equal for all children.

Key Concepts

What Is Inflation, and How Is It Measured?

A few years ago, a taquería in Bakersfield charged $4.50 for a plate of three tacos. Today, the same plate costs more. The tortillas, the meat, the cooking oil, the rent, and the wages of the people behind the counter all cost the owner more than they once did, and she passes some of that increase on to customers. No single price tells you how fast prices are rising for everyone. To find that out, economists look at the average of many prices over time.

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general level of prices. It does not mean that a single price rises, or that prices rise for a few weeks. When economists say that inflation is 3 percent, they mean that the average price of the goods and services that people buy is 3 percent higher than it was a year earlier. The opposite is deflation, a sustained decrease in the general level of prices. The word disinflation describes a slowdown in the rate of inflation: prices are still rising, but more slowly than before.

The most widely used measure of inflation in the United States is the Consumer Price Index (CPI). The BLS calculates the CPI by pricing a large "market basket" of goods and services that typical urban households buy, such as food, housing, transportation, medical care, clothing, and recreation. Interviewers and data collectors check about 80,000 prices each month. The BLS compares the total cost of the basket in a given month with its cost in a base period. For the CPI for All Urban Consumers (CPI-U), the base is the average of 1982 to 1984, which is set equal to 100. The CPI-U was 336.94 in September 2026, which means that the basket that cost $100 in the base period cost about $337 then. Chapter 16 explained how the CPI is built.

To find the inflation rate between two dates, divide the change in the index by the starting index, and multiply by 100. The CPI-U was 324.8 in September 2025 and 336.94 in September 2026. The change was 336.94 − 324.8 = 12.14 points, and 12.14 ÷ 324.8 × 100 = about 3.7 percent. That is the 12-month inflation rate for September 2026, which means that prices were rising at about 3.7 percent a year. (The BLS could not publish 12-month changes for October 2026, because it did not collect October 2025 prices during the federal government shutdown.)

Key Concepts

Nominal and Real Values

A raise is good news only if it is larger than inflation. Economists therefore distinguish between nominal and real values. A nominal value is a value measured in current dollars, with no adjustment for changes in prices. A real value has been adjusted for inflation, so that it shows what the money can actually buy. Real values are measured in constant dollars of a chosen base year.

Suppose a Kern County worker earned $20 an hour in January 2020 and earns $24 an hour in January 2026. The worker's nominal wage rose by 20 percent, from $20 to $24. During the same period, the CPI-U rose from 257.971 to 325.252, an increase of about 26.1 percent, because 325.252 ÷ 257.971 ≈ 1.261. To find the real wage, convert the $24 into January 2020 dollars: $24 × (257.971 ÷ 325.252) ≈ $19.04. In terms of purchasing power, the worker's raise was really a cut of about 4.8 percent, because $19.04 is less than $20. (The worker in this example is hypothetical, written for this textbook.) The reverse is also possible: if wages rise faster than prices, real wages rise and workers can afford more.

The same logic applies to interest rates. A nominal interest rate is the rate that a lender or a bank advertises. The real interest rate is approximately the nominal rate minus the rate of inflation. If a savings account pays 4 percent and inflation is 3 percent, the real return is about 1 percent. If inflation is 5 percent, the real return is about −1 percent, and the saver's money buys less over time even though the balance grew. Chapter 14 introduced short-term and long-term interest rates, and Chapter 20 explains how the Federal Reserve uses them to affect inflation.

What Causes Inflation?

Economists group the causes of inflation into three broad categories. They often work together, and in a real episode it can be difficult to say which one mattered most.

Demand-pull inflation happens when total spending in the economy grows faster than the economy's ability to produce goods and services. The common description is "too much money chasing too few goods." If households, businesses, and governments try to buy more than the economy can produce, sellers can raise prices without losing customers. Demand can rise because of tax cuts, higher government spending, easy credit, or a surge of exports. Economists who emphasize the money supply argue that when the money supply grows faster than output for a long time, the result is higher prices, which is the basic logic of the quantity theory of money.

Cost-push inflation happens when the costs of producing goods rise, so that firms raise prices to cover them even if demand is no stronger. The cost of energy is a common cause, since fuel is used to produce and move nearly everything. Other causes are poor harvests, disruptions to shipping, higher wages not matched by higher productivity, and new taxes or rules. A supply shock is a sudden event that raises costs or limits supply, such as a war that interrupts oil shipments or a pandemic that closes factories. Because a supply shock reduces output while raising prices, it can cause stagflation, a combination of high inflation and high unemployment that you will study in the Case Study.

A third cause is expectations. If workers and businesses expect prices to rise, they act in ways that make it happen. Workers ask for higher wages, and firms raise prices to cover the higher wages. The result is a wage-price spiral: higher wages raise costs, higher costs raise prices, and higher prices lead to demands for still higher wages. Once people expect inflation, it becomes harder to reduce, which is one reason that policymakers try to keep expectations steady.

Key Concepts

Who Gains and Who Loses?

Inflation does not hurt everyone equally. When prices rise faster than incomes, people on fixed incomes lose purchasing power. A retiree who receives a pension of a fixed dollar amount can buy less every year. Savers who hold cash or accounts with low interest rates also lose, because the real value of their money shrinks. Lenders lose if they are repaid in dollars that buy less than the dollars that they lent. Workers whose wages do not rise as fast as prices lose too, as the worker in the earlier example did.

Others gain. Borrowers who owe a fixed amount repay it in dollars that are worth less, which helps a family with a fixed-rate mortgage. People who own assets whose value rises with inflation, such as real estate, can gain, and businesses whose prices rise faster than their costs can see higher profits. Governments that owe money also benefit, because they repay in cheaper dollars. These gains and losses are a redistribution that no one voted for, which is one reason that inflation is a political issue.

Inflation also has costs that fall on everyone. People spend time and effort trying to protect themselves, which economists sometimes call "shoe-leather costs": making extra trips to the bank, shopping for lower prices, or moving cash into assets that hold their value. Businesses face "menu costs": the expense of changing prices on menus, catalogs, and shelves. When prices are rising unpredictably, businesses and households find it harder to plan, sign long-term contracts, or make investments, and the economy can become less efficient. To protect themselves, some contracts include a cost-of-living adjustment (COLA), a provision that raises a payment automatically when the CPI rises. Social Security benefits include a COLA, and so do some labor contracts.

In the extreme case, inflation can run out of control. The word hyperinflation describes inflation that is extremely high and accelerating, often defined as more than 50 percent a month. In hyperinflation, money loses its value so quickly that people stop accepting it, and the three functions of money that you studied in Chapter 14 begin to fail. Hyperinflation has usually come when a government finances its spending by printing money.

Key Concepts

How Do Policymakers Respond to Inflation?

Because inflation harms some people and creates uncertainty for everyone, governments have long tried to control it. The goal of price stability means a low and predictable inflation rate. In the United States, the Federal Reserve has the primary responsibility, and it has set a long-run goal of 2 percent inflation, as measured by a different price index that you will meet in Chapter 20. The tools differ depending on the cause.

Monetary policy is the use of interest rates and the money supply to affect the economy. When inflation is too high, the Federal Reserve can raise interest rates. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, so families and businesses borrow and spend less, and total demand slows. The risk is that if the Fed slows demand too much, the result may be a recession and higher unemployment. Chapter 20 explains how this works. The relevant state standard, 12.3.4, asks students to understand the aims and tools of monetary policy and their influence on economic activity, and this section previews that idea.

Fiscal policy is the use of taxes, government borrowing, and spending to influence the economy. When demand is too strong, a government could reduce spending or raise taxes to slow it, although doing so is politically difficult. Chapter 19 develops fiscal policy and the state standard, 12.3.3, which asks students to describe the aims of fiscal policies and their influence on production, employment, and price levels. Other approaches focus on supply. Policies that expand production, such as investment in energy supply, shipping capacity, or workforce skills, can ease cost pressures. Some governments have also tried direct controls on wages and prices, with mixed results. The Case Study examines one such effort in the United States.

These options involve trade-offs. A policy that reduces inflation quickly can raise unemployment for a time, and a policy that avoids unemployment may allow inflation to persist. Economists and citizens differ about how to balance these goals, and the right balance depends on how much each problem is hurting people.

CASE STUDY

The Effects of Inflation
in the 1970s

In the 1970s, prices in the United States rose faster than at any other time since the 1940s, and unemployment rose too. Families, farmers, and businesses had to decide how to protect what they earned. This case study asks what caused the inflation, how it was experienced, and what it took to bring it down.

Background

The Great Inflation

Economists often use the term "Great Inflation" for the years from about 1965 to 1982. According to the Federal Reserve's history, inflation in 1964 was a little more than 1 percent a year and unemployment was about 5 percent. By 1980, inflation was above 13 percent by the annual-average CPI measure used in Figure 17.17 (the Federal Reserve's history says that it reached more than 14 percent that year on a year-over-year basis). Prices rose in every year of the decade, and the rise was not a one-time event.

Many causes came together. Government spending rose in the late 1960s, while the economy was already running strong. In the summer of 1971, the Nixon administration introduced wage and price controls, which were extended in three phases between 1971 and 1974. In October 1973, an Arab oil embargo began and lasted about five months, and the Fed's history says that crude oil prices quadrupled during that period. In 1979, the Iranian revolution set off a second energy crisis that tripled the cost of oil. Economists still debate how much each factor mattered, and how much blame belongs to government spending, to the Federal Reserve's money policy, to oil prices, and to expectations.

The 1970s also brought something that the economics textbooks of the time had not planned for: stagflation, the combination of high inflation and high unemployment. Earlier economists had believed that policymakers could trade one for the other. In the 1970s, the economy suffered both at once, and the recession of 1973 to 1975 was the longest to that point since the 1930s (16 months, NBER). The 1970s ended with a change in policy. Paul Volcker became Chairman of the Federal Reserve in August 1979, and in October 1979, the Fed announced that it would target the growth of bank reserves rather than the federal funds rate. Interest rates rose sharply. Two recessions followed, one from January to July 1980 and another from July 1981 to November 1982. Inflation fell, but unemployment reached nearly 11 percent.

Chapter 17 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Unemployment in Today's Economy
Unemployment is measured by the unemployment rate, the share of the labor force that is jobless and looking for work, and other measures such as U-6 capture discouraged and underemployed workers. Unemployment can be frictional, seasonal, structural, or cyclical, and some is normal at full employment. The business cycle moves through expansions and contractions, and recessions raise unemployment. Unemployment insurance eases the cost for workers who lose jobs.
Section 2 · Poverty and Income Distribution
The official poverty line compares family money income with a threshold set in the 1960s and updated for inflation, and the Supplemental Poverty Measure adds taxes, benefits, and costs. Poverty rates are higher in Kern County than in California or the nation. Income is unequally distributed, as quintiles, the Lorenz curve, and the Gini index show. Wage differences reflect supply, demand, and human capital, and people disagree about the best antipoverty tools.
Section 3 · Causes and Consequences of Inflation
Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level, measured with the CPI. Real values adjust nominal values for inflation. Causes include demand-pull, cost-push, and expectations, and inflation redistributes income between savers and borrowers and creates uncertainty. Policymakers respond with monetary policy, fiscal policy, supply-side measures, and in the past, price controls, each with trade-offs between inflation and unemployment.
