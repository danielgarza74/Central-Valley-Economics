Unit Overview

The Big Picture

Every family has to answer two questions: how will we pay for what we need, and what will we do if the money runs short? A government answers the same two questions on a vastly larger scale. Unit 5 follows that story across three chapters. Chapter 18 asks where government money comes from and where it goes. Chapter 19 asks whether taxing, spending, and borrowing can be used on purpose to steady the economy, and what the bill looks like afterward. Chapter 20 turns to a different institution altogether, the Federal Reserve, which does not tax or write a budget but still has great influence over the price of borrowing money. Together, the three chapters explain how government shapes the economy you will work in, pay taxes in, and borrow money in.

Start with the money. In Chapter 18 a part-time worker at a Shafter taquería opens a first paycheck and finds that Social Security and Medicare taxes have already been subtracted. You will learn how the federal government raised about $5.4 trillion in fiscal year 2026, with income taxes supplying about 53 percent and payroll taxes about 34 percent, and how it spent about $7.4 trillion, leaving a gap of nearly $2 trillion. You will compare tax types (progressive, regressive, and proportional) and learn why the person who writes the check to the government is not always the person who bears the burden. The same chapter brings the topic home: in spring and summer 2026 the sales tax rate was 7.25 percent in Shafter, 8.25 percent in Bakersfield, and 9.25 percent in McFarland, because cities and voters add their own taxes to the statewide base.

Chapter 19 puts the pieces to work. Economies swing through booms and recessions, and in a recession families in Kern County feel it directly: in August 2026 the county's unemployment rate was 8.4 percent, compared with 5.4 percent for California. Fiscal policy is the deliberate use of taxes and government spending to push the economy back toward steady growth. Economists disagree about how to do it. One tradition focuses on total demand and the spending multiplier, in which each dollar the government spends becomes income for someone who spends part of it again. Another focuses on supply and on incentives to work, save, and invest. Both approaches can require borrowing, and borrowing adds up. By November 2026 total public debt had passed $40 trillion, and the interest alone cost the federal government about $1.14 trillion in fiscal year 2026, more than it spent on the military.

Chapter 20 introduces the Fed, the nation's central bank. Congress gave it a dual mandate, to promote maximum employment and stable prices, and its main tool is a target for the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge one another for overnight loans. After its September 17, 2026, meeting, the Fed's committee set that target range at 3.75 to 4.00 percent and held it there on October 28. Those decisions ripple out to home loans, car loans, and the financing a farmer near Wasco needs for new equipment. Fiscal policy moves slowly because it must pass through Congress; monetary policy can change in a single meeting. Chapter 20 ends by asking what happens when the two work together, and when they pull in opposite directions.

COUNTER-ARGUMENTShould the government spend to fight a recession?
The case for acting
When families and businesses all cut back at once, total spending falls and jobs disappear. Supporters argue that government is the one buyer that can step in, that unemployment benefits and public projects put money into the hands of people who will spend it, and that waiting for the economy to heal itself can cost millions of people years of lost work.
The Counter-Argument
Critics answer that government spending must be paid for by taxes or borrowing, which can discourage private spending and investment. They point out that laws take time to pass, so help can arrive after the recession is over, and that lower taxes and fewer rules may do more to encourage hiring than new programs.

As you read Chapter 19, decide which kind of evidence would best settle this disagreement.

COUNTER-ARGUMENTHow worried should we be about the national debt?
The case for concern
Interest payments now take roughly one of every five federal revenue dollars, leaving less for schools, roads, or tax relief. Supporters of this view warn that high debt can push interest rates up, crowd out private investment, and leave less room to respond to the next emergency.
The Counter-Argument
Others reply that a country that borrows in its own currency and has a large, growing economy can carry a large debt, that borrowing paid for useful investment can be worth its cost, and that cutting spending or raising taxes too quickly could itself harm the economy.

The Chapter 19 Case Study, Is the Federal Deficit Too Large?, asks you to weigh the data for yourself.

BIG IDEAS

  • EVERY TAX AND EVERY PROGRAM HAS A COSTGovernments pay for public goods and services with revenue, and citizens must weigh both benefits and costs (Ch. 18).
  • FISCAL POLICY STEERS WITH BUDGETSTaxes, spending, and borrowing can be used to fight recessions or inflation, but they work with delays and side effects (Ch. 19).
  • MONETARY POLICY STEERS WITH CREDITThe Fed influences interest rates and the money supply to pursue stable prices and high employment (Ch. 20).
  • REASONABLE PEOPLE DISAGREE, SO USE EVIDENCEEconomists debate the size of multipliers, the effect of tax cuts, and the danger of debt. Data and Counter-Arguments help settle what can be settled.

WHY THIS UNIT MATTERS

A hypothetical week for Marisol, a senior at Shafter High School.

Monday: Her first taquería paycheck is smaller than she expected, and the stub lists taxes she cannot yet explain.

Wednesday: The news says Congress is arguing over how much to borrow, while her uncle, who grows almonds near Wasco, wonders whether the interest rate on his equipment loan will rise.

Friday: She hears that the Fed met this week and left rates unchanged. Her cousin wants to buy a used truck and asks whether to wait.

By the end of this unit, you will be able to explain each of these three moments and decide for yourself what policy would be best.

Skills You Will Practice

Tools for Reading Government Data

Reading budget dataCompare federal revenue, spending, and the deficit for two fiscal years, and read pie and bar charts of where the money comes from and goes.Ch. 18, Figures 18.5, 18.7, 18.9
Computing tax by bracketApply each rate only to the dollars in its bracket: ($12,400 × 0.10) + ($23,500 × 0.12) = $4,060 on $35,900 of taxable income.Ch. 18, Section 2 Math Handbook
Comparing tax burdensUse a tax rate (tax ÷ amount taxed), burden shares, and shares of income versus shares of tax paid to judge who bears a tax.Ch. 18, Sections 1–2
Benefit-cost thinkingDivide total benefits by total costs and decide when the costs of a government action outweigh its benefits.Ch. 18, Math Handbook; Case Study
Growth rates and output gapsCalculate (new − old) ÷ old × 100 and the output gap, (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100.Ch. 19, Section 1, Figure 19.2
Multiplier arithmeticUse multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC). With MPC = 0.75 the multiplier is 4, so $10 million of spending can raise total spending by up to $40 million.Ch. 19, Section 2, Figure 19.6
Testing the Laffer curveCompute revenue = rate × income at different tax rates to see why a tax cut can lose or raise revenue.Ch. 19, Section 2, Figure 19.8
Debt, interest, and ratiosCompute debt-to-GDP, return on a Treasury bill ($200 ÷ $9,800 ≈ 2.04%), and read net interest as a share of revenue and GDP.Ch. 19, Section 3, Figures 19.12–19.14
Reading Fed policy decisionsRead a target range, a vote count, and statement wording; convert basis points to percentage points; compare the federal funds rate with inflation and unemployment.Ch. 20, Section 3, Figures 20.10, 20.16, Case Study
Deposit expansionUse the deposit multiplier, 1 ÷ required reserve ratio, to trace how $1,000 of new reserves can become $10,000 of deposits at a 10 percent ratio.Ch. 20, Section 2, Figure 20.6
Short-term and long-term ratesCompare Treasury yields from 3 months to 30 years and explain why the two ends of the range can differ.Ch. 19, Section 3; Ch. 20, Section 3
Weighing Counter-ArgumentsRead a claim, state the strongest Counter-Argument, and point to evidence that supports each side.All Case Studies

LOOK BACK · UNIT 4, MONEY AND THE MACROECONOMY

  • Chapter 14, Money and Banking: money serves as a medium of exchange, unit of account, and store of value, and banks create much of the money supply when they make loans. Chapter 20 shows how the Fed influences that process.
  • Chapter 16, Economic Indicators and Measurements: real GDP, unemployment, and inflation tell policymakers when to act. Chapters 19 and 20 use these same measures as warning lights.
  • Chapter 17, Facing Economic Challenges: unemployment, inflation, and poverty are the problems that fiscal and monetary policy try to ease.
  • Chapter 15, Financial Markets: savers and borrowers meet in markets, where the government also borrows by selling Treasury securities.

LOOK AHEAD · UNIT 6, THE GLOBAL ECONOMY

  • Chapter 21, International Trade: tariffs raise revenue (about 3 percent of federal revenue in fiscal year 2026) but are also a tool of trade policy. Exchange rates and trade are also introduced here.
  • Foreign lenders: Chapter 19 shows that Japan, the United Kingdom, and China are among the largest foreign holders of U.S. Treasury securities. Government borrowing connects the United States to the world.
  • Chapter 22, Issues of Economic Development: many countries face the same questions about taxes, debt, and central banks, often with fewer resources.
