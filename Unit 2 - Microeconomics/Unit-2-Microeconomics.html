Unit Overview

How Markets Work

Every day, millions of people in Kern County make choices that no one coordinates. A farm worker in Shafter decides whether to pick up another shift. A family in Bakersfield decides whether to buy beef or chicken this week. A grower decides whether the price of pistachios is high enough to plant more trees. No central office tells any of them what to do, yet grocery shelves get stocked, jobs get filled, and prices rise and fall on their own. Unit 1 introduced scarcity and the idea that every choice has a cost. Unit 2, Microeconomics, shows how millions of separate choices add up in markets. Microeconomics is the study of the economic choices made by individuals, households, and businesses in particular markets.

The unit begins with the two sides of every market. In Chapter 4: Demand you stand in the buyer's shoes. You will learn why buyers purchase less when a price rises, what causes the whole demand curve to shift, and how elasticity measures how strongly buyers respond. In Chapter 5: Supply you move to the seller's side. You will study the costs of production that sit behind every supply curve, what causes supply to change, and why a producer can react quickly in some markets and slowly in others. Kern County is a good laboratory for this chapter: its pistachios were the county's top crop in 2025, worth about $1.39 billion at the farm gate, and a tree crop cannot be expanded or cut back overnight.

Chapter 6: Demand, Supply, and Prices is the hinge of the unit. It puts the two curves on one graph and shows where they meet. At the equilibrium price, the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer. Above that price there is a surplus, below it there is a shortage, and a shift in either curve moves the market to a new price. You will also see that prices are more than numbers: they are signals that tell producers what to make and give them an incentive to make it. Then you will ask what happens when a government overrides the signal with a price ceiling or a price floor. The federal minimum wage has been $7.25 an hour since July 24, 2009, while California's rose to $16.90 on January 1, 2026. A minimum wage is a price floor, and it is one of the most debated prices in the country.

The last three chapters widen the lens. Chapter 7: Market Structures asks how the number of sellers changes everything. A Kern County farm is a tiny price taker among thousands, while an electric utility or a large grocery chain has much more power over price. Chapter 8: Types of Business Organizations looks inside the seller: whether a business is a sole proprietorship, a partnership, a corporation, or a cooperative decides who owns it, who is responsible for its debts, and who pays which taxes. Chapter 9: The Role of Labor turns to the people who do the work. Wages are the price in the labor market, so the same demand and supply tools explain why an electrician earns more than a cashier and why workers form unions. Read the unit as a widening circle: the buyer (Chapter 4), the seller (Chapter 5), the meeting of the two (Chapter 6), the competition among sellers (Chapter 7), the organization of the seller (Chapter 8), and the workers who make the whole system run (Chapter 9). Each chapter ends with a Case Study, from electric cars to egg prices to the Delano grape strike, that lets you test the tools on a real event.

Big Ideas

1
Markets run on two sides. Buyers' willingness and ability to pay (demand) and producers' costs and incentives (supply) together set prices and quantities.
2
Prices are signals and incentives. A price tells people how scarce something is and rewards those who respond, which is how a market coordinates millions of choices.
3
Competition limits power. The number of sellers in a market shapes prices, quality, and choice, and government sometimes steps in to protect competition.
4
People are behind the curves. The form a business takes and the market for labor decide who owns, who bears risk, and who earns what.

Essential Questions

  1. How do buyers' choices create demand, and what makes demand change or respond strongly to a price change? (Ch. 4)
  2. How do costs and incentives shape what producers offer for sale? (Ch. 5)
  3. How do prices bring buyers and sellers into balance, and what happens when government overrides the price? (Ch. 6)
  4. How does the number of competing sellers affect prices, quality, and choices, and when should government step in? (Ch. 7)
  5. How do the legal form of a business and the market for labor determine who owns, who decides, who bears risk, and who earns what? (Chs. 8–9)
WHY THIS UNIT MATTERSPicture a Saturday in Bakersfield. At the grocery store you notice that eggs cost more than they did last month. Your cousin has just started a first job at $16.90 an hour, California's minimum wage in 2026, and two of her coworkers argue about whether that wage is too high or too low. Meanwhile you and a friend have an idea for a weekend lawn-care business and wonder whether to share ownership, and who would pay if something went wrong. Each of these is a Unit 2 question about prices, wages, and business choices. By the end of this unit you will be able to explain what is happening in each case with evidence instead of guesses.

Key Terms Preview

Twenty Words You Will Own by the End of the Unit

Each definition below comes from the chapter where the term is taught, with the Spanish term the chapter gives. Look for the highlighted terms as you read.

CH. 4demand (demanda)
The willingness to buy a good or service and the ability to pay for it.
CH. 4law of demand (ley de la demanda)
When a product's price rises, the quantity demanded falls, and when the price falls, it rises, other things equal.
CH. 4substitute (sustituto)
A good that can be used in place of another; when one's price rises, demand for the other rises.
CH. 4price elasticity of demand (elasticidad precio de la demanda)
The percent change in quantity demanded divided by the percent change in price.
CH. 5supply (oferta)
The willingness and ability of producers to offer goods or services for sale at various prices.
CH. 5law of supply (ley de la oferta)
When a product's price rises, the quantity supplied rises, and when the price falls, it falls, other things equal.
CH. 5marginal cost (costo marginal)
The extra cost of producing one more unit of a good.
CH. 5economies of scale (economías de escala)
Lower costs per unit that come from producing a larger quantity in the long run.
CH. 6equilibrium price (precio de equilibrio)
The price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied.
CH. 6shortage (escasez)
A situation in which the quantity demanded is greater than the quantity supplied at a given price.
CH. 6surplus (excedente)
A situation in which the quantity supplied is greater than the quantity demanded at a given price.
CH. 6price ceiling (precio máximo)
A legal maximum price; it creates a shortage if set below the equilibrium price.
CH. 7perfect competition (competencia perfecta)
A market structure with many buyers and sellers, a standardized product, good information, free entry and exit, and price-taking sellers.
CH. 7monopoly (monopolio)
A market structure in which one seller supplies a good with no close substitutes.
CH. 7oligopoly (oligopolio)
A market structure in which a few large firms dominate the market.
CH. 8limited liability (responsabilidad limitada)
The protection that limits stockholders' losses to the amount they invested.
CH. 8corporation (corporación)
A business organization that the law treats as a separate legal person, owned by stockholders.
CH. 9wage (salario)
The payment a worker receives for labor; the price in the labor market.
CH. 9productivity (productividad)
The amount of goods or services a worker produces in a given amount of time.
CH. 9labor union (sindicato)
An organization of workers who join together to improve wages, benefits, and working conditions.

Connect to the Book

Look Back and Look Ahead

LOOK BACK · UNIT 1

Chapter 1 gave you the tools of economic thinking: scarcity, opportunity cost, thinking at the margin, and incentives. Unit 2 uses all four. Demand and supply are incentives at work, marginal cost drives a producer’s choice, and every business owner and worker faces an opportunity cost.

Chapter 2 compared economic systems and showed that a market economy lets prices spread information. Chapter 3 described the American free enterprise system and its features of private property, the profit motive, and competition. This unit shows those features in action: prices (Chapter 6), competition (Chapter 7), and the profit motive and property rights in the business forms of Chapter 8.

LOOK AHEAD · UNITS 3 AND 4

Unit 3, Consumer and Personal Finance (Chapters 10–13), turns from markets to your own money. Chapter 10 on budgeting uses the demand ideas of this unit (income, prices, and substitutes) to build a budget and compare your spending with national averages. Chapter 13, Getting Out on Your Own, explains the income, Social Security, and Medicare taxes that workers and the self-employed pay, which builds on Chapters 8 and 9.

Unit 4, Money and the Macroeconomy (Chapters 14–17), zooms out from single markets to the whole economy. Chapter 15, Financial Markets, builds on the stocks and bonds in Chapter 8 and on how expectations move prices. Chapter 17, Facing Economic Challenges, returns to the unemployment and worker programs you meet in Chapter 9. Later units apply the same tools to government policy (Unit 5) and international trade (Unit 6).
