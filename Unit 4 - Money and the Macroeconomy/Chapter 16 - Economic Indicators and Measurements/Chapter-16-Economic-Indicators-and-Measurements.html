Concept Review

Macroeconomics is the study of the economy as a whole. Chapter 14 explained money and banking, and Chapter 15 explained how financial markets move savings to borrowers. Both chapters used totals, such as the money supply and the number of banks. This chapter shows how economists measure the biggest totals of all: everything a nation produces, everyone who has a job, and the general level of prices.

Chapter 16 Key Concept

National income accounting uses statistical measures of output, income, employment, and prices to describe what is happening in an economy. Indicators such as GDP, the unemployment rate, and the inflation rate help people recognize the phases of the business cycle, tell real changes from changes in prices, and judge whether an economy is growing over the long run.

Why the Concept Matters

Suppose you are a senior at Shafter High School looking for a first full-time job. On the news you hear that the economy "added 165,000 jobs last month," that prices are "up 3.7 percent from last year," and that the economy "grew at a 3.4 percent rate last quarter." Are those numbers good news? Should you take a job now, wait for a better offer, or enroll at Bakersfield College? What about your parents, who may be deciding whether to buy a house or a truck? Businesses make the same kinds of choices when they decide whether to hire workers or build a new packing shed. Each decision depends on a guess about where the economy is heading. In this chapter you will learn what those numbers mean, how they are calculated, and why careful readers ask where the data came from before they trust a headline.

Key Concepts

Measuring an Economy

Imagine that a coach at Shafter High School wants to know whether the football team is getting better. She could not simply watch one play. She would look at the scoreboard, the yards gained, and the record over several seasons. In the same way, an economist who wants to know how a country is doing cannot simply look around. A nation has more than 340 million people making billions of choices every day. To describe all of that activity, economists use a system of statistics called national income accounting: the measurement of output, income, spending, and prices for a whole economy.

The idea is fairly new. Before the 1930s the government kept no regular count of the nation's total output. When the Great Depression hit, President Herbert Hoover and Congress had to make decisions without knowing how badly the economy had shrunk. In 1932 the Senate asked the Department of Commerce to measure national income, and an economist named Simon Kuznets led the work. His team's estimates, and the system that grew out of them, now come from the Bureau of Economic Analysis (BEA), an agency of the Department of Commerce. Today the United States publishes a full set of national accounts every quarter, along with monthly reports on jobs and prices from the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS).

The most widely quoted number in the system is gross domestic product (GDP), the total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given period of time. Each part of that definition matters. Read it slowly, because it explains most of what GDP does and does not tell you.

Market value. A country produces millions of different things: almonds, oil, haircuts, software, and school lessons. To add them together, economists use prices. If a farm near Shafter sells 100 tons of almonds at $5,000 a ton, those almonds add $500,000 to the economy's output. A tax-preparation office that earns $200,000 from services adds $200,000. Prices let us add very different things.

Final goods and services. Final goods and services are products sold to their last user, such as a consumer buying a loaf of bread. GDP does not count intermediate goods, which are used up in making something else, such as the flour a bakery buys. If GDP counted the flour and also the bread, it would count the flour twice, because its value is already in the price of the bread. Economists count the bread only.

Produced within a country's borders. GDP counts what is made inside the United States, no matter who owns the factory. A Japanese company's car plant in Kentucky adds to U.S. GDP. A Bakersfield company's factory in Mexico does not.

In a given period. GDP is a flow, like the water running from a faucet in an hour, not a pool of stored water. In the United States it is reported by quarter and by year. The BEA states quarterly numbers at an annual rate, which means that a quarter's output is multiplied by four, so that quarters can be compared with whole years.

Only current production. Selling a used truck or a ten-year-old house does not add to GDP, because it was counted in the year it was made. The commission paid to the sales agent does count, because the agent performed a service this year. Neither does GDP count purely financial transactions such as buying stock or receiving a Social Security check, because no new good or service has been produced.

EXAMPLECounting Value Added (Hypothetical)

A Kern County almond grower sells almonds to a processor for $30,000. The processor shells and packs them and sells them to a snack company for $42,000. The snack company turns them into snack packs that it sells to stores for $60,000. Stores sell the packs to shoppers for $80,000.

Final sales method: GDP counts only the final sale to shoppers, $80,000. Value-added method: add each stage's contribution. The grower adds $30,000 (assume the grower bought no inputs). The processor adds $42,000 − $30,000 = $12,000. The snack company adds $60,000 − $42,000 = $18,000. The stores add $80,000 − $60,000 = $20,000. The total is $30,000 + $12,000 + $18,000 + $20,000 = $80,000. Both methods give the same answer, and neither counts the almonds more than once.

Key Concepts

The Four Parts of GDP

Economists calculate GDP in two main ways that must give the same answer. The income approach adds up everything earned by the people who produced the output: wages, rent, interest, and profit. The expenditure approach, which is easier to see in daily life, adds up everything spent on final goods and services. Every dollar spent by one person is a dollar of income for someone else, so the two approaches match. The BEA divides spending into four groups.

Consumption (C) is spending by households on goods and services, from groceries and haircuts to health care and rent. It is by far the largest part of the U.S. economy. Investment (I), in the language of GDP, means spending by businesses on equipment, buildings, and software, spending on new houses, and changes in the stock of unsold goods that businesses hold. It does not mean buying stocks and bonds. Government purchases (G) is spending by federal, state, and local governments on goods and services, such as school buildings, teachers' salaries, and road repairs. It does not include transfer payments, such as Social Security checks, because no good or service is produced in exchange. Net exports (X − M) equals the value of goods and services sold to other countries (exports) minus the value of goods and services bought from other countries (imports). Imports are subtracted because they are included in C, I, and G even though they were produced elsewhere.

Putting the four groups together gives the most important formula in this chapter: GDP = C + I + G + (X − M). Figure 16.2 shows what the formula looked like in 2025.

Key Concepts

Nominal GDP and Real GDP

Suppose a news report says that GDP grew by 5 percent. Does that mean the economy produced 5 percent more goods and services? Not necessarily. GDP is measured in dollars, and dollars buy different amounts in different years. If every price rose by 5 percent and the economy did not produce a single additional item, GDP measured at current prices would still go up by 5 percent. That is why economists distinguish between two kinds of data.

Nominal GDP (or current-dollar GDP) is the value of output measured at the prices of the year in which the output was produced. Real GDP is the value of output measured at the prices of a fixed base year, so that only changes in the quantities produced affect the result. The same idea applies to any dollar figure, which economists call nominal when it is measured in the dollars of the day, and real when it has been adjusted for changes in prices. This distinction between nominal and real data is one of the most important ideas in macroeconomics and one of the standards in California's economics course (12.5.1).

The BEA adjusts for prices by using a price index, a number that tracks the average price of a large group of goods against a base year set to 100. The most general index of prices used for GDP is the GDP deflator. In the formula below, the deflator is expressed as a number in which the base year equals 100, so a deflator of 130 means prices are 30 percent higher than in the base year.

Real GDP = (Nominal GDP ÷ Price index) × 100. Real GDP can then be compared across years. The BEA currently uses chained 2017 dollars, so published real GDP is expressed in the prices of 2017 (a technical process called chaining updates the weights each year).

EXAMPLEFrom Nominal to Real: A Pizza Economy (Hypothetical)

Suppose a tiny economy produces only pizzas. In Year 1 (the base year) it produces 100 pizzas and each sells for $10, so nominal GDP is $1,000. In Year 2 it produces 100 pizzas again, but each now sells for $12, so nominal GDP is $1,200.

Nominal GDP rose by ($1,200 − $1,000) ÷ $1,000 = 20 percent, but the economy did not produce a single extra pizza. The price index is 12 ÷ 10 × 100 = 120. Real GDP in Year 2 is $1,200 ÷ 120 × 100 = $1,000, the same as in Year 1. Real GDP growth is 0 percent. Now suppose that in Year 3 the economy makes 110 pizzas at $12. Nominal GDP is $1,320, and real GDP at Year 1 prices is 110 × $10 = $1,100, a gain of 10 percent over Year 2's $1,000.

ECONOMICS PACESETTER

Simon KuznetsThe Economist Who Gave the Nation Its First Scoreboard

Economists today take it for granted that someone will report how large the economy is. In the early 1930s no one could. When the stock market crashed and factories closed, leaders in Washington had only scattered facts: some railroad freight figures, some bank records, and some newspaper estimates. They could not say how much the nation's output had fallen or which parts of the economy had been hurt the most.

In 1932 the Senate asked the Department of Commerce to find out. The Department hired Simon Kuznets, a young economist who had been born in the Russian Empire and who worked for the National Bureau of Economic Research. Kuznets and a small staff collected data from tax returns, company reports, and government records and organized it into a consistent set of accounts. In 1934 he reported that the nation's national income had fallen by more than half between 1929 and 1932, from about $87 billion to about $42 billion. For the first time, the country could see the size of the collapse in numbers.

Kuznets also warned against reading too much into one number. In his report to Congress he wrote that the welfare of a nation can scarcely be inferred from a measure of national income. He argued that economists should ask what kinds of growth were being counted and what costs were being ignored. During World War II, the government adopted a related measure, gross national product, as the main tool for planning wartime production, and in 1991 the United States switched to GDP as its main measure. Kuznets received the Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences in 1971 for his work on how economies grow.

The numbers that Kuznets helped invent are the ones you will use throughout this chapter. His warning is still good advice: a number can guide decisions, but only if you understand what it leaves out.

Key Concepts

What GDP Leaves Out

GDP is a useful tool, but it is not a measure of everything that matters. Economists agree that it has several limitations, and you should keep them in mind whenever someone uses GDP to argue that a country is better or worse off.

First, GDP leaves out most nonmarket activities, which are goods and services produced and consumed without being sold for money. When a parent cooks dinner at home, GDP does not change, but when the same family buys dinner at a Bakersfield restaurant, it does. Unpaid work such as caring for children, volunteering at a food bank, and fixing your own car is valuable, and it is missing from GDP. A country in which more of that work moves into the marketplace can show a rise in GDP even though no more work is done.

Second, GDP leaves out the underground economy: activity that is hidden from the government, either because it is illegal or because the people involved do not report it to avoid taxes or regulations. Illegal activities such as drug dealing are not recorded, and neither are some cash-only transactions, such as a worker paid under the table. The BEA does make estimates for some unreported income, but the full size of the underground economy is unknown.

Third, GDP does not count leisure. Two countries could have the same GDP, but if one has people working 60 hours a week and the other 35, the second country's citizens enjoy more free time. Fourth, GDP does not subtract the costs of pollution, resource depletion, or crime. A cleanup after an oil spill raises GDP because someone is paid to do it, even though the spill made people worse off. Fifth, GDP says nothing about the distribution of income. A country with a high GDP per person could have a few very rich people and many poor ones. Finally, GDP does not measure quality of life: health, safety, education, and freedom matter to people but do not have a price.

None of this makes GDP useless. When economists compare the same country across years, or compare countries that measure output in similar ways, GDP is a good guide to the amount of goods and services produced. Many economists also look at other measures, such as the Human Development Index published by the United Nations, which combines income with life expectancy and years of schooling.

Key Concepts

Other Measures of National Income

GDP is only the starting point. The BEA publishes a chain of related measures that move from the value of output toward the amount of money households can actually spend. Each measure answers a different question.

Gross national product (GNP) measures the value of final goods and services produced by the citizens and companies of a country, wherever they are in the world. To go from GDP to GNP, add the income that Americans earn from abroad, such as profits that a U.S. company earns from a plant in Mexico, and subtract the income that foreign citizens and companies earn in the United States. Because the two flows are roughly equal for the United States, GNP is very close to GDP, as Figure 16.4 shows. For countries with many workers or companies abroad, the difference can be larger.

Machines and buildings wear out as they are used. The loss of value of capital through wear and obsolescence is called depreciation, or the consumption of fixed capital. Net national product (NNP) equals GNP minus depreciation. It is a better measure of how much the nation produced after replacing worn-out equipment. National income (NI) is the total income earned by the nation's resources: wages and salaries, rent, interest, and profits. It is calculated from NNP after other adjustments, mainly for indirect business taxes such as sales taxes and for some statistical differences.

Personal income (PI) is the income that households actually receive from all sources before paying income taxes. It starts with national income, removes income that is earned but not paid to households (such as corporate profits that are kept inside the company and social insurance contributions), and adds transfer payments such as Social Security and unemployment benefits. Finally, disposable personal income (DPI) is personal income minus personal income taxes. It is the amount households have available to spend or to save, and it is closely watched because consumer spending is two-thirds of GDP.

Key Concepts

Measuring Jobs: The Unemployment Rate

GDP counts output. A second set of indicators counts people. Each month the Bureau of Labor Statistics and the Census Bureau survey about 60,000 households across the country in what is called the Current Population Survey. Interviewers ask whether each adult in the household is working, and if not, whether the adult is looking for work. From the answers, the BLS estimates the status of the whole population.

The BLS divides the civilian population age 16 and older who are not in institutions such as prisons into three groups. People are employed if they did any paid work in the survey week, or worked in a family business. People are unemployed if they do not have a job, have actively looked for work in the past four weeks, and are available to work. Everyone else, including students, retirees, parents who stay home, and people who have given up looking, is not in the labor force. The labor force is the sum of the employed and the unemployed, and the unemployment rate is the percent of the labor force that is unemployed:

Unemployment rate = (Number unemployed ÷ Labor force) × 100. In October 2026, about 163.6 million people were employed and 7.1 million were unemployed. The labor force was 163.6 + 7.1 = 170.7 million, and the unemployment rate was 7.1 ÷ 170.7 × 100 ≈ 4.1 percent. The labor force participation rate, the percent of the adult population that is in the labor force, was 61.9 percent.

The unemployment rate is a good indicator, but it has limits. A person who stops looking because he believes that no job is available is called a discouraged worker. Because discouraged workers are not looking, they are not counted as unemployed, and the rate can fall even if no new jobs are created. People who work part-time because they cannot find full-time jobs count as employed. The BLS therefore publishes broader measures too. Types of unemployment, and why some unemployment is natural, are the subject of Chapter 17.

Key Concepts

Measuring Prices: The Consumer Price Index

The third kind of indicator tracks prices. Chapter 14 introduced the idea that inflation reduces the purchasing power of money. To measure it, the BLS builds the Consumer Price Index (CPI), a measure of the average change over time in the prices paid by urban consumers for a fixed basket of goods and services. Each month, BLS employees and data collectors record about 80,000 prices in stores, restaurants, doctors' offices, and online, and rents from thousands of landlords and tenants.

The basket is based on a survey of what families buy. Figure 16.8 shows how it was weighted in December 2025. Housing, which includes rent and the cost of owning a home, is the largest group at 44.5 percent. Transportation, mainly cars, car repairs, and gasoline, is 16.3 percent. Food and beverages are 14.5 percent. The weights are updated each year so that the basket reflects what people actually buy. The BLS sets an index base period, 1982–84, equal to 100. The CPI-U for September 2026 was 336.9, meaning that the typical basket of goods that cost $100 in 1982–84 cost about $337 in September 2026.

Economists use the CPI to calculate the inflation rate, the percent change in a price index over a period of time. Key Concepts

The Phases of the Business Cycle

If you plotted U.S. real GDP for the past century, you would not see a straight, steady climb. You would see a rising line with dips. Most years the economy produces more than the year before, and people find work and spend freely. Then, every few years, output stalls or falls, businesses lay off workers, and people spend cautiously. Later the economy recovers. The repeated pattern of expansion and contraction in a nation's economy is called the business cycle.

The word cycle can be misleading. The ups and downs do not come at regular intervals, as the phases of the moon do. Some expansions last a few years and some last a decade. Economists describe the cycle in four phases, which Figure 16.10 shows. In an expansion, real GDP, employment, and incomes rise. The expansion ends at a peak, the high point of economic activity before a downturn. A contraction follows, a period in which economic activity falls. It ends at a trough, the lowest point of the downturn, and a new expansion (sometimes called a recovery) begins.

Over the long run, the economy tends to grow, so each peak is usually higher than the one before it. The long-term increase in a nation's output is called economic growth, and it is the subject of Section 3. The business cycle describes the short-run fluctuations around that long-run trend.

Not every downturn is equally serious. A recession is a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months. Many people use a quick rule: two or more consecutive quarters of falling real GDP. In the United States, the official judges are the members of the Business Cycle Dating Committee of the National Bureau of Economic Research (NBER), a private research group. The committee looks at employment, income, spending, and production, not only at GDP, and it announces the months of the peak and trough after the fact, sometimes more than a year later. The 2020 recession lasted only two months (February to April 2020), the shortest on record, even though the fall was very steep, because the economy reopened quickly.

A depression is an unusually severe and long downturn. The word has no official definition, but the Great Depression of the 1930s is the model. Between 1929 and 1933, real output in the United States fell by more than a quarter, and about one in four workers was unemployed. A depression is not simply a bigger recession, because it usually brings widespread bank failures, falling prices, and years of slow recovery.

On average, the NBER finds that recessions have lasted about 10 months and expansions about 64 months, and expansions have become longer over time. Expansions do not die of old age; they end when something disrupts them.

Key Concepts

Aggregate Demand and Aggregate Supply

To explain why the economy expands and contracts, economists use a model that you can think of as the macroeconomic version of the supply and demand diagram you studied in Unit 2. Chapter 6 explained how the price and quantity of one good, such as a pizza, are set by demand and supply. The new model explains the overall price level and total output.

Aggregate demand is the total quantity of final goods and services that households, businesses, governments, and foreign buyers want to purchase at each price level. In terms of the GDP formula you have already learned, it is the total of C + I + G + (X − M). The aggregate demand curve slopes downward: when the overall price level is lower, people's money buys more, so they buy a larger quantity of output.

Aggregate supply is the total quantity of final goods and services that businesses are willing to produce at each price level. In the short run, the aggregate supply curve slopes upward, because many costs, such as wages and rents, are slow to change. When the price level rises and input costs have not yet caught up, producing more is more profitable. In the long run, economists believe that an economy's output is limited by its resources, technology, and institutions, so the long-run aggregate supply curve is vertical at the economy's potential output, the level it can sustain when its workers and machines are fully employed.

Macroeconomic equilibrium occurs where the aggregate demand curve crosses the aggregate supply curve. At that point the quantity of output that buyers want to purchase equals the quantity that producers want to supply, and the economy has an equilibrium price level and a level of real GDP. In Figure 16.13, point E marks the starting equilibrium in each graph.

What moves the curves? Changes in the business cycle come from shifts in aggregate demand or aggregate supply, which economists call shocks when they are sudden. A shift of aggregate demand to the left, for example because families become worried and cut spending, lowers output and, in many cases, leads to layoffs. A rightward shift of aggregate demand has the opposite effect: output rises, employment rises, and prices tend to rise too. The main things that shift aggregate demand are changes in consumer confidence, business investment, government spending and taxes, interest rates, and spending by foreign buyers.

Shocks to aggregate supply come from changes in the costs of production or in productive resources. A sudden rise in the price of oil raises costs for farmers, truckers, and manufacturers, and shifts short-run aggregate supply to the left. The result, shown in Graph B, is higher prices and lower output at the same time, a combination that economists call stagflation, a word made up of stagnation and inflation. Bad weather, a pandemic that disrupts factories and shipping, or a war can have the same effect. A new technology or a fall in energy prices shifts supply to the right.

Expectations matter too. If businesses expect weak sales, they cancel plans to hire or to build, and weak spending makes their forecast come true. If households expect their incomes to grow, they spend more. Economists measure these moods with surveys of consumer confidence and business expectations. Finally, government policy can shift the curves. In Chapters 19 and 20 you will see how fiscal policy (taxes and spending) and monetary policy (the Federal Reserve) are used to push aggregate demand in the direction that policymakers want.

Key Concepts

Business Cycles in U.S. History

Since the nation's founding, the American economy has had many downturns. They were called panics in the 1800s and recessions or depressions in the 1900s. The NBER has dated 34 contractions since 1854. The following episodes show how different causes can produce downturns.

The Great Depression (1929–1933). A stock market crash in October 1929, the failure of thousands of banks, and a collapse in spending set off the deepest downturn in U.S. history. Real output fell by more than a quarter, prices fell, and unemployment reached roughly 25 percent in 1933. Families that had lost their farms or jobs moved long distances to find work. From Oklahoma, Arkansas, and Texas, tens of thousands of migrants came to California's Central Valley, including Kern County. Photographers working for the federal government recorded the camps and shacks where they lived. The government responded with programs that created jobs, insured bank deposits, and set up Social Security, which are examined in Chapters 14 and 18.

World War II and the postwar boom. Spending on war production lifted output and ended the Depression. After the war, the economy shifted to peacetime production and entered the longest period of steady expansion until then, with only short and mild recessions through the 1950s and 1960s.

The stagflation of the 1970s. The 1973 oil embargo and a second oil price shock in 1979 shifted aggregate supply to the left. Unemployment rose to 9.0 percent in May 1975, while prices rose by more than 12 percent in 1974. The usual policy answers to unemployment (more spending) and to inflation (less spending) pointed in opposite directions, and policymakers struggled to find a solution.

The early 1980s and 1990s. To end double-digit inflation, the Federal Reserve raised interest rates sharply, and in the 1981–82 recession unemployment reached 10.8 percent in late 1982. Inflation then fell to about 4 percent. A shorter recession in 1990–91 followed an oil price spike, a savings and loan crisis, and a drop in confidence.

Predicting the Business Cycle

Because recessions are costly, businesses, workers, and policymakers would like to see them coming. No one has found a reliable way to do so, but economists track groups of statistics that change at different times in relation to the cycle.

Leading indicators change before the economy changes direction, so they point to what may come next. Examples are new building permits, new orders for durable goods, the stock market, consumers' expectations, and the number of people filing first-time claims for unemployment insurance. Coincident indicators change at the same time as the economy and tell you where it is now, such as payroll employment, industrial production, and personal income. Lagging indicators change after the economy has turned, confirming what has already happened. The unemployment rate and the CPI are two examples, because employers hire and cut prices slowly. Figure 16.14 gives examples of each.

The Conference Board, a private research group, publishes an index of leading indicators each month. When the index declines for several months in a row, some analysts predict a downturn. The record of prediction is mixed, however. The indicators sometimes signal a recession that does not happen, and they miss shocks, such as a pandemic or a war, that no one can see ahead of time. A good forecaster combines many signals and expresses its forecast as a range of possibilities rather than a promise.

One of the most closely watched leading indicators has to do with interest rates. Chapter 14 explained that the price of borrowing money is the interest rate, and that rates differ by the length of the loan. A short-term interest rate is the interest rate on loans that must be repaid within a short time, usually a year or less. The federal funds rate, which banks charge each other for overnight loans and which the Federal Reserve targets, and the 3-month Treasury bill rate are examples. A long-term interest rate is the rate on loans for many years, such as the 10-year Treasury note or a 30-year home mortgage.

The two kinds of rates are set in different ways, and that is why they have different meanings. Short-term rates are closely tied to the Federal Reserve's decisions about how tight or loose to make credit, so they tell you about current policy. Long-term rates reflect what investors expect inflation, growth, and short-term rates to be over many years, plus a premium for risk and for tying up their money. A rise in long-term rates raises the cost of mortgages, car loans, and business investment, so it matters for the housing market and for businesses planning for the long term. A change in short-term rates changes the cost of credit card debt, short business loans, and variable-rate loans quickly.

Normally, lenders demand a higher rate for tying up their money longer, so long-term rates are above short-term rates. A graph of interest rates by length of time to repayment is called a yield curve, and a normal yield curve slopes upward. Sometimes, short-term rates rise above long-term rates and the curve is inverted. An inversion has often been a signal that investors expect slower growth and falling interest rates in the future, and it has preceded most recent recessions. In Figure 16.15, the difference between the 10-year and 2-year Treasury yields fell below zero in 2000 and again in 2006–2007, and a recession followed each time, in 2001 and in 2007. The curve inverted again in 2022 and stayed inverted until mid-2024, and no recession has been dated through October 2026. Economists continue to debate whether the signal is as reliable as it once seemed.

Key Concepts

How Economists Measure Growth

Section 2 explained the short-run ups and downs of the business cycle. This section turns to something slower and, for most people, more important: the long-run rise in the amount that an economy can produce. A household in the United States today consumes far more goods and services than a household in 1960 did, and a family in China today lives far better than a family there in 1990. Economists want to know why, so that other countries, and the United States, can keep it up.

The most common measure of economic growth is the economic growth rate, the percent change in real GDP from one period to the next. Growth rate = ((Real GDP this year − Real GDP last year) ÷ Real GDP last year) × 100. Real GDP is used because it removes the effect of price changes. In 2025, for example, real GDP averaged $23,943 billion, compared with $23,395 billion in 2024, so growth was (23,943 − 23,395) ÷ 23,395 × 100 ≈ 2.3 percent. Between the third quarter of 2025 and the third quarter of 2026, real GDP grew by about 2.1 percent (24,611.1 ÷ 24,113.0 − 1).

Total output can grow just because a country has more people. That is why economists also look at real GDP per capita, real GDP divided by population. It tells you the average output per person, adjusted for price changes, and it is the standard way to judge whether the typical person's material standard of living is rising. Figure 16.16 shows that U.S. real GDP per person rose from about $19,400 in 1960 to about $70,000 in 2025 (in 2017 dollars), which is 3.6 times as much, or an average annual rate of about 2.0 percent.

Key Concepts

What Makes an Economy Grow?

If real GDP is the amount of goods and services a country produces, then growth can come from only two places: using more inputs, or getting more output from each input. Economists, who study the sources of growth, divide the inputs into labor and capital, and call everything else that raises output technology, organization, and institutions. Figure 16.18 summarizes the three main sources.

The first is labor input: the total hours of work performed in the economy. It grows when the population grows, when a larger share of adults take jobs, and when workers put in more hours. In the United States, labor input grew rapidly in the 1960s and 1970s as the baby boomers came of age and women entered the labor force in large numbers. Now the population is aging, and growth of the labor force is much slower than in the past. Immigration affects labor input, as do health and the amount of time that people choose to work.

Labor input is not just a matter of how many hours people work but also of what they know. The knowledge, skills, and health that workers bring to their jobs are called human capital. A worker who has finished high school, earned a certificate in welding or nursing, or completed a college degree can produce more per hour than a worker without the training. Education and job training are therefore investments, with costs now and payoffs later. Chapter 9 showed that workers with more education tend to earn more, and the earnings reflect their higher productivity.

The second source is capital. When businesses invest in more equipment, buildings, and software per worker, the growth is called capital deepening. A farm worker with a tractor and a harvesting machine can handle many more acres than a worker with a hoe. In the Central Valley, a grower who installs a drip irrigation system and mechanized equipment can harvest more nuts with fewer hours of labor. Capital deepening needs saving and investment: people and businesses must be willing to forgo consumption today to buy capital that will pay off later. Chapter 15 explained how financial markets turn savings into investment.

The third source is the improvement in how inputs are used. Productivity is the amount of output produced per unit of input, and the most common version, labor productivity, is output per hour of work. Productivity rises with human capital and capital deepening, but it also rises through new technology, better management, and better organization of work. Economists call the part of productivity growth that cannot be explained by more capital or more skilled workers multifactor productivity (also called total factor productivity). It includes the effect of new ideas, new production methods, better supply chains, and an economy's laws and institutions. Over the long run, multifactor productivity is the source that is most closely connected with large increases in living standards.

Policies That Promote Growth

Because growth raises living standards, governments, businesses, and families all have reasons to encourage it. Economists agree on several things that matter, although they disagree about how much each should receive and who should pay for it.

Saving and investment. Capital deepening requires funds. Policies that encourage saving, such as tax-advantaged retirement accounts, and policies that make credit available at reasonable rates, such as a stable banking system (Chapter 14), help finance new equipment. Governments also invest directly in infrastructure such as highways, ports, water systems, and electricity grids, which private businesses depend on.

Education and health. Public schools, community colleges, apprenticeships, and public health programs raise human capital. For Kern County, institutions such as Bakersfield College and the local career technical programs prepare workers for jobs in energy, health care, and agriculture. Students and families pay a cost now, in tuition and foregone earnings, to earn more later.

Research and technology. New ideas can be used by many people at once, and the person who creates one often cannot capture all of its benefits, so private firms may invest too little in research. Governments therefore support research through universities and agencies, and they protect inventors through patents.

Institutions. Economists have found that growth depends heavily on the rules of the game. Secure property rights, enforceable contracts, an honest court system, and stable money give people confidence that they will keep what they earn, so they are willing to save, invest, and start businesses. Competition and trade open markets and push firms to find better methods, which Chapter 21 discusses in more detail.

Trade-offs. Policies to promote growth are not free. Money spent on research or roads cannot be used for something else, and tax breaks that encourage investment reduce government revenue. Faster growth can also bring costs: new technology can eliminate some jobs even as it creates others, and rapid industrialization can damage the environment. Not everyone benefits equally. Citizens choose among these goals through their votes and their purchases, and it is the choice among them that economists call opportunity cost, the idea you learned in Chapter 1.

CASE STUDY

Reading the Numbers:
How Is Kern County's Economy Doing?

The national indicators describe the country, but families live in places. Kern County is one of the largest agricultural and oil-producing counties in the United States, and its numbers tell a story that no single indicator captures: real output is growing, yet unemployment is far above the national rate. Learning to read local data is a skill that voters, workers, and business owners can use.

Background

Three Ways to Look at a Local Economy

Economists use the same ideas to study a county that they use to study a nation, but the data come from different sources. The Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes county GDP once a year, with a delay of about a year. The most recent county figures are for 2024. The Bureau of Labor Statistics, working with California's Employment Development Department, estimates unemployment for each county every month, using a statistical model that combines the household survey, unemployment insurance claims, and the payroll survey. And the payroll survey reports nonfarm jobs by industry for the Bakersfield metropolitan area, which is Kern County.

Each source answers a different question. County GDP asks how much the local economy produces, adjusted for price changes. The unemployment rate asks what share of people who want work cannot find it. The jobs by industry data ask where the jobs are. A good reader of the numbers looks at all three, compares them with state and national numbers, and asks which differences are real and which come from how the numbers are measured.

Kern County's economy is unusual. It produces more crops, by value, than almost any other county in the nation, including almonds, grapes, citrus, pistachios, and carrots, and it produces most of California's oil. It also has some of the nation's largest wind and solar projects, large warehouses and distribution centers, and a large health care and government sector. Farm work is highly seasonal, with many jobs in the spring and fall harvests and fewer in the winter, so unemployment in the county rises and falls with the calendar. Farm jobs are counted in the household survey, but not in the payroll survey's nonfarm total, so the two sources paint different parts of the picture.

In 2025, Kern County's unemployment rate averaged about 9 percent, compared with about 5.5 percent for California and 4.3 percent for the nation (Figure 16.6). In September 2026 the county's preliminary rate was 7.9 percent, the lowest of the year. Over the same period, county real GDP was growing. How can a county's output rise while many workers are jobless? The four documents below help you investigate.

A Community College Counselor
"The numbers show that the largest groups of jobs are in government, warehouses and trucking, and health care. Many students ask me about those fields. An Oilfield Supervisor
"Our industry produces a huge share of the county's output with a small number of workers, because equipment does much of the work. When oil prices drop, county GDP can fall even though only a few thousand jobs are involved. I would not read much into a single year of data. Look at the trend over many years, and look at prices."
A County Planner
"I use all three sources. GDP tells me the economy is bigger than ever in real terms. The unemployment rate says that many residents are still not finding steady work, and it is consistently higher than California's. And the jobs data show that a large share of work is in government and in industries that do not pay as much as oil. Growth and well-being are not the same thing."

Chapter 16 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Gross Domestic Product and Other Indicators
National income accounting measures output, income, jobs, and prices. GDP is the market value of final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a period. It equals C + I + G + (X − M). Nominal GDP uses current prices, and real GDP removes price changes so that growth can be measured. GDP leaves out nonmarket and underground activity. GNP, NNP, national income, personal income, and disposable personal income measure income at different stages. The unemployment rate is the percent of the labor force without a job and looking for one. The CPI measures the prices of a fixed basket, and the inflation rate is the percent change in the CPI.
Section 2 · Business Cycles
The business cycle has expansion, peak, contraction, and trough phases. Recessions are dated by the NBER. Aggregate demand and aggregate supply explain changes in output and prices, and shocks to either can cause downturns, including stagflation. Leading, coincident, and lagging indicators help economists judge the economy. Short-term rates reflect Federal Reserve policy and long-term rates reflect expectations, and an inverted yield curve has often come before a recession.
Section 3 · Stimulating Economic Growth
Economic growth is the percent change in real GDP, and real GDP per capita measures average output per person. The rule of 70 estimates doubling time. Growth comes from labor input (including human capital), capital deepening, and multifactor productivity. Saving, education, research, and strong institutions raise growth, but every policy has trade-offs and an opportunity cost.
