Concept Review

In Chapter 4 you learned that demand is the willingness and ability to buy, that the law of demand links higher prices to lower quantities, and that elasticity measures how strongly buyers respond. Every purchase needs a seller. In this chapter you will see why sellers offer more of a good when prices rise, what it costs them to produce, what shifts their willingness to sell, and how quickly they can respond.

Chapter 5 Key Concept

Supply is the willingness and ability of producers to offer goods and services for sale at various prices. The law of supply says that, other things equal, when the price of a good rises, producers offer more of it for sale, and when the price falls, they offer less. Supply changes when something other than price changes, such as the cost of inputs, technology, taxes, or the number of sellers.

Why the Concept Matters

You may not think of yourself as a producer, but you are one whenever you offer your time for pay. Suppose a neighbor offers you $8 an hour to mow a lawn. You might say no, because mowing in the Bakersfield heat is hard work and you could use the time better. At $15 an hour you may say yes. At $25 an hour you might also hire your cousin and take on three more lawns. As the price offered goes up, the quantity of work you supply goes up, because the reward now outweighs the cost. Businesses make the same calculation on a larger scale. By the end of this chapter you will be able to draw a supply curve, explain the costs that sit behind it, say what moves it, and judge how quickly producers can respond to a price change.

Key Concepts

Supply and Incentives

Every item in a store was made by a producer, a person or business that makes goods or provides services. A pistachio grower in Lost Hills, a baker on Chester Avenue, and a mechanic in Shafter are all producers. The decisions they make about how much to sell are as important to a market as the decisions of buyers. Economists call the willingness and ability of producers to offer goods and services for sale at various prices supply.

As with demand, both parts matter. A producer must be willing to sell at a price, because the price covers the cost of production and leaves a reward. A producer must also be able to sell, which means having the land, labor, equipment, and materials to make the product. A farmer who would love to sell more tomatoes but has no water has a willingness but not an ability.

What makes producers willing to supply more? The answer is the profit incentive. Profit is the money a producer has left after paying all costs. When the price of a product goes up and costs stay the same, each unit sold earns more profit. That gives producers a reason to make more, and it also attracts new producers who see a chance to earn money. When the price falls, profit per unit shrinks, and some producers cut back or leave the business. Prices send signals, and the incentive of profit makes producers respond.

Key Concepts

Where Supply Comes From: Resources and Opportunity Cost

Behind every product on a store shelf is a collection of resources that someone gathered and put to work. Economists group them into land (and all natural resources), labor, capital, and entrepreneurship. On a Kern County almond farm, the land is the orchard soil, and the water that irrigates it is a natural resource too. The labor is the crew that prunes the trees and the driver who hauls the harvest. The capital is the tractors, shakers, pumps, and the huller that cracks the nuts. The entrepreneur is the person who decides to take the risk of planting trees at all. Supply exists only when someone is willing and able to bring these resources together.

Every resource has other possible uses, and that is where opportunity cost comes in. A 40-acre field outside Shafter could grow almonds, potatoes, or cotton, or it could hold rows of solar panels. A packing house could pack carrots, citrus, or grapes. A worker could pick fruit, drive a truck, or take a job at a warehouse. Whatever the choice, the next-best use is given up. For a producer to be willing to sell a product, the price must be high enough to make that use of the resources worth more than the next-best alternative. A higher price pulls more resources away from their other uses, which is one more way to explain why the quantity supplied rises when the price rises.

Producers also differ in what they must give up. A grower with deep, productive soil and a cheap source of water can make a box of oranges for less than a neighbor with poor soil and an expensive pump. The first grower is willing to sell at a lower price, and the second grower joins only when the price climbs higher. If you lined up all the growers in order from lowest cost to highest cost, you would see the supply curve forming as a kind of ladder. As the price rises, it reaches one rung after another, and each new grower who steps in adds to the quantity supplied. You saw this with Grower 3 in Figure 5.3.

Key Concepts

The Law of Supply

The pattern is so reliable that it has a name. The law of supply says that when the price of a good rises, the quantity supplied rises, and when the price falls, the quantity supplied falls, other things being equal. The quantity supplied is the amount producers are willing and able to sell at one particular price.

Three reasons explain the law. First, as you just read, a higher price means a higher profit on each unit, so producers want to make more. Second, a higher price can cover higher costs. In Section 2 you will see that making more of a good usually raises the cost of each additional unit, because producers must use less efficient land or pay overtime. A producer will only take on those costs if the price is high enough. Third, higher prices attract new producers who were not selling at the lower price.

The words “other things being equal” apply here too. The law of supply is about what happens when only the price of the product changes. If costs, technology, or taxes change, the whole supply changes, which is the topic of Section 3. Notice the contrast with demand. The demand curve slopes down because buyers want to pay less. The supply curve slopes up because sellers want to receive more.

The Supply Schedule and the Supply Curve

Economists show supply with the same two tools they use for demand. A supply schedule is a table that lists the quantity supplied at each price. A supply curve is the graph of the same information, with price on the vertical axis and quantity supplied on the horizontal axis.

Figure 5.1 shows a hypothetical supply schedule for navel oranges from a Kern County grower. At $10 per 40-pound box, the grower is willing to sell 100 boxes a week. At $14 it is willing to sell 300 boxes, and at $20 it will sell 600. Each pair of numbers becomes a point, and the points make a line that slopes upward from left to right. The upward slope is the picture of the law of supply.

You read a supply curve the same way you read a demand curve. Find the price on the vertical axis, move across to the curve, and drop down to read the quantity. At $16, the grower supplies 400 boxes. You can also read it the other way: to persuade the grower to supply 500 boxes, the market must offer a price of at least $18.

Key Concepts

Reading Supply Curves Carefully

A supply curve can be read in two directions, and both are useful. Read from the price up and over, it answers the question, “How much will producers offer at this price?” Read from the quantity up and over, it answers a different question: “What is the lowest price that will persuade producers to offer this much?” The second reading shows that the supply curve is also a record of the cost of the last unit produced. If a grower will not supply the 500th box unless the price is at least $18, then something about that box, such as overtime pay or picking from the least productive trees, costs about $18 to bring to market.

Notice a strange feature of the graph. Price is the cause that makes producers change their quantity, yet it sits on the vertical axis, where the effect usually goes. Economists inherited this habit from the nineteenth-century economist Alfred Marshall, and every textbook has kept it so that supply and demand curves can be drawn on the same graph in Chapter 6. Remember that the axes show the relationship. They do not tell you which way the cause runs.

Every supply curve is drawn with a long list of things held constant: the cost of inputs, the technology, the number of sellers, taxes and subsidies, and what producers expect about the future. This is the ceteris paribus assumption that you met in Chapter 1. It lets you ask a clean question, “What does a change in price alone do?” If one of the held-constant things changes, you cannot answer by sliding along the curve. You must draw a new curve, which is the subject of Section 3.

Three habits will help you read any supply graph correctly. First, check the labels and units on both axes. A price of 14 could be dollars per box or cents per pound. Second, find the time period. The same producer supplies 300 boxes per week, not 300 boxes in total. Third, ask whether the numbers are real or hypothetical. A hypothetical curve teaches a pattern, while a real data graph records what happened and is always affected by many things at once.

Key Concepts

From Individual Supply to Market Supply

As with demand, individual producers each have a supply, and the market supply is the sum of the quantities supplied by all producers at each price. Figure 5.3 shows three orange growers whose willingness to sell differs because their land, equipment, and costs differ. At $14, Grower 1 supplies 100 boxes, Grower 2 supplies 150, and Grower 3 supplies 50. The market quantity supplied at $14 is 300 boxes.

Notice two things. Grower 3 does not supply anything until the price reaches $12, because at a lower price its costs are not covered. And as the price rises, the market supply grows for two reasons: each grower supplies more, and, at higher prices, more growers join the market. When producers enter or leave, market supply increases or decreases. That is why the market supply curve is flatter than any one grower's curve in this example.

QUICK REFERENCEMarket supply is the total quantity supplied by all producers in a market at each price. It is the sum of all individual supplies. Spanish: oferta del mercado

Key Concepts

The Law of Supply in the Oil Fields

Real markets are messier than hypothetical ones, but the law of supply still shows through. Kern County is the leading oil-producing county in California, and oil is a good test because crude oil is sold at one world price that is reported every day. Figure 5.4 shows two real series. The upper graph is the average yearly price of West Texas Intermediate (WTI), a widely used benchmark for U.S. crude oil. The lower graph is the amount of crude oil produced in the United States each day, in millions of barrels.

Start with 2011 through 2014. The WTI price averaged between $93 and $98 per barrel, and U.S. production climbed from about 5.7 million barrels per day in 2011 to about 8.8 million in 2014. When the price collapsed to $48.69 in 2015 and $43.14 in 2016, production slipped from 9.4 million barrels per day to 8.9 million. Then it fell again in 2020, when the price dropped to $39.23 and production fell from 12.3 million to 11.3 million. When the price jumped to $94.79 in 2022, production rose from 11.3 million to 12.0 million barrels per day. Price and quantity supplied moved in the same direction in each of these episodes, just as the law predicts.

The data also show why economists are careful. Production kept rising in 2023, 2024, and 2025 even though the price drifted down to $65.46. A large part of the long climb after 2008 came from new drilling methods, especially horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing, that made it cheaper to get oil out of shale rock. That is a change in technology, not in price, and it moved the whole supply curve to the right. Section 3 explains the difference. For now, the lesson is that a price change leads to a change in quantity supplied, but a real graph also records everything else that happened at the same time. It also takes time for producers to respond, because drilling a new well takes months of planning and work.

Kern County's Crops Respond to Prices, Costs, and Weather

The newest Kern County Crop Report, for 2025, shows producers responding to many signals at once. The county's total gross value rose 12 percent, from $7.96 billion in 2024 to $8.93 billion in 2025. Pistachios became the top commodity, with a gross value of $1.39 billion, nearly double the $723 million they brought in a year earlier. Grapes rose to $1.38 billion, while citrus slipped from $1.31 billion to $1.19 billion. Figure 5.5 compares the three. The report counts only gross value, which is price times quantity. It does not subtract the costs of production, so it does not show profit.

Why did pistachios jump? One reason is biology. Pistachio trees tend to produce a heavy crop in one year and a lighter crop the next, a pattern called alternate bearing. The U.S. Department of Agriculture's Economic Research Service described 2024 as an off year, with a crop expected to be 26 percent smaller than the 2023 record, so part of the 2025 jump is a return to a bigger harvest. A second reason is incentives. California's bearing pistachio acreage reached about 488,000 acres in 2024, nearly five times its level two decades earlier. Growers planted those trees years ago because the price of pistachios promised a profit, and the trees take years to start producing. That delay between the price signal and the supply is a theme of Section 4.

Citrus shows the opposite direction. Its gross value fell about 9 percent. Gross value can fall because the quantity harvested fell, because the price fell, or both, and a crop report alone cannot tell you which. This is why economists ask for more data before explaining a change. The report also notes that Kern growers harvested 786,599 acres in 2025, about 4 percent fewer acres than in 2024, so the county earned more from less land. Higher prices, bigger yields on the acres still planted, and a shift toward more valuable crops can each play a part.

Key Concepts

Fixed Costs, Variable Costs, and Total Cost

Behind every supply curve is a story about cost. A producer will not offer a product unless the price covers what it costs to make. Economists divide costs into two groups.

Fixed costs are costs that do not change when the amount produced changes. A taco truck must pay for its permit, its insurance, and the monthly payment on the truck whether it sells ten tacos or a thousand. A pistachio grower pays property taxes and the loan on the land no matter how many pounds are harvested. In the short run, fixed costs are owed even if the business produces nothing.

Variable costs are costs that do change with the amount produced. Meat, tortillas, cooking fuel, and the hours of workers on the grill all rise when the truck makes more tacos. For a grower, water, fertilizer, fuel, and picking crews are variable costs. Add them together and you get total cost: total cost = fixed cost + variable cost.

Costs matter to economics because they are opportunity costs. The money, time, and land a producer uses in one way cannot be used in another. A grower who plants carrots gives up the profit that the same field could earn as pistachios. In Chapter 1 you learned that every choice has an opportunity cost. A price that covers the explicit payments but not the best alternative use of the producer's own time and land does not truly cover the cost.

Key Concepts

Explicit and Implicit Costs

Some costs arrive as bills. A grower writes checks for water, fuel, fertilizer, wages, insurance, and loan payments. Economists call these out-of-pocket payments explicit costs. Other costs never appear on a bill, yet they are just as real. Suppose a grower owns her land outright and also works in the orchard herself. She does not pay rent to herself or write herself a paycheck, but she is giving up the rent she could receive by leasing the land and the wages she could earn working somewhere else. These give-ups are implicit costs, the opportunity cost of resources the producer already owns.

Here is a hypothetical example. Elena owns 20 acres of orange trees near Arvin. In one year her farm earns $120,000 in revenue, and she pays $85,000 in explicit costs for water, labor, fuel, fertilizer, insurance, and loan interest. A bookkeeper would call the $35,000 that is left her accounting profit. But Elena could have leased her land for $10,000 a year and could have earned $40,000 as a supervisor at a packing house. Those implicit costs total $50,000. Her economic profit is revenue minus both kinds of cost: $120,000 − $85,000 − $50,000 = −$15,000. She has an accounting profit, but she would do better by leasing the land and taking the supervisor job.

This is why supply decisions depend on opportunity cost and not just on bills. Economists say that a producer needs to earn at least a normal profit, which is just enough to cover all explicit and implicit costs, to stay in the business over the long run. If the price of oranges falls so far that economic profit turns negative, producers like Elena will switch to a better use of their land and time, and the supply of oranges will fall. If the price rises so that economic profit is positive, other people will notice and enter, and supply will rise. Profit and loss are the signals that move resources from one use to another.

Costs on a Real Farm: One Acre of Almonds

Cost schedules in textbooks use small numbers, so it helps to see one built from a real farm's records. University of California Cooperative Extension and the UC Davis Department of Agricultural and Resource Economics publish “sample cost” studies that estimate what it takes to grow a crop. They describe a typical, hypothetical farm rather than a specific one, but the prices come from real markets. The 2024 study for almonds in the northern San Joaquin Valley is a useful example, because almonds are one of Kern County's leading crops. Figure 5.7 shows its estimates for one acre in one year.

The study separates costs the way this chapter does. The operating costs that change with how the orchard is run add up to about $3,807 per acre. They include $700 of irrigation water, $420 to rent two beehives to pollinate the blossoms, and the pruning, spraying, fertilizing, and harvest work. The overhead costs add up to $4,808 per acre: $739 in cash overhead such as property taxes, insurance, and office costs, plus $4,069 in non-cash overhead, which is the yearly share of the cost of the land, the trees, the pump, and the equipment. Economists treat overhead as fixed cost, because it must be paid whether the grower makes a large crop or a small one. The total comes to $8,615 per acre.

Now compare costs with revenue. The study assumes a yield of 2,200 pounds of almonds per acre sold at $1.60 a pound, which brings in $3,520. That does not even cover the $3,807 in operating costs, so the sample farm loses money in the short run. Divide by the 2,200 pounds and you get $1.73 of operating cost per pound, $2.07 of cash cost, and $3.92 of total cost, against a price of $1.60. Why would anyone grow almonds at such a price? Often growers expect prices to improve, the trees last for decades, and the land and trees have already been paid for, so those costs cannot be recovered by quitting. The next lesson shows how economists think about this choice.

Key Concepts

Diminishing Marginal Returns

Why does it cost more to produce each additional unit? Imagine the truck has one small grill. With one cook, the truck makes 20 tacos an hour. Adding a second cook allows one to prep while the other grills, and output jumps to 40. A third cook helps again, bringing output to 60. A fourth and fifth cook, however, crowd the tiny kitchen. They bump into one another, wait for the grill, and add much less. Each additional worker adds less to output than the one before.

This pattern is called diminishing marginal returns: as a producer adds more of one input (such as labor) while other inputs (such as the grill) stay fixed, the extra output from each additional unit of the input eventually falls. The extra output from one more unit of input is called marginal product. When marginal product falls, each extra taco requires more labor than the one before, and so it costs more to make.

Diminishing marginal returns is why supply curves slope upward. For the truck to produce more tacos, it must pay for more workers who each add less output. The price must be high enough to cover that rising cost, so producers will only supply larger quantities at higher prices. It also explains why a farmer who tries to grow more on the same acre eventually reaches a point where extra water or fertilizer adds almost nothing.

Key Concepts

Marginal Product and the Shape of Costs

Diminishing marginal returns is easier to see with a table. Imagine a packing shed in Wasco with a single packing line. The line cannot be expanded in the short run, but the owner can hire more packers. The extra boxes that each new packer adds to total output are called the marginal product of labor. Figure 5.8 uses hypothetical numbers. One packer can pack 30 boxes an hour. Two packers pack 70 boxes an hour, so the second adds 40. A third brings the total to 100, adding 30. The fourth adds 20, the fifth 12, the sixth 6, and the seventh only 2.

Notice that marginal product first rises and then falls. At first, extra workers allow specialization: one loads boxes, the other seals and stacks them, and together they do more than twice the work of one alone. Soon, though, the line is crowded. Workers wait for their turn, get in each other's way, and add less each time. This is the point where diminishing marginal returns sets in. In the example it begins with the third packer.

Marginal product connects directly to cost. Suppose each packer is paid $20 an hour, including payroll taxes. The third packer adds 30 boxes, so each extra box costs $20 ÷ 30 = $0.67 in labor. The fifth adds 12 boxes, so each extra box costs $20 ÷ 12 = $1.67. The seventh adds 2 boxes, so each extra box costs $20 ÷ 2 = $10. The rule is simple: marginal cost = the price of the input ÷ its marginal product. When marginal product falls, marginal cost rises, and the producer needs a higher price to supply more. This is the cost story behind the upward-sloping supply curve.

Key Concepts

Marginal Cost and Average Total Cost

Marginal cost is the extra cost of producing one more unit. You find it by dividing the change in total cost by the change in quantity: marginal cost = change in total cost ÷ change in quantity. Marginal cost connects directly to Chapter 1's idea of marginal decision making. The producer asks, “Will the price I receive for one more unit be at least as large as what that unit costs me to make?”

Average total cost is total cost divided by the quantity produced: average total cost = total cost ÷ quantity. It tells the producer the cost per unit.

Figure 5.9 uses the hypothetical taco truck. The fixed cost is $30 per hour. At 20 tacos the variable cost is $20, so the total cost is $50, and the average total cost is $50 ÷ 20 = $2.50. The marginal cost of the first 20 tacos is $1.00 per taco, because the first batch is cheap to make. The marginal cost then falls to $0.80 per taco as the cooks share work, and begins rising: $1.20, $2.00, and $3.00. Average total cost falls at first because the fixed cost is spread over more tacos, reaches a low of $1.50 at 60 tacos, and rises afterward as marginal cost climbs above it.

Two relationships are worth remembering. When marginal cost is below average total cost, average total cost falls. When marginal cost is above average total cost, average total cost rises. The marginal cost curve crosses the average total cost curve at its lowest point.

Key Concepts

Revenue, Profit, and How Much to Produce

Revenue is the money a producer receives from selling its product: revenue = price × quantity sold. Profit is what is left after all costs are paid: profit = total revenue − total cost. Profit is the reward for taking risk and organizing production. It is also the incentive that Standard 12.2.3 asks you to connect to a market economy, because producers who earn a profit stay in business and those who suffer losses must change or close.

How many tacos should the truck make? Economists suggest a simple rule based on marginal thinking: keep producing as long as the price you can get for another unit is at least as high as the marginal cost of making it. Suppose the price is $2.00 per taco. Look at Figure 5.9. Marginal cost is below $2.00 for the first 60 tacos, so each of those adds to profit. The next 20 tacos have a marginal cost of exactly $2.00, which adds nothing, and the 20 after that cost $3.00 each, so they lose money.

Figure 5.10 shows the profit. At $2.00 per taco, producing 40 tacos gives revenue of $80 and total cost of $66, a profit of $14. Producing 60 tacos gives $120 − $90 = $30. Producing 80 tacos gives $160 − $130 = $30. Producing 100 gives $200 − $190 = $10. The truck can stop at 60 or 80. This is why the marginal cost curve above the lowest point of average variable cost is the producer's supply curve: it shows how many units the producer will offer at each price.

Key Concepts

When to Keep Producing at a Loss: The Shutdown Rule

A business that loses money may still be right to stay open. The reason is that some costs must be paid no matter what. Return to the taco truck. Its fixed cost is $30 an hour. If the truck closes for the hour, it still owes that $30. So the right question is not “Do I make a profit?” but “Is staying open better than closing?” The answer depends on average variable cost, which is variable cost divided by the quantity produced. In Figure 5.11, average variable cost is lowest at $0.90 per taco when the truck makes 40 tacos.

Suppose the price is $1.40 per taco. The truck keeps producing while marginal cost is below the price, which means about 60 tacos (the marginal cost of tacos 41 to 60 is $1.20, and the next 20 cost $2.00 each). Revenue is 60 × $1.40 = $84. Total cost is $90, so the truck loses $6. If the truck shut down, it would lose the whole $30 of fixed cost. By staying open, it covers its variable cost of $60 and contributes $24 toward the fixed cost. The loss is smaller, so the truck should operate.

Now suppose the price falls to $0.85, below the lowest average variable cost of $0.90. If the truck made 40 tacos, revenue would be $34, but total cost would be $66, a loss of $32. Closing loses only the $30 fixed cost. The price does not even cover the cost of the cooks and ingredients, so every taco makes the loss bigger. The shutdown rule is: produce if the price is at least the lowest average variable cost; shut down if the price is below it. This explains a claim made earlier in this section. The part of the marginal cost curve that lies above the lowest point of average variable cost is the producer's supply curve, because at those prices the producer chooses to operate and follows marginal cost to decide how much to make.

Real businesses use the same logic. A Kern County oil producer may keep an old well pumping when the price of oil falls because the well costs little to run, but will shut it in when the price falls below the cost of electricity and labor. A grower may harvest a crop at a low price to cover the cost of picking rather than leave it on the trees. A supply curve is made of decisions like these.

Economies of Scale: Why Big Packers Often Have Lower Costs

So far the cost examples have been about the short run, in which the size of the plant is fixed. In the long run a producer can change everything: build a bigger shed, buy a second packing line, or plant more acres. Economists ask how the cost per unit changes as the whole operation grows. Figure 5.12 shows a hypothetical long-run average cost curve for a packing shed. At 10,000 boxes a year, the cost is $5.00 per box. At 60,000 boxes it falls to $3.00 per box.

When average cost falls as output grows, the producer enjoys economies of scale. There are several reasons. A large operation can spread the cost of expensive equipment, such as a cooling facility or a sorting machine, across many more boxes. Workers can specialize in narrow tasks and become faster. Large buyers get discounts on boxes, fuel, and fertilizer. In Kern County, where tractors, packing lines, and cold storage cost a great deal, these advantages help explain why many orchards and packing houses are large.

Growth does not lower cost forever. In the figure, the curve is flat between 60,000 and 80,000 boxes, which is called constant returns to scale, and then it rises. When average cost rises with output, the producer faces diseconomies of scale. The firm becomes so large that managers cannot watch everything, communication slows, and mistakes spread. A shed that tries to pack 120,000 boxes has a cost of $3.70 per box in the example. The lowest cost per unit shows the size at which the producer is most efficient, and competitors who are much smaller or much larger will find it hard to sell at the same price. Economists will return to this idea in Chapter 7 when they ask why some industries have many small firms and others have only a few giants.

Key Concepts

A Change in Quantity Supplied Versus a Change in Supply

You have already learned that two different events can change what happens in a market. The same distinction applies on the supply side. A change in the price of the product itself causes a change in the quantity supplied, a movement along one supply curve. A change in anything other than the product's own price causes a change in supply: the whole supply curve shifts.

An increase in supply shifts the curve to the right, so that producers offer more at every price. A decrease in supply shifts the curve to the left, so that producers offer less at every price. In Figure 5.13, S1 is the original supply of oranges. At $14, producers offer 300 boxes. After an increase in supply to S2, they offer 450 boxes at the same price. After a decrease to S3, they offer only 200.

Another way to think about a shift: a shift to the right means that producers would now accept a lower price for each quantity, because their costs fell. A shift to the left means they now require a higher price, because their costs rose. Costs are at the center of almost every determinant of supply.

Key Concepts

Reading Shifts Like a Detective

Questions about supply usually begin with a news story: “A freeze hits the citrus groves,” “The state raises the minimum wage,” or “Three new warehouses open on the north side of Bakersfield.” Your job is to turn the story into a statement about a supply curve. A four-step method works for almost every case.

Step 1: Name the product and the market. Supply is always supply of something, by someone, in some place. A freeze affects the supply of oranges, not the supply of pistachios. Step 2: Decide what changed. If the only change is the price of the product itself, the answer is a movement along the curve, a change in quantity supplied. If the change is in anything else (input costs, technology, taxes and subsidies, expectations, the number of sellers, the prices of related goods in production, or natural events), the answer is a shift of the curve. Step 3: Decide the direction. Anything that raises the cost of producing, or reduces the number of sellers, shifts supply left. Anything that lowers the cost or adds sellers shifts supply right. Step 4: State the result. Say which determinant changed, which way the curve shifted, and what that means: at every price, producers offer less (or more).

Try the method on the freeze. The product is oranges. The price of oranges did not cause the freeze, so this is a shift. A freeze damages trees and fruit, so growers can harvest less at every price, which makes supply decrease. The curve shifts left. Notice that the price of oranges will probably rise afterward. That is a result of the shift, not its cause. Many mistakes come from mixing up the two, so keep the story in order: first the event, then the shift, then the effect on price (which you will see in Chapter 6).

Two cautions apply. First, do not guess that every event has one effect. A new law might raise costs and also draw new sellers. Say what you know and which determinant you are looking at. Second, remember that only one thing is held constant at a time. If two determinants change in opposite directions, the net shift depends on which one is larger, and a graph alone may not tell you.

Key Concepts

Input Costs and Technology

The first determinant is the price of inputs, the resources used to make a product. Inputs include land, labor, materials, energy, and equipment. When the cost of an input rises, producing each unit becomes more expensive, so producers will supply less at every price. When input costs fall, supply increases. In California, wages, water, and fuel are major input costs for growers.

Labor is an important example. California's statewide minimum wage rose from $12.00 per hour in 2019 to $16.90 in 2026 for employers with 26 or more employees (Figure 5.15). A higher required wage raises the variable cost of every business that pays workers near the minimum. Businesses may respond by raising prices, using fewer hours of labor, adopting machines, or accepting lower profit. Economists continue to study how big each effect is, and they disagree on some of them. What the theory predicts is a shift of supply to the left for goods that use a lot of minimum-wage labor, other things equal.

The second determinant is technology, the knowledge and methods used to turn inputs into outputs. Better technology lets producers make more with the same inputs, which lowers the cost of each unit and shifts supply to the right. The mechanical tomato harvester in this chapter's Case Study, drip irrigation, and software that tells a trucking company the best route are all examples. Technology is the most important long-run reason that goods have become cheaper and more plentiful over time.

Technology improves when people find a way to make the same output with fewer resources or to make a product that did not exist before. One of the most dramatic recent examples is the solar panel. Because the cost of making each unit fell, the supply of solar power shifted to the right, and developers in sunny places such as Kern County had a reason to build far more solar farms than before. Key Concepts

Taxes, Subsidies, Expectations, and the Number of Sellers

The third determinant is the action of government. A tax on production raises the cost of making a good, so supply decreases. A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers that lowers their cost, so supply increases. Governments subsidize some farm products, energy production, and certain industries to encourage output. Standard 12.3.1 describes several roles of government in a market economy, such as addressing environmental concerns and making markets more competitive. Taxes and subsidies are two of the most direct tools government uses to carry out those roles.

The fourth determinant is producer expectations. If producers expect prices to rise in the future, they may hold goods back (store them or delay harvest) to sell later, which decreases supply today. If they expect prices to fall, they may sell more now. Expectations also affect investment: if growers expect almond prices to stay high for years, they plant more trees, and supply rises when the trees mature.

The fifth determinant is the number of sellers. When new producers enter a market, market supply shifts right. When producers leave, supply shifts left. Section 1 showed this with three growers. In a county such as Kern, where thousands of acres of orchards come into production each year, the number of sellers and the quantity supplied can change a great deal.

A sixth factor is the prices of related goods in production. A grower can plant carrots or garlic on the same field. If the price of garlic rises, growers switch some land to garlic, and the supply of carrots decreases. Goods that producers can substitute for one another in production are called substitutes in production.

Weather, Water, and Other Natural Events

A seventh determinant matters a great deal to farmers: natural events. A late frost can kill the blossoms on an orchard, a heat wave can shrink a crop, and a drought can force growers to leave land unplanted. Because these events change how much can be produced at every price, they shift the supply curve. A good harvest shifts supply right, and a bad one shifts it left. Weather is not under the producer's control, which is one reason that farming is risky.

Figure 5.17 shows total gross crop value in Kern County for three recent years. The 2023 crop was a record at $8.63 billion. Rules, Costs, and the Supply of California Oil

Government actions that change costs or the ability to produce also shift supply. The oil industry in Kern County is a useful case. What explains the gap? Economists look at the determinants. Some of Kern County's oil fields have been pumped for more than a century, and older fields give less oil per well each year, which raises the cost of each barrel. Companies compare the cost of drilling in California with the cost in places such as West Texas, and they invest where the cost is lower. Government rules also matter. The economic reasoning does not decide who is right, but it does clarify what is at stake. A rule that raises the cost of producing, or limits the number of producers, shifts supply to the left, and that shift has a price effect and a quantity effect. A decision to weigh benefits such as cleaner air against costs such as fewer jobs and a smaller local supply is a decision about trade-offs, the central idea of Chapter 1. Economists can estimate the size of the effects, while voters and lawmakers decide how much each one is worth.

Key Concepts

Prices of Related Goods: Substitutes and Joint Products

The prices of other goods that a producer could make also shift supply. Two kinds of relationships matter. The first is substitutes in production. A grower with 80 acres can plant almonds or pistachios, and a farmer can switch from cotton to alfalfa. If the price of pistachios rises, some growers who might have planted almonds plant pistachios instead, and the supply of almonds falls. A rise in the price of a substitute in production shifts the supply of the other good to the left.

The second kind is the joint product, a good that is made at the same time as another. A dairy herd in Kern County produces milk, and the same cattle eventually produce beef. A cattle ranch that sells beef also gets hides, and a refinery that makes gasoline from crude oil also produces diesel fuel and asphalt. Oil wells often bring up natural gas along with crude oil. When the price of one joint product rises and producers make more of it, the supply of the other product rises too. The two goods are tied together, so a change in the market for one changes the quantity supplied of the other.

These links explain why price changes spread across markets. When pistachio prices climbed, land, trees, and water moved toward pistachios and away from other crops. Key Concepts

Measuring How Producers Respond

Section 1 showed that producers supply more when the price rises. This section asks a sharper question: how much more? A rise in the price of tomatoes might lead growers to double their output or to add almost nothing. Price elasticity of supply measures how strongly the quantity supplied responds to a change in price. It is the percent change in quantity supplied divided by the percent change in price.

As you did with demand in Chapter 4, use the midpoint method, which gives the same answer whichever direction the change goes: elasticity of supply = (change in quantity ÷ average quantity) ÷ (change in price ÷ average price). Because the law of supply says price and quantity supplied move in the same direction, the result is a positive number.

Supply is elastic when the elasticity is greater than 1: quantity supplied changes by a larger percent than price. Supply is inelastic when the elasticity is less than 1: quantity supplied changes by a smaller percent than price. It is unit elastic when the elasticity equals 1. Figure 5.19 shows how each looks on a graph. The flatter the curve, the more elastic the supply. The steeper the curve, the more inelastic.

Key Concepts

Calculating Elasticity of Supply, Step by Step

The formula for elasticity of supply has several parts, so it helps to follow the same steps every time. Step 1: Write down the two prices and the two quantities. Step 2: Find the change in quantity and the average of the two quantities, then divide to get the percent change in quantity. Step 3: Do the same for price. Step 4: Divide the percent change in quantity by the percent change in price. Step 5: Compare the answer with 1 and name the category.

Here is a worked example with hypothetical numbers. A taquería in Delano raises the price of its burrito from $8 to $10, and the number of burritos it is willing to make each day rises from 100 to 140. The change in quantity is 40, and the average quantity is (100 + 140) ÷ 2 = 120, so quantity rose 40 ÷ 120 = 33.3 percent. The change in price is $2, and the average price is $9, so price rose 2 ÷ 9 = 22.2 percent. Elasticity = 33.3 ÷ 22.2 = 1.5. Because 1.5 is greater than 1, supply is elastic. The taquería can easily add more burritos by running the grill longer and buying a few more ingredients.

Now try an orchard with different facts. When the price of a nut crop rises from $2.00 to $2.20 per pound, growers increase the quantity they bring to market from 500 to 510 thousand pounds. Price rose 0.20 ÷ 2.10 = 9.5 percent. Quantity rose 10 ÷ 505 = 2.0 percent. Elasticity = 2.0 ÷ 9.5 = 0.21. Because it is less than 1, supply is inelastic. The trees that exist this year can only produce so much.

Four tips will prevent errors. Use percent changes, not dollar or unit changes, so that the answer does not depend on the units. Put quantity on top and price on the bottom, since supply elasticity answers the question, “How much does quantity respond to price?” Use the average of the two numbers in the denominators, which is what makes it the midpoint method. And remember that the answer for supply is always positive, because price and quantity supplied move together. A negative answer means that something was entered wrong.

Key Concepts

The Extremes and the Range Between

Elasticity of supply can take any positive value, and economists have names for the extreme cases. Supply is perfectly inelastic, with an elasticity of 0, when the quantity supplied does not change at all, whatever the price. The curve is a vertical line. A stadium with a fixed number of seats on the night of a sold-out concert is close to this case, as is the supply of a one-of-a-kind painting, or the amount of land inside a city's boundaries in the very short run. No higher price can create another seat or another acre.

Supply is perfectly elastic, with an elasticity that is infinite, when producers will supply any quantity at one price but none at a lower price. The curve is a horizontal line. No real supply is exactly this, but it is a close description of a very small producer who sells into a huge market. A single grower whose carrots are one drop in an ocean of carrots can sell all she wants at the market price, and could not sell any above it. Figure 5.20 draws both extremes.

Most real supplies lie between the extremes. A number line is a useful way to remember the ranges: 0 is perfectly inelastic, between 0 and 1 is inelastic, 1 is unit elastic, above 1 is elastic, and an infinite value is perfectly elastic. A tree crop in the short run is near the left end of the line. A taco stand or a house-cleaning service, which can add hours easily, is toward the right. When you read a news story about a market, ask yourself where on that line the supply is. The answer will tell you whether a rise in price will bring a large or a small response.

Key Concepts

Why Time Matters: Short Run and Long Run

The most important determinant of elasticity of supply is time. In the short run, producers cannot change everything. A pistachio grower with mature trees cannot make the trees grow faster. A taco truck cannot add a second grill overnight. In the long run, producers have time to plant new orchards, build new factories, buy machines, and enter or leave the industry. For that reason supply is usually more inelastic in the short run and more elastic in the long run.

Figure 5.21 uses hypothetical numbers for a tree crop to show the difference. The price doubles from $1.50 to $3.00 per pound. In the short run, quantity supplied rises only from 60 to 70 million pounds. Using the midpoint method, quantity changes by 10 ÷ 65 = 15.4 percent and price by 1.50 ÷ 2.25 = 66.7 percent, so the elasticity is about 0.23: inelastic. In the long run, growers plant more trees and the quantity supplied rises from 40 to 100 million pounds on the long-run curve. That is a change of 60 ÷ 70 = 85.7 percent, so the elasticity is about 1.29: elastic.

Other factors also matter. Supply is more elastic when the product can be stored (a seller can hold goods in a warehouse and release them when the price rises), when producers have spare capacity (unused equipment and workers), and when inputs are easy to find. Supply is more inelastic for goods that take a long time to make or that depend on a limited resource, such as land with a special climate or soil.

Key Concepts

Storage, Perishability, and Spare Capacity

Beyond time, three practical features shape how elastic supply is. The first is storage. A grower of dried goods, such as almonds or pistachios, can keep the nuts in a warehouse or silo, and so can choose when to sell. If the price rises, the grower can release some stored nuts and increase the quantity offered right away, even though the orchard cannot grow more nuts this season. That makes the supply from stored goods more elastic than the supply from the orchard alone.

The second is perishability. Fresh strawberries, lettuce, and milk spoil quickly. A strawberry grower with ripe fruit in the field must sell it within days, whatever the price. A dairy must milk its cows every day and cannot hold the milk back for a better price. These products have less ability to wait for a better price, so the quantity supplied on a given day is almost fixed, and the supply is inelastic in the short run. For perishable goods, the producer's choices are made before the harvest, in what to plant and how much to plant, rather than after it.

The third is spare capacity. A packing house that runs one shift in the slow season can add a second shift when prices rise. A trucking company with idle trucks can quickly add deliveries. When producers have idle workers and equipment, they can respond quickly, and supply is elastic. When they are already running at full capacity, as with refineries during the summer driving season, the quantity cannot rise much without new construction, and supply is inelastic. In short, elasticity of supply depends on how easily and cheaply producers can change their output, and the answer varies from product to product and from season to season.

Key Concepts

Why Elasticity of Supply Matters

Elasticity of supply helps explain why prices of some products swing wildly while others barely move. Suppose buyers suddenly want 10 percent more citrus after a news report about health benefits. If the supply of citrus is elastic, growers quickly add output and the price rises only a little. If supply is inelastic, as it is for tree crops in the short run, growers cannot add much, and the price must rise by a large amount to ration the limited supply. The same increase in demand leads to a small price change in one market and a big one in another. Chapter 6 shows this with graphs.

For producers, an inelastic supply is both a risk and a lesson. A grower who plants trees must live with the market price that happens to prevail when the trees finally bear fruit. Planting decisions are based on expectations, which can be wrong. When many growers react to the same high price by planting at the same time, supply can jump years later and prices can fall. Economists call this a boom and bust. It is one reason that farm incomes can be unsteady even when demand for food is steady.

For governments and communities, the lesson is to look at how quickly supply can respond before making promises. If a city wants more housing, it must consider that building takes years, so supply of housing is inelastic in the short run. If the state wants more electricity from solar farms, it must account for the time to permit and build them. If the country wants more workers in a certain career, it must remember that training takes years. In Chapter 9 you will see that the supply of labor has elasticity too. A policy that ignores how slowly supply responds may expect results sooner than the economy can deliver.

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · What Is Supply?
Supply is the willingness and ability of producers to offer a good at various prices. The law of supply says that, other things equal, the quantity supplied rises as the price rises, because higher prices cover higher costs and increase profit. A supply schedule and curve show this relationship, and market supply is the sum of individual supplies. Producers in Kern County respond to prices and costs.
Section 2 · What Are the Costs of Production?
Total cost is fixed cost plus variable cost. Diminishing marginal returns cause marginal cost to rise as output rises. Average total cost is total cost divided by quantity. Revenue is price times quantity, and profit is revenue minus cost. A producer increases output as long as price is at least as large as marginal cost.
Section 3 · What Factors Affect Supply?
A change in the price of the product causes a change in quantity supplied. A change in input costs, technology, taxes and subsidies, expectations, the number of sellers, or the prices of related goods in production shifts the whole supply curve (a change in supply).
Section 4 · What Is Elasticity of Supply?
Elasticity of supply is the percent change in quantity supplied divided by the percent change in price. Supply is elastic above 1, inelastic below 1, and unit elastic at 1. Supply is usually more inelastic in the short run and more elastic in the long run, and more elastic when goods can be stored or producers have spare capacity.
