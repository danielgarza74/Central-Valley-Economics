Concept Review

In Chapter 2 you learned that every economy answers the three basic economic questions, and that no modern economy stands alone: globalization connects economies through trade, investment, and technology. Chapter 3 and Unit 2 showed how prices coordinate buyers and sellers inside one country. This chapter follows that idea across borders. When a Kern County grower sells pistachios to a buyer overseas, or a Bakersfield family buys a phone made on another continent, the same forces of scarcity, opportunity cost, and incentives are at work, but two new things appear: national borders and different currencies.

Chapter 21 Key Concept

International trade is the exchange of goods and services across national borders. Countries gain from trade when each specializes in what it produces at the lowest opportunity cost, but trade also creates winners and losers inside each country, so governments debate how much to open or restrict their borders. Exchange rates and the balance of trade link a country's trade to the value of its money.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine you work a summer job at a packing house in Shafter. Almonds and pistachios roll past you on a belt, headed for shipping containers bound for ports in Asia, Europe, and the Middle East. One July, a news report announces that a foreign government has placed a new tax on the nuts you are packing. Within weeks, orders slow down, and your manager cuts your hours. At the same time, the store where you shop has raised the price of a pair of shoes, because the U.S. government has placed its own tax on shoes arriving from overseas. Is the tax on shoes good for American shoemakers? Is the foreign tax on nuts fair? Who gains and who loses? Economists, farmers, factory workers, and elected officials give different answers. By the end of this chapter you will be able to explain why countries trade, describe the tools governments use to shape trade, understand how exchange rates and trade balances work, and weigh the arguments on several sides of one of the most debated issues in American politics.

Key Concepts

Why Countries Trade

Look at the tag on your shirt, then at the phone in your pocket, then at the fruit in your lunch. Each of those items probably crossed at least one international border before it reached you. The shirt may have been sewn in Bangladesh or Vietnam from cotton grown somewhere else. The phone was probably designed in the United States, assembled in Asia, and filled with parts from a dozen countries. The strawberries or avocados may have come from Mexico in the winter and from California in the summer. Meanwhile, the almonds grown just a few miles from Shafter are very likely to be eaten by someone in India, Japan, Germany, or Canada.

This movement of goods and services across national borders is called international trade. Goods and services that a country sells to buyers in other countries are its exports. Goods and services that a country buys from sellers in other countries are its imports. Every trade has two sides: when a Shafter grower exports pistachios, a foreign buyer is importing them. The word international simply means that the buyer and the seller live in different countries. Economists use the same tools to study trade across a border that they use to study trade across a street, because the basic question is the same: why do people trade at all?

People trade because they expect to be better off. In Chapter 1 you learned that scarcity forces choices and that every choice has an opportunity cost. No person, family, or country has enough time, land, labor, and tools to produce everything it wants. A trade lets each side give up something it values less in return for something it values more. If you ever traded lunch items with a friend, or sold a video game to buy a pair of shoes, you have already seen the idea. The only difference with international trade is the scale, and the fact that the traders usually pay in different currencies.

Why do countries have different things to sell? The answer starts with the resources each country has, which are unevenly spread around the world. Climate and soil differ: the Central Valley has hot, dry summers and irrigation water from mountain snow, which suits nuts, grapes, citrus, and carrots, while Brazil's rainy climate suits coffee and sugar cane. Natural resources differ: Saudi Arabia sits on large oil reserves, and Chile has rich copper mines. Labor differs: some countries have many young workers, and others have workers with advanced technical skills. Capital differs, too, because some countries have many factories, ports, and computers and others have few. Because no country has the best of everything, each one does best by focusing on what its resources let it do well.

Economists call this focus on a narrow range of tasks or products specialization. You met specialization in Chapter 2, when Adam Smith described a pin factory in which each worker did one step. A country can specialize in the same way. When countries specialize and then trade, they become connected. The result is economic interdependence, a condition in which producers in one nation depend on producers in other nations for goods, services, and markets. Interdependence is not good or bad in itself. It brings more variety and lower prices, but it also means that events far away, such as a drought in Brazil, a labor strike at a port, or a new tax in another country, can change prices and jobs at home.

Key Concepts

Absolute and Comparative Advantage

Suppose one country is better at making everything than another country. Would it still make sense for the two to trade? Many people at first say no, and for a long time, many leaders agreed. The surprising answer is yes, and it was explained clearly in the early 1800s by an English economist you will meet in this section's Pacesetter. To follow the answer, you need two terms.

A country has an absolute advantage in producing a good if it can produce more of that good than another country with the same amount of resources, or, in other words, if it can produce the good using fewer resources. Suppose that, with one day of work, a worker in Valleyland can pick 10 crates of oranges and a worker in Harborland can pick only 4. Valleyland has an absolute advantage in oranges. Absolute advantage is easy to see because you can just compare output.

A comparative advantage is different. A country has a comparative advantage in producing a good if it can produce that good at a lower opportunity cost than another country. Remember that opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative you give up. To find a country's opportunity cost of one good, divide what it gives up by what it gains. A country can have an absolute advantage in everything, yet it cannot have a comparative advantage in everything, because making more of one good always means giving up some of the other. The country with the lower opportunity cost of a good should specialize in producing it. This idea is the law of comparative advantage.

An everyday example shows why. Imagine that you can mow a lawn in one hour and your neighbor can mow it in two hours, so you have an absolute advantage in mowing. You can also fix a bike in 30 minutes, but your neighbor needs three hours. Even though you are better at both jobs, your opportunity cost of an hour of mowing is two bike repairs, while your neighbor gives up only one-and-a-half bike repairs by mowing. So your neighbor has a comparative advantage in mowing, and you have one in bike repair. If each of you focuses where your opportunity cost is lower and then trades, you will both end up with more mowed lawns and more repaired bikes than if each tried to do everything alone.

Notice what comparative advantage does not say. It does not say that the country that is best at everything should do everything. It does not say that trade has no losers. It says that the total amount of goods available rises when each country puts its scarce resources where their opportunity cost is lowest. Economists consider this one of the most reliable ideas in the entire field. The next example makes the numbers concrete.

EXAMPLEValleyland and Harborland: A Numerical Example

The countries and numbers in this example are hypothetical, chosen to make the arithmetic clear. Two countries each have 10 workers and make two goods, crates of oranges and tablet computers. Figure 21.1 shows what one worker can make in one day. Valleyland is more productive in both goods, so it has an absolute advantage in both. But its opportunity costs are different from Harborland's.

Step 1: Find the opportunity costs. In Valleyland, 1 crate of oranges costs 5 ÷ 10 = 0.5 tablet, and 1 tablet costs 2 crates. In Harborland, 1 crate costs 1 tablet, and 1 tablet costs 1 crate. Valleyland gives up less to produce oranges (0.5 tablet, compared with 1), so it has the comparative advantage in oranges. Harborland gives up less to produce tablets (1 crate, compared with 2), so it has the comparative advantage in tablets.

Step 2: Look at output without trade. Suppose that each country puts half its workers on each good. Valleyland makes 50 crates and 25 tablets. Harborland makes 20 crates and 20 tablets. Together: 70 crates and 45 tablets.

Step 3: Move toward comparative advantage. Valleyland moves 1 worker from tablets to oranges (6 workers on oranges, 4 on tablets): 60 crates and 20 tablets. Harborland moves 2 workers from oranges to tablets (3 on oranges, 7 on tablets): 12 crates and 28 tablets. Together: 72 crates and 48 tablets. The world has 2 more crates and 3 more tablets without using any additional workers.

Step 4: Trade. Valleyland needs tablets, and Harborland needs oranges. They agree that Valleyland will send 8 crates to Harborland in return for 5 tablets. Valleyland ends with 52 crates and 25 tablets, which is 2 more crates than before. Harborland ends with 20 crates and 23 tablets, which is 3 more tablets than before. Both countries consume more than they could produce alone. The price they agreed on, 1.6 crates per tablet, is called the terms of trade. It falls between the two countries' opportunity costs (1 crate for Harborland and 2 crates for Valleyland), which is why both sides gain.

ECONOMICS PACESETTER

David RicardoThe Theory of Comparative Advantage

David Ricardo was born in London in 1772, one of many children of a stockbroker. He started work in the family's business as a teenager and became wealthy as a dealer in government bonds. After reading Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations at the age of about 27, he became fascinated with economics, and he later wrote a number of influential works on money, taxes, and trade. He also served as a member of the British Parliament.

In 1817, Ricardo published On the Principles of Political Economy and Taxation. In it, he used a simple example involving England and Portugal, two countries that produced cloth and wine. In the example, Portugal could make both goods using less labor than England, so Portugal had an absolute advantage in both. Ricardo showed that the two countries would still benefit from trade if Portugal specialized in the good in which its advantage was greatest, wine, and England specialized in the one in which its disadvantage was smallest, cloth. In other words, the gains come from comparative advantage rather than absolute advantage.

Ricardo's idea was radical because Britain at the time restricted grain imports through laws that protected landowners. Ricardo argued that these laws raised food prices and held back the rest of the economy. His argument became one of the central reasons economists later supported freer trade. At the same time, Ricardo's model made simplifying assumptions: it assumed that workers could move easily from one industry to another and that no costs were involved in changing jobs. Modern economists point out that workers who lose jobs to imports often face real costs when they try to move into other work, a topic you will study in Section 2.

Exports, Imports, and the American Economy

Now that you know why trade happens, look at how large it is. The simplest way to measure a country's involvement in trade is to compare its exports and imports with the size of its economy. Figure 21.3 does this for seven large economies in 2024, using the World Bank's measure of trade (exports plus imports) as a percentage of gross domestic product (GDP). A value above 100 percent is possible, because trade is measured on the value of all goods sold across borders, not on value added.

The pattern is striking. Smaller or more closely linked economies, such as South Korea, Germany, and Mexico, trade an amount equal to roughly 75 to 85 percent of their GDP. The United States, with the world's biggest economy and a very large domestic market, trades an amount equal to about 25 percent of its GDP. That does not mean that trade is small for the United States. Because the U.S. economy is so large, it is still the world's largest importer of goods and one of the largest exporters. In 2025, U.S. exports of goods and services were about $3.43 trillion and imports were about $4.33 trillion, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis.

Trade matters to Americans in two ways. As consumers, we gain from imports because they give us more choice and often lower prices. According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture, imports supplied about 59 percent of the fresh fruit and about 35 percent of the fresh vegetables available to Americans in 2023, up from about 50 percent and about 20 percent in 2007 (Figure 21.4). Without imports, many fruits and vegetables would disappear from supermarket shelves for months each winter. As producers and workers, we gain from exports because they give businesses more customers. The same imports that benefit consumers, however, compete with American producers, which is why trade has supporters and critics.

California and Kern County in the World Economy

California is the nation's leading state for farm output, and Kern County is one of its biggest farm counties. The California Department of Food and Agriculture reports that the state's agricultural exports were worth about $23.8 billion in 2024. The leading export was almonds, at about $4.95 billion, followed by pistachios at about $2.93 billion, dairy products at about $2.60 billion, and wine at about $1.08 billion (Figure 21.5). Almonds and pistachios are Kern County crops. According to the county's 2024 crop report, Kern County's farms produced about $7.96 billion in gross value, led by citrus ($1.31 billion), grapes ($1.18 billion), carrots ($1.01 billion), almonds (nearly $800 million), and pistachios ($723 million).

Why do Kern County growers export nuts? It is comparative advantage at work. The southern San Joaquin Valley has mild winters, long hot summers, deep soil, and irrigation water delivered by canals and wells. Growers have invested heavily in orchards, and trees take years to mature. Those same conditions are rare elsewhere in the world, so Central Valley growers can produce nuts at a lower cost than most countries. Meanwhile, the United States imports many goods that other countries can produce at a lower opportunity cost, such as clothing, electronics, and fresh fruit in the winter. Trade lets the Central Valley specialize in the crops it grows best and exchange its harvest for things it does not.

Kern County has other export links. The oil and gas produced in the county are sold on world markets, and the price of oil is set by supply and demand across the globe. Trucks and trains carry Kern County goods to the ports of Los Angeles, Long Beach, and Oakland. Goods imported through those same ports arrive in the warehouses of the Central Valley. The result is a local economy that is closely tied to what happens in the rest of the world, a topic the Case Study explores in detail.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEJapan: Trade Without Mineral Wealth

Japan has few oil, coal, or iron deposits of its own, and only a small share of its land is flat enough to farm. Yet Japan is one of the world's largest manufacturers of cars and electronics. How? Japan imports the raw materials and energy it needs, uses its skilled workers and its factories to turn them into valuable products, and sells many of the products abroad. The country's wealth does not depend on what lies under its soil. It depends on what its workers can do with imported resources.

Japan's experience shows that a country does not need a long list of natural resources to gain from trade. It needs workers with skills, capital such as machinery, and the ability to exchange its products for the things it lacks. It also shows why interdependence has risks: when a natural disaster disrupted factories in one part of Japan in 2011, car plants in other countries had to slow production because they were short of parts.

Key Concepts

The Tools of Trade Policy

In Section 1 you learned why trade can make countries better off. If that is true, why do governments ever limit it? The reason is that trade creates both winners and losers inside a country. Cheap imported shoes help every shopper, but they can hurt the workers in a shoe factory. Cheap imported tomatoes help the families who buy them, but they can hurt the farmers who grow tomatoes. Because voters and industries have different interests, governments use a set of tools to influence trade. Any law, rule, or practice that makes it harder or costlier to bring goods across a border is called a trade barrier.

The most common trade barrier is a tariff, which is a tax on imported goods. When an importer brings a shipment into the United States, a federal agency, Customs and Border Protection, collects the tariff before the goods are released. The importer usually passes some or all of the cost on to customers, so tariffs usually raise the price of the imported good. Tariffs can serve two purposes. A revenue tariff is meant to raise money for the government. A protective tariff is meant to make imports more expensive so that domestic producers can compete. For much of American history, tariffs were the federal government's main source of revenue. Today, income taxes and payroll taxes raise far more, as you will see in Chapter 18, but tariff collections still reached about $195 billion in fiscal year 2025, according to the Congressional Budget Office and the Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget.

A quota is a limit on the quantity of a good that can be imported in a given period. A country might say, for example, that only 1 million tons of imported sugar may enter each year. A quota works by restricting supply, so it usually raises the domestic price. Sometimes a country agrees to limit its own exports to avoid a stricter law abroad. That arrangement is called a voluntary export restraint. Japan agreed to limit its car exports to the United States in the 1980s, for instance. Governments can also tax or restrict trade through nontariff barriers, such as safety standards, licensing rules, and long customs procedures. Some of these rules protect consumers, and others mainly protect domestic businesses, and it can be hard to tell the difference.

A subsidy is a payment or other help that a government gives to a business or industry to lower its costs. A subsidy to farmers or manufacturers lets them sell at lower prices, in their home markets and abroad. Other countries may complain that subsidies give exporters an unfair advantage. Meanwhile, an embargo is a government order that bans trade with a particular country, usually for political or security reasons, and economic sanctions are penalties that limit trade or financial dealings with a country or group. The United States has used embargoes and sanctions many times, such as its embargo on Cuba, which began in the early 1960s.

Finally, some barriers target a practice known as dumping, which means selling a good in a foreign market at a price below its cost of production or below the price charged at home. Producers in the importing country often complain that dumping is unfair because it can drive them out of business. Trade rules allow governments to impose extra duties on dumped goods, but economists note that it can be hard to prove that a price is truly below cost, and that consumers may benefit while the low prices last.

Key Concepts

How a Tariff Works: Supply and Demand

To understand the effects of a tariff, apply what you learned in Unit 2. Figure 21.7 shows the market for work boots in a country that is open to imports. The numbers are hypothetical. Without trade, the domestic supply and demand curves would cross at a price of $60 and a quantity of 60 thousand pairs. But the world price, the price at which boots are sold on the international market, is only $40. Because imported boots cost less, the domestic price falls to $40. At that price, domestic buyers want 80 thousand pairs, but domestic producers supply only 40 thousand. The difference, 40 thousand pairs, is imported.

Now the government places a $10 tariff on each imported pair. Importers must pay $50 for each pair, because $40 plus $10 equals $50, so the domestic price rises to $50. At $50, domestic buyers purchase 70 thousand pairs, and domestic producers supply 50 thousand. Imports shrink to 20 thousand pairs. Notice the four effects. First, consumers pay a higher price and buy less. Second, domestic producers receive a higher price and produce more, so they gain. Third, the government collects revenue equal to the tariff times the quantity imported, which is $10 × 20 thousand = $200 thousand. Fourth, there is a net loss to society, shown as the two small triangles. Economists call this net loss the deadweight loss, because it is value that is lost to consumers but not gained by anyone else. Here, consumers lose $750 thousand, producers gain $450 thousand, and the government gains $200 thousand, so the net loss is $750 − $450 − $200 = $100 thousand.

This example captures why economists tend to be wary of tariffs: the losses to consumers are larger than the gains to producers and the government combined. But it also shows why tariffs are politically popular. The gains are concentrated in a small group, such as boot makers and their workers, who can easily see what they have gained and organize to ask for protection. The losses are spread among millions of buyers, each of whom loses a small amount and may not notice. Supporters of tariffs reply that the diagram leaves out things that matter, such as whether a country would have any boot factories at all without the tariff, and what happens to the workers who lose jobs when factories close. The next part explores those arguments.

Key Concepts

Arguments About Trade: Protection and Free Trade

Government policy that shields domestic producers from foreign competition through tariffs, quotas, and other barriers is called protectionism. The opposite idea, in which trade among countries is left largely free of such barriers, is free trade. In practice, no country has either one entirely. The real debate is about where to draw the line, and the people involved often disagree because their interests differ. This section sets out the main arguments on each side, so that you can judge them for yourself.

Arguments for protection. Supporters of trade barriers make several points. The first is jobs and communities. When imports surge, factories can close and workers in those industries may lose their jobs. Supporters argue that a town that loses its main employer may never recover, so government should protect industries that employ many people. The second is the infant industry argument: a new industry in a country may need temporary protection until it grows large enough to compete with experienced foreign firms. The third is national security: a country that depends on other countries for steel, medicine, computer chips, or food may be vulnerable in a crisis, so it may want to keep some production at home. The fourth is fairness, which includes the claim that foreign producers sometimes dump goods or receive large government subsidies, so tariffs level the playing field. A fifth argument is bargaining power: the threat of tariffs may push other countries to open their own markets.

Arguments for free trade. Supporters of open trade respond with their own points. The first is efficiency and living standards: because of comparative advantage, trade increases total output, and consumers get lower prices and more choices. The second is exports: if the United States restricts imports, other countries may retaliate with barriers of their own that hurt American exporters, including Kern County growers. The third is competition and innovation: foreign competition pushes domestic firms to improve. The fourth is cost per job saved: economists who study tariffs point out that protecting one job can cost consumers far more than the wages of that job, and that tariffs on inputs such as steel raise costs for other American manufacturers who use them. The fifth is that protection can become permanent, because industries rarely give up a tariff once they have it.

How strong is each argument? Economists generally agree with some points on both sides. Most agree that trade raises total national income, and that tariffs tend to raise prices for consumers. Most also agree that the national-security argument can be valid in a few cases, such as defense equipment, and that workers who lose jobs to imports often face real hardship, because moving to a new industry or town costs money and time. Where they disagree is on how large those costs are, whether tariffs are the right remedy, and how to weigh gains that are widespread but small against losses that are concentrated and severe. Those disagreements often come down to values, such as how much weight to give to the security of a particular community compared with lower prices for everyone. A good citizen, like a good economist, separates the facts that can be tested from the values on which people may reasonably differ.

Key Concepts

Trade Restrictions in the Great Depression

Today's debates have a long history. In the 1920s, the United States already had high tariffs. In the early 1930s, as the Great Depression closed factories and farms, many Americans demanded more protection. Unemployment was high, prices for crops had collapsed, and producers wanted to keep imports out so that sales would stay at home. In June 1930, Congress passed and President Herbert Hoover signed the Tariff Act of 1930, usually called the Smoot-Hawley tariff after its two sponsors, Senator Reed Smoot and Representative Willis Hawley. It raised duties on many imported goods, including many farm products.

The law did not end the Depression, and it may have made trade conditions worse. Other countries raised their own tariffs and set quotas, and world trade shrank sharply. According to the U.S. Department of State's Office of the Historian, world trade declined by about 66 percent between 1929 and 1934. U.S. exports to Europe fell from $2.34 billion in 1929 to $784 million in 1932, and imports from Europe fell from $1.33 billion to $390 million. Figure 21.9 shows that the average tariff, measured as customs duties divided by the value of all imports, rose to about 19.6 percent in 1932. Farmers, who depended on exports, suffered when foreign buyers stopped buying.

How much of this decline was caused by the tariff, and how much by the Depression itself, remains debated. The Office of the Historian notes that scholars disagree over whether Smoot-Hawley provoked foreign retaliation. Many economists believe the tariff made the downturn worse by shrinking trade, but others think that the collapse in incomes and the banking crisis were far more important causes. What most agree on is that the experience changed how American leaders thought about trade. In 1934, Congress passed the Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act, which let the president negotiate tariff reductions with other countries. After World War II, the United States helped found the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) in 1947, which led countries to reduce tariffs step by step, and the World Trade Organization replaced it in 1995. Figure 21.9 shows the result: duties fell from about 19.6 percent of imports in 1932 to about 1.4 percent by 2010.

The Great Depression also shows who tends to back protection in hard times and why. Industries that face imports, and the workers in them, usually ask for tariffs. Industries that export, and consumers who buy imports, usually ask for lower barriers. The same alignments appear in modern debates, as the next part shows.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEBritain Repeals the Corn Laws

The debate over protection is older than the United States. After the Napoleonic Wars ended in 1815, Britain passed Corn Laws that taxed or blocked imports of grain so that British landowners could keep prices high. Landowners gained, but workers and manufacturers paid more for bread. Economists such as David Ricardo argued that the laws held back the economy. After years of political struggle, Parliament repealed the Corn Laws in 1846. The repeal is often cited as a landmark in the move toward freer trade.

But the story is not simple. Critics of the repeal worried about the future of British farming, while supporters pointed to cheaper food. Historians still debate how much the repeal changed the economy, but the episode shows a pattern that repeats: producers who face foreign competition tend to defend protection, and groups that buy those products tend to oppose it.

Trade Policy Today

In recent years, tariffs have returned to the center of American politics. A series of tariff increases beginning in 2018, and a much larger set in 2025, changed the average tax on imports. According to the Yale Budget Lab, an economic research group at Yale University, the average effective tariff rate in the United States (the tariff revenue the government would collect relative to the value of imports) reached about 11 percent in spring 2026 before accounting for changes in buying habits, the highest level since 1943 apart from 2025. For comparison, the U.S. International Trade Commission's historical series shows that customs duties were only about 2.3 percent of the value of all imports in 2024.

The legal basis for many of these tariffs has changed quickly. In 2025, the president imposed broad tariffs under a law called the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA). On February 20, 2026, the Supreme Court ruled 6 to 3 in Learning Resources v. Trump that IEEPA does not authorize the president to impose tariffs, and that the tariffs based on it could not stand. A few days later, the administration imposed a temporary 10 percent tariff on most imports under a different law, Section 122 of the Trade Act of 1974, which limits such tariffs to 150 days. When that period ended, new duties took effect on July 24, 2026, under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974, covering imports from 60 trading partners at rates of 10 percent or 12.5 percent for most of them, with exemptions. Tariffs on products such as steel, aluminum, copper, and automobiles, imposed under a separate law, Section 232, stayed in place. Importers filed lawsuits challenging the new duties. This is a fast-changing area, so check current news for the latest status.

People who study these tariffs, and people who are affected by them, disagree sharply. Supporters argue that tariffs bring factories and jobs back to the United States, raise revenue, protect industries important to national security, and give the country leverage to open other countries' markets. Critics argue that tariffs are a tax paid mostly by American consumers and businesses, that they raise costs for manufacturers who use imported parts, and that retaliation hurts exporters such as farmers. Economists who model the effects generally find that tariffs raise consumer prices and reduce total output, though they differ on how large the effects are and how much the burden falls on importers, foreign producers, and consumers. Among labor leaders, business groups, and political leaders, positions vary: some manufacturers and unions favor protection for specific industries, while many retailers, farm groups, and businesses that depend on imported parts or export markets favor lower barriers.

Workers who lose jobs because of trade deserve special attention in this debate. For decades, a federal program called Trade Adjustment Assistance provided job training, income support, and relocation help to workers whose jobs were affected by trade. That program expired on June 30, 2022, and only Congress can restore it. Whatever you think about tariffs, the question of what to do for workers harmed by trade is one on which people on all sides of the debate can disagree productively.

Key Concepts

Foreign Exchange and Exchange Rates

Suppose a restaurant owner in Guadalajara, Mexico, wants to buy 1,000 pounds of California pistachios. The seller in Kern County wants to be paid in U.S. dollars, because dollars are what the grower can use to pay workers, buy fertilizer, and cover the mortgage. But the buyer has Mexican pesos. Before the sale can happen, someone must exchange pesos for dollars. That is the problem the foreign exchange market solves. The foreign exchange market, often called the forex market, is the global market in which currencies are bought and sold. It is the largest financial market in the world, and most of its trading is done electronically by banks, businesses, investors, and governments.

The price at which one currency exchanges for another is the exchange rate. On November 13, 2026, one U.S. dollar could buy about 18.57 Mexican pesos, according to Federal Reserve data. At that rate, a $10 bag of pistachios would cost the Mexican buyer about 186 pesos. If the exchange rate were instead 20 pesos to the dollar, the same $10 bag would cost 200 pesos. The seller's price in dollars would not have changed, but the buyer's cost would have risen. Because exchange rates affect the prices of every imported and exported good, they matter a great deal to businesses that trade, even when the businesses are small.

When the value of one currency rises compared with another currency, we say the currency has appreciated. When its value falls, it has depreciated. These words always compare two currencies, so they are always relative. If the dollar buys more pesos than it did before, the dollar has appreciated against the peso and the peso has depreciated against the dollar. Newspapers often say the dollar is "strong" when it appreciates and "weak" when it depreciates. The California standards ask you to understand what happens when the dollar gains or loses value, and the next parts of this section explain both causes and effects.

How are exchange rates determined? In most large economies today, the answer is the same as in any other market: by supply and demand. Under a floating exchange rate system, also called a flexible exchange rate, the value of a currency is set in the foreign exchange market by the buying and selling decisions of millions of people. When foreign buyers want more U.S. goods, services, or investments, they must buy more dollars, which raises the demand for dollars and tends to push the dollar's value up. When Americans want more foreign goods, services, or investments, they must supply dollars to buy foreign currencies, which increases the supply of dollars and tends to push the dollar's value down.

Not every country lets its currency float freely. Under a fixed exchange rate, a government sets the value of its currency in terms of another currency or of gold, and uses its reserves to defend that value. From 1944 until the early 1970s, the world's major currencies were linked to the U.S. dollar under the Bretton Woods system, and the dollar was linked to gold. In August 1971, President Richard Nixon ended the dollar's convertibility to gold, and the major currencies began to float. Some governments still manage their currencies closely. Many European countries share a single currency, the euro, which 21 countries use as of January 2026.

Key Concepts

What Moves the Dollar?

Because exchange rates depend on supply and demand, anything that changes the demand for or supply of a currency can move its value. Economists point to several main factors. The first is demand for a country's exports. If foreign customers want more California almonds or American software, they need more dollars, which pushes the dollar up. The second is demand for imports. If Americans buy more foreign cars and electronics, they supply more dollars and demand more foreign currency, which pushes the dollar down.

The third factor is interest rates and investment returns. Investors move money to where they can earn the highest safe return. If interest rates in the United States rise compared with those in other countries, foreign investors want to buy U.S. bonds and bank deposits, which increases the demand for dollars. You will study interest rates and the Federal Reserve in Chapter 20. The fourth is inflation. If prices rise faster in one country than in its trading partners, its goods become more expensive and its currency tends to lose value over time. The fifth is confidence and expectations. Currencies often rise when investors expect a country's economy to be stable and fall when they fear instability. The dollar is also a safe haven: in a global crisis, investors often buy dollars because they consider U.S. government bonds to be safe, even when the crisis started in the United States.

A sixth factor is government and central bank policy. A country's central bank may buy or sell currency or change interest rates in ways that affect its value. Trade policy can also matter. Economists debate how tariffs affect exchange rates, because a tariff may reduce the demand for imports and therefore the supply of dollars, which could push the dollar up, while retaliation and uncertainty may push in the other direction. Because many forces act at the same time, exchange rates are hard to predict, and professionals who trade currencies for a living often guess wrong.

Measuring Trade: Surpluses and Deficits

Economists summarize a country's trade with a single number called the trade balance, which is the value of its exports minus the value of its imports. If exports are greater than imports, the country has a trade surplus. If imports are greater than exports, the country has a trade deficit. For example, in 2025, the United States exported about $3,432 billion in goods and services and imported about $4,334 billion, so its trade balance was 3,432 − 4,334 = −$901 billion, a trade deficit of about $901 billion, according to the Bureau of Economic Analysis.

The deficit has two parts. The United States has a large goods deficit and a smaller services surplus. In 2025, the deficit in goods, such as cars, clothes, electronics, and fuel, was about $1,241 billion, while the surplus in services, such as travel, software, education, and financial services, was about $340 billion (1,241 − 340 ≈ 901). Services include the education that international students buy at American colleges and the vacations that foreign tourists spend in the United States, because those count as exports even though no object crosses a border.

The United States has run a trade deficit almost every year since the mid-1970s. Figure 21.13 shows U.S. exports and imports from 1990 to 2025. Both have grown, but imports have grown faster, so the gap between the two lines is the deficit. The deficit was about $81 billion in 1990 and had grown to about $716 billion by 2005. It narrowed during the 2007–2009 recession, because Americans bought fewer imports, and then widened again to about $924 billion in 2022. In 2025, the deficit was nearly unchanged from 2024, at about $901 billion, even though tariffs rose during the year. Year-to-year changes can be hard to interpret because businesses also change when they import and where they buy.

Which countries and regions account for the deficit? Figure 21.14 shows the largest U.S. goods trade deficits in 2025. The European Union, China, Mexico, Vietnam, and Taiwan were at the top. The deficit with China shrank by about $93 billion from 2024, as companies shifted purchases to other suppliers, including Vietnam and Taiwan, while the deficits with those two countries grew. The United States ran goods surpluses with some partners, such as the Netherlands ($61 billion), the United Kingdom ($32 billion), and Central and South America combined ($52 billion). Economists caution that a deficit with one country is not a measure of fairness or of who is winning, because trade patterns depend on what each country produces and on global supply chains.

Key Concepts

Is a Trade Deficit a Problem?

Many people instinctively think of a deficit as a bad thing, the way an overdrawn bank account would be. Economists are more careful, because of how trade connects to the rest of the economy. When Americans buy more than they sell abroad, foreigners end up holding dollars. They do not keep the dollars in a drawer. They use them to buy American assets, such as Treasury bonds, company stocks, real estate, and entire businesses. The records of these flows are collected in the balance of payments, which is an accounting of all of a country's transactions with the rest of the world. In simple terms, a country that buys more goods and services than it sells must make up the difference by borrowing from abroad or by selling assets abroad. A trade deficit, in other words, is matched by an inflow of foreign investment.

That connection helps explain why deficits can persist for decades. A country that saves less than it invests, because households and the government spend a lot and businesses want to build and borrow, will tend to import capital from abroad, and therefore run a trade deficit. Some economists see this as a sign of strength, since foreign investors choose to invest in the United States, and the dollar's role as the world's main reserve currency makes foreign demand for U.S. assets strong. Other economists see risks: persistent deficits mean that the country is borrowing from the rest of the world and sending interest and profits abroad, that foreign creditors could one day change their minds, and that certain industries, such as manufacturing, may shrink as imports rise.

The effect on jobs is also debated. Imports can eliminate jobs in industries that compete with them, as studies of communities hit by rapid import growth from China in the 2000s found. But imports also support jobs in transportation, retail, and in industries that use imported parts, and exports support jobs too. Overall employment depends more on the total level of spending and on conditions in the labor market than on the trade balance. Because the total number of jobs is mostly shaped by factors you will study in Chapters 16 through 20, economists usually say that the trade deficit alone is a poor guide to whether the economy is healthy.

In sum, there are two main views. One view is that a trade deficit is a sign of a problem that policy should try to shrink. The other is that a trade deficit is neither good nor bad in itself and reflects choices about saving, investing, and spending. As a student of economics, you should ask what is causing the deficit, how it is financed, and which groups are helped or hurt, instead of treating the number as a score in a game.

Key Concepts

Trade Agreements and Trading Blocs

Section 2 showed that governments can restrict trade. Governments can also negotiate to open it. A trade agreement is a treaty or other arrangement in which two or more countries agree to reduce barriers to trade among themselves. Negotiated agreements are called bilateral when two countries take part and multilateral when many do. Countries sign them because each hopes that its own exporters will gain access to bigger markets in return for allowing more imports. The agreement is a bargain, and, as with any bargain, the parties may disagree about whether they got a fair deal.

Countries can link their economies at several levels. In a free-trade area, member countries remove tariffs and most quotas on goods traded among themselves, but each member keeps its own tariffs on goods from non-members. The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA), which took effect in January 1994, created a free-trade area among the United States, Canada, and Mexico. In a customs union, members go further: besides removing barriers among themselves, they adopt a common set of tariffs on goods from outside countries. A common market adds free movement of workers and capital across borders. An economic union adds a common currency or shared economic policies. The European Union is the world's best-known example: its members form a customs union and a single market for goods, services, workers, and capital, and 21 of its members, as of January 2026, share a single currency.

Trade agreements have effects on people in the countries that sign them. Economists generally find that they increase trade among members and lower prices for consumers. But some of the new trade comes at the expense of trade with non-members, a result called trade diversion, and workers in industries that cannot compete with the other members may lose their jobs. That is why every trade agreement tends to be controversial, and why supporters and critics of each one cite different evidence, as the discussion of NAFTA later in this section shows.

Key Concepts

Trade in the Western Hemisphere

California's standards call particular attention to the main products and the changing patterns of twentieth-century trade among the countries of the Western Hemisphere, the region that includes North America, Central America, the Caribbean, and South America. The story begins with raw materials. In the early 1900s, the United States bought tropical crops and minerals from its southern neighbors, such as coffee from Brazil and Colombia, bananas from Central America, sugar from Cuba, copper from Chile, and oil from Mexico and Venezuela. In return, Latin American countries bought manufactured goods, machinery, and capital from the United States and Europe. Canada, a large neighbor with a developed economy, sold the United States wood products, paper, minerals, and wheat, and bought U.S. manufactured goods.

The Great Depression changed these patterns. As you saw in Section 2, high tariffs choked world trade in the early 1930s. Many Latin American countries, having lost the export revenue that paid for imports, decided to build their own factories so that they would no longer depend on imported manufactured goods. After the Second World War, many governments in the region adopted this approach, called import substitution, using high tariffs and other barriers to protect new domestic industries. The strategy created factories and jobs in some countries, but critics argue that protected industries became inefficient and that the region fell behind others that focused on exports. In the 1980s, many Latin American countries faced debt crises and began lowering trade barriers.

The 1990s brought regional agreements. Canada and the United States had already linked their auto industries under the Auto Pact of 1965 and signed a free-trade agreement that took effect in 1989. NAFTA then added Mexico in 1994. In South America, Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, and Uruguay formed the Southern Common Market, known as Mercosur, in 1991. Later, the United States signed trade agreements with several Central American and Caribbean countries. Economists who study NAFTA find that trade among the three countries rose from about $290 billion in 1993 to more than $1.1 trillion in 2016, and that U.S. trade with its two neighbors more than tripled. Estimates of the effect on jobs differ widely: some studies find net losses of hundreds of thousands of U.S. jobs over two decades, particularly in manufacturing, while other studies find small net effects and large gains for export industries and consumers. NAFTA also changed Mexico's farm economy, with its farm exports to the United States growing greatly while many small-scale farmers lost work.

In 2020, the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) replaced NAFTA. It raised the share of each car's parts that must be made in North America from 62.5 percent to 75 percent and added labor rules. It also set a review after six years. At the joint review on July 1, 2026, the United States declined to renew the agreement in its current form, which, under the terms of the agreement, leads to annual reviews. The agreement remains in force while the three governments keep negotiating. Today, the largest U.S. trading partners are Western Hemisphere neighbors. In 2025, two-way goods trade with Mexico reached a record $872.8 billion, with Canada $712.8 billion, and with China $414.7 billion, according to U.S. Census Bureau data (Figure 21.16).

Key Concepts

Borders, Sovereignty, and the Global Economy

The California standards also ask you to understand the changing role of international political borders and territorial sovereignty in a global economy. Two terms come first. A political border is the line that marks where one country's authority ends and another's begins. Sovereignty is a nation's supreme authority to govern itself and its territory without control by outside powers. Within its borders, a sovereign government makes laws, collects taxes, sets standards, and decides who and what may enter.

Trade policy is one of the most important ways governments exercise sovereignty. When Congress, which the Constitution gives the power to lay and collect duties, sets a tariff, or when a state inspects fruit at the border for pests, it is using sovereign authority. Customs agents at ports like Otay Mesa and the Port of Los Angeles enforce those laws. At the same time, the global economy ties countries together in ways that borders cannot fully control. Companies build products with parts from several countries, money moves across borders in seconds, and ideas spread through the internet. A decision in one country can affect jobs and prices in another.

A supply chain is the network of businesses and steps that turn raw materials into a finished product and deliver it to customers. A smartphone, for example, may have chips made in one country, screens made in another, and final assembly in a third, with the finished phones shipped through ports such as Los Angeles. Supply chains show how borders matter and how they do not. Each time a part crosses a border, it may face tariffs, inspections, and paperwork. But the chain as a whole depends on businesses in many places working together. When a port is congested or a factory closes, the effects can ripple through many industries, as happened during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Countries also give up some freedom of action when they join agreements. By signing a trade agreement, a country promises not to raise tariffs above agreed levels, to treat foreign firms fairly, and sometimes to accept a ruling from an international panel. Supporters argue that this is a good bargain, because the promises of other countries are worth more than the freedom given up, and that nations still choose to join and can leave. Critics argue that agreements can limit the ability of voters and elected leaders to control their own economy, from environmental rules to labor standards. In 2016, voters in the United Kingdom chose to leave the European Union, in part over such concerns about sovereignty, and the United Kingdom formally left the EU on January 31, 2020.

Borders also affect the movement of people. Trade in goods can substitute for the movement of workers, but many industries, such as California agriculture, rely on workers who come from or have family in other countries. Because of that, debates over trade, immigration, and border security are often connected, although economists treat them as separate questions with different tradeoffs. The 2026 Supreme Court ruling on tariffs also reminded Americans that the sovereign power to tax imports is shared among institutions within the country: the Court held that Congress had not delegated the power to impose these particular tariffs to the president under the emergency law that was used.

Key Concepts

Global Institutions That Shape Trade

Countries also work together through international organizations. The most important for trade is the World Trade Organization (WTO), based in Geneva, Switzerland, which was created in 1995 after eight years of negotiations known as the Uruguay Round, to replace the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) of 1947. As of 2026, the WTO has 166 members, which together account for about 98 percent of world trade. The WTO sets rules on tariffs and other barriers, provides a place to negotiate new agreements, and offers a way to settle disputes between members. One basic WTO principle is most-favored-nation treatment: a member that gives one trading partner a low tariff should generally give the same tariff to all members, with exceptions such as free-trade agreements.

The WTO's dispute settlement system has been under strain. Its appeals body stopped functioning in 2019 because it lacked enough judges, after U.S. administrations, beginning with the first Trump administration, blocked new appointments and argued that the body had overreached its authority. Some members have set up a separate arrangement for appeals among themselves. In recent years, some members and observers have said that U.S. tariffs and retaliation by other countries have strained the system, while the U.S. government has argued that matters of national security are for each country to decide. Even so, about 72 percent of world goods trade is still covered by the basic WTO tariff terms, according to the Congressional Research Service.

Other institutions support trade and development. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) lends money to countries that run into balance-of-payments trouble, and the World Bank funds development projects. Chapter 22 examines their work. Producer groups, such as the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC), a cartel of oil-producing countries that coordinates oil output to influence prices, also affect world trade. Kern County has a stake in the OPEC story, because oil from Kern's fields competes in a world market in which OPEC decisions can change the price. Whatever the institution, one lesson applies: the rules of trade are the outcome of negotiation, and countries and the people in them hold a range of views about how those rules should change.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEBrexit: A Border Comes Back

For nearly half a century, the United Kingdom was a member of the European Union's single market, so goods, services, workers, and capital could cross its borders with the other members without tariffs or most checks. After the 2016 referendum, the United Kingdom left the EU on January 31, 2020, and a transition period ended December 31, 2020. The two sides signed a trade agreement that avoids most tariffs on goods, but exporters now face customs forms and checks that did not exist before.

Supporters of leaving argued that the change returned authority over laws and trade policy to the British Parliament. Critics argued that it raised costs for businesses and reduced trade with the country's nearest and largest trading partners. The episode shows that decisions about borders involve both economic costs and political values such as self-government.

CASE STUDY

Tariffs, Retaliation, and
Kern County's Nuts

A tariff is a tax on imports. But when one country taxes another country's goods, the other country can respond by taxing the first country's goods. Kern County's pistachio and almond growers, who sell large amounts of their crops overseas, have found themselves in the middle of that exchange. Studying their experience shows how a trade policy can create winners and losers in unexpected places.

Background

Nuts in a Trade Dispute

Pistachios and almonds are two of the most valuable crops in the southern San Joaquin Valley. According to the California Department of Food and Agriculture, almonds were California's top agricultural export in 2024, worth about $4.95 billion, and pistachios were second at about $2.93 billion. Kern County's 2024 crop report counted about $723 million of pistachios and nearly $800 million of almonds, out of a county total of roughly $7.96 billion. Much of those harvests is sold abroad.

China has been an important buyer. Between 2018 and 2019, during the first U.S.-China trade war, the U.S. government raised tariffs on goods imported from China, and China responded with its own tariffs on many American products, including tree nuts. To help farmers hurt by the retaliation, the U.S. Department of Agriculture paid billions of dollars through the Market Facilitation Program. According to a report in the trade publication Agri-Pulse, California received less than 2 percent of the $23 billion in program payments, even though its growers sell many of the crops that were targeted.

In 2025, the dispute flared again. The U.S. government placed new tariffs on Chinese imports using an emergency law, and China placed new tariffs on American goods, including many California farm products. Researchers Colin Carter, Sandro Steinbach, and Yasin Yildirim, writing for the Giannini Foundation of Agricultural Economics, a research arm of the University of California, measured what happened. Their paper, released in February 2026, found that California's agricultural exports to China fell about 64 percent in 2025 compared with their average from 2020 to 2024. Economists caution that part of a drop like this can reflect trade diversion, in which buyers turn to other suppliers when tariffs make some goods more expensive.

This case study looks at the evidence and at the views people hold. Remember that evidence about harm does not by itself settle whether the policy was wise: supporters of the tariffs say they serve broader goals, such as rebuilding American manufacturing or pressing China to change its trade practices. Economists can measure the effects, but whether the goals are worth the costs is a question of values.

DOCUMENT C · ECONOMIC REASONINGWhy Orchards Cannot Switch Quickly (Hypothetical Example)

The numbers in this document are made up for teaching and are not from a real farm.

Suppose a grower plants 100 acres of pistachio trees. Planting, irrigation equipment, and the years of care before the trees reach full production cost the grower, say, $10,000 per acre, or $1 million in all. Once the trees are planted, that money is a sunk cost, because it has already been spent and cannot be recovered. If the price of pistachios falls because a foreign market is closed, the grower's best choice may still be to harvest and sell, because the trees are already there and a new crop would cost another large sum to plant. The grower may accept a lower price for many years rather than lose everything.

A farmer who grows vegetables each year faces a different situation. If one market closes, the farmer can switch to another crop next season. That is why economists say that growers of tree crops are especially exposed to changes in trade policy: they have committed their resources for decades, and they cannot easily adjust.

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Why Nations Trade
Countries specialize and trade because resources differ and opportunity costs differ. A country gains by specializing where its opportunity cost is lowest (comparative advantage), even if another country has an absolute advantage in every good. Exports and imports link California, Kern County, and the United States to the world.
Section 2 · Trade Barriers and Trade Policy
Governments use tariffs, quotas, subsidies, and embargoes to shape trade. A tariff raises prices, helps some producers, raises revenue, and creates a deadweight loss. Arguments about protection and free trade, from the Great Depression to today, involve both facts and values.
Section 3 · Exchange Rates and the Balance of Trade
Exchange rates are set by supply and demand for currencies. A stronger dollar makes imports cheaper and U.S. exports costlier abroad, and a weaker dollar does the reverse. A trade deficit means imports exceed exports and is matched by capital inflows, and economists disagree on whether it is a problem.
Section 4 · Trade Agreements, Borders, and Global Institutions
Free-trade areas, customs unions, and other agreements reduce barriers among members. Twentieth-century trade in the Western Hemisphere moved from raw-material exports to regional agreements such as NAFTA and the USMCA. Borders and sovereignty shape trade, and the WTO sets rules for most of the world's trade.
