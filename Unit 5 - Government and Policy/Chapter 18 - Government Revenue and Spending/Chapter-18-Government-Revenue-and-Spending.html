Concept Review

In Chapter 3 you learned that the United States has a mixed economy, one in which private individuals and businesses make most economic decisions while government plays important supporting roles. Governments enforce contracts, protect property rights, supply public goods that markets would not provide on their own, and help people who face hardship. Those jobs cost money, and in Chapter 16 you saw how the economy's size, jobs, and prices are measured. This chapter asks how governments pay for what they do and how they decide what to buy.

Chapter 18 Key Concept

A tax is a mandatory payment to a local, state, or national government. Revenue is the income a government receives from taxes and other sources. Governments raise revenue to pay for public goods, services, and transfer payments, and every tax and every spending program has both benefits and costs that citizens must weigh.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine that you take your first job, working fifteen hours a week at a taquería in Shafter for what is, in this hypothetical, $17 an hour. You expect a paycheck of $255. Instead, the stub shows a smaller number, and the missing money has labels you do not recognize. Where did it go? Some of it pays for the Social Security benefits of a retired farmworker and for the Medicare coverage of a grandparent. Some of it may pay for the highway you drove to work, the school where you study, and the firefighters who would answer if the kitchen caught fire. Meanwhile, the owner of the taquería pays taxes too: a sales tax collected from every customer, a property tax on the building, and an income tax on whatever profit is left. Taxes touch everyone, and by the end of this chapter you will be able to explain what kinds of taxes exist, who ultimately pays them, where the money is spent, and how to judge whether a tax or a program is worth its cost.

Case Study Preview

When you order a pair of shoes from a website, should the seller charge you the same sales tax that a shoe store on Bakersfield's Ming Avenue must charge? For many years, the answer was often no. In 2018, the Supreme Court changed the rules. At the end of this chapter, you will examine the debate over taxing online sales and decide how to weigh fairness to local stores, convenience for shoppers, and the burden on small sellers.

Key Concepts

Why Governments Collect Taxes

Think about everything that was paid for before you got to school this morning. A city crew maintained the street you crossed. A county employee tested the water that came out of the tap. A traffic signal, paid for with public money, changed to green. Public school funding paid for the bus, the building, and the teacher. Nobody sent you a bill for any of it. These services are funded by a tax, a mandatory payment to a local, state, or national government. The word mandatory matters. A person who buys a taco from a taco truck chooses to pay for it. A person who owes a tax must pay it whether or not he or she wants the particular services the government will buy with it, and the law can punish those who refuse.

The money that governments take in is called revenue. Taxes are the most important source of government revenue, but they are not the only one. Governments also collect fees for services such as driver's licenses and national park entrances, earn income from public property, and receive grants from other levels of government. When they spend more than they collect, they borrow by selling bonds, a topic you will explore in Chapter 19. The power to tax is written into the U.S. Constitution and into every state constitution. Article I gives Congress the power "to lay and collect taxes," and the Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, confirmed that Congress may tax incomes.

Why are taxes needed at all in an economy built mostly on private choices? In Chapter 3 you learned that markets do many jobs well, but some jobs are better done collectively. California's standard for this course asks you to understand that the role of government in a market economy often includes providing for national defense, addressing environmental concerns, defining and enforcing property rights, making markets more competitive, and protecting consumers' rights. Each of these jobs has a price tag. A national defense protects everyone at once, and a single family cannot buy its own share of an army. Courts cannot enforce contracts, and police cannot protect property, unless someone pays the judges and officers. Because many people would prefer to let their neighbors pay for these goods and enjoy them free, a society uses compulsory taxes to make sure that the bill is covered.

Taxes also pay for transfer payments, which move money from one group to another without anything being produced in exchange. Social Security checks, unemployment benefits, and food assistance are examples. Governments also use taxes for purposes beyond raising money, such as encouraging home ownership or discouraging smoking. Throughout this chapter you will see that every tax has a purpose, a burden, and a side effect. As you study each one, remember the question that this course's standard asks: when might the costs of a government action outweigh its benefits?

Key Concepts

Principles of Taxation

People argue about taxes constantly, and the arguments usually come down to a few big ideas about what makes a tax good. Economists and philosophers have proposed several principles for judging taxes. None of these principles settles every debate, and thoughtful people weigh them differently, which is one reason tax policy is among the most contested subjects in American politics. Learning the principles will help you evaluate proposals from any direction.

The first principle is benefits received. It says that people who benefit from a government service should pay for it in proportion to the benefit they receive. A familiar example is the gasoline tax. Drivers pay it at the pump, and the money pays for roads, so the people who use the roads most (and wear them out most) pay the most. Tolls and entrance fees to state parks work the same way. The benefits-received principle is easy to defend where the users can be identified, but it is hard to apply to services such as national defense, where everyone benefits and no one can be charged separately. It also has a drawback for programs that help the poor. If only people who receive the benefit pay for it, those who need the most help would have to pay for their own assistance.

The second principle is ability to pay. It says that people with greater incomes or wealth should pay more, because they can give up some dollars without giving up necessities. A family with $25,000 a year must use almost all of it for food, shelter, and transportation, while a family with $250,000 a year can pay a larger tax and still afford those basics. Supporters argue that this approach is fair. Critics reply that it can punish success, reduce the reward for working and investing, and place most of the burden on a small number of taxpayers. Both sides appeal to fairness; they simply define it differently.

Beyond these two ideas, economists use several practical tests. A good tax should be simple, so taxpayers can understand what they owe without hiring an expert. It should be certain, so people know when and how much to pay. It should be efficient, meaning that it raises revenue without causing people to change their behavior in wasteful ways and without costing too much to collect. And it should be fair, though people disagree about what fair means. Economists distinguish horizontal equity, the idea that people in similar situations should pay similar taxes, from vertical equity, the idea that people in different situations should pay different amounts. More than two centuries ago, Adam Smith, whom you met in Chapter 2, proposed similar tests: a good tax should be fair, certain, convenient to pay, and cheap to collect.

The Tax Base

Every tax is applied to something. The thing being taxed is called the tax base. For an income tax, the base is income; for a sales tax, it is the dollar value of purchases; for a property tax, it is the value of land and buildings. The size of the base matters as much as the rate. A small tax rate on a very large base can raise more money than a large rate on a narrow base. Imagine a 1 percent tax on all the groceries sold in a county compared with a 10 percent tax on only the diamonds sold in the county. The grocery tax has a rate ten times smaller, but the base is so much larger that it would almost certainly collect more.

Governments tend to prefer broad bases, because they raise more revenue with lower rates and are harder to avoid. When lawmakers carve out exceptions, such as exempting certain goods or income, the base shrinks, and rates must rise to collect the same amount. In California, for example, most groceries that you take home to cook are exempt from the state sales tax, while hot prepared food is taxable. That exemption keeps the tax from falling heavily on a basic necessity, but it also means the tax applies to a narrower set of purchases.

Key Concepts

Tax Structures

Taxes differ in how the amount owed changes as the taxpayer's income changes. Economists describe three structures. Under a proportional tax, everyone pays the same percentage of the tax base regardless of income. Under a progressive tax, the percentage rises as income rises. Under a regressive tax, the percentage falls as income rises. The key word in each definition is percentage. A flat percentage tax still takes more dollars from a richer person, but it takes the same share.

A proportional tax is sometimes called a flat tax. If a state taxed every resident 5 percent of income, someone earning $20,000 would pay $1,000 and someone earning $100,000 would pay $5,000. The rich person pays five times as many dollars, but both pay one-twentieth of their income. A few states do apply a single income tax rate to all residents, and some proposals for reforming the federal tax code would do the same.

A progressive tax is built on the ability-to-pay principle. The federal individual income tax is the best-known example. It applies increasing rates to successive slices of income, so a taxpayer with a higher income pays a larger percentage of that income overall. California's state income tax is also progressive. Supporters say progressive taxes spread the burden according to the ability to pay. Opponents argue that high rates on additional income discourage work, saving, and investment, and that wealthier people have more ways to shift income to avoid higher rates.

A regressive tax takes a larger share of income from people with lower incomes. The sales tax is the common example, even though everyone pays the same rate at the register. The reason is that lower-income families spend most of what they earn, while higher-income families save or invest more of theirs, so the sales tax claims a bigger fraction of a low income. Excise taxes on gasoline and on cigarettes tend to be regressive for the same reason. Figure 18.1 compares the three structures using hypothetical numbers.

EXAMPLEThree Taxes on One Taqueria Lunch

On a Saturday, Ana, a high school junior who earns $17 an hour at a taqueria in Shafter, buys lunch for $12 at the counter. Hot prepared food is taxable in California, so at Shafter's combined 7.25 percent rate she pays about 87 cents in sales tax. The taqueria owner collects the tax at the register and sends it to the state. Down the road, the building's owner pays a property tax on the land and building, and the owner of the taqueria pays an income tax on whatever profit is left after expenses. Ana's weekly paycheck, meanwhile, has Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes withheld. In one day, one lunch and one paycheck have passed through four different taxes collected by three levels of government.

Notice how the tax changes the price. The lunch costs $12.00 on the menu board, but Ana's total is $12.87. Because the tax is added to the sticker price, consumers sometimes forget that it exists, while the business must keep track of every cent.

Key Concepts

Common Types of Taxes

Most of the taxes you will meet fall into a few families. The individual income tax is a tax on the income of individuals, such as wages, salaries, and interest. It is the largest single source of federal revenue, and 41 states also collect an income tax of their own, including California. The corporate income tax is a tax on the profits of corporations. Because a corporation is a legal entity separate from its owners, the profits it earns are taxed at the corporate level, and many owners are taxed again when they receive dividends or sell stock. You will study this tax in Section 2.

A sales tax is a tax on purchases of goods and services, collected by the seller at the time of sale. In California, the state, the county, and cities or special districts may each add to the rate. The property tax is a tax on the value of real estate and, in some places, other property, and it is the main source of revenue for local governments such as school districts, cities, and counties. Other families of taxes include excise taxes, which apply to specific products such as gasoline, tobacco, and alcohol, and tariffs (also called customs duties), which apply to imported goods. A few taxes, such as the federal estate tax, apply when wealth is passed from one generation to the next.

Different levels of government rely on different taxes. The federal government depends mostly on income and payroll taxes. State governments depend on income and sales taxes. Local governments depend on property taxes. This division of responsibility, together with grants that flow from federal to state to local governments, makes American government finance complicated, but it gives each level a source of money suited to its needs.

Key Concepts

The Incidence of a Tax

Who really pays a tax? The obvious answer is the person who writes the check. But the incidence of a tax, which is the final burden of a tax, can fall on people who never send a payment to the government. Suppose the government places a $3 tax on each unit of a product and requires the seller to send the money. The seller may raise the price to cover the tax. If buyers pay a higher price, they bear part of the tax even though the seller handed over the money. Economists therefore separate the legal incidence, who is required by law to pay, from the economic incidence, who actually loses purchasing power because of the tax.

To see how the burden is divided, use the supply-and-demand model from Unit 2. A tax on sellers acts like an increase in their costs. At every quantity, sellers need a price that is $3 higher to be willing to supply the same number of units, so the supply curve shifts upward by the amount of the tax. The new equilibrium has a higher price for buyers and a lower quantity. Sellers also end up receiving less than the new price after they pay the tax. The buyer's extra price and the seller's lost revenue add up to the tax.

How the burden is divided depends on the elasticity of demand and supply, the sensitivity of buyers and sellers to price. If buyers have few alternatives, such as drivers who must buy gasoline to commute from Shafter to Bakersfield, demand is inelastic, and buyers bear most of the tax. If buyers can easily switch, the seller must absorb most of it or lose customers. Figure 18.2 shows the two cases with hypothetical numbers. The same logic helps explain why governments often tax products with inelastic demand, such as gasoline and cigarettes: the quantity sold falls only a little, so the tax raises reliable revenue.

Key Concepts

How Taxes Affect the Economy

Taxes do more than move money from taxpayers to the government. They change what people and businesses choose to do, because they change the rewards for working, saving, spending, and investing. A tax on cigarettes makes smoking more expensive, so some people smoke less. A tax on wages lowers the reward for an extra hour of work. A tax on corporate profits changes how much businesses are willing to invest. In each case, the tax shifts incentives, the rewards and penalties that guide decisions.

Governments often use that power deliberately by offering a tax incentive, a feature of the tax law that rewards a particular behavior by reducing the tax owed. Examples include tax credits for buying energy-efficient appliances, deductions for interest on a home mortgage, and credits for businesses that hire workers from certain groups. A deduction lowers the income that is taxed; a credit reduces the tax itself, dollar for dollar. A $1,000 credit is therefore worth more to a taxpayer than a $1,000 deduction, which saves only the taxpayer's tax rate times $1,000. Supporters of tax incentives say that they can steer private choices toward goals that markets overlook, such as clean energy. Critics say that they complicate the tax code, mostly help people who already planned to act, and let lawmakers pick winners and losers.

California's standard for this course asks you to identify the factors that may cause the costs of government actions to outweigh the benefits. Taxes provide a clear lens for that question. First, a tax may cause deadweight loss. When a tax raises the price of a good, some trades that would have benefited both buyer and seller no longer happen. The lost gains from those missing trades are a cost to society that does not show up as revenue for anyone. Second, there are compliance costs, the time and money taxpayers and businesses spend to figure out and report what they owe. A taqueria owner who hires an accountant is paying a cost of the tax system. Third, there are administrative costs, the expense of the agencies that collect taxes and enforce the rules. Fourth, taxes may lead to avoidance and evasion. Avoidance means legally arranging affairs to owe less, while evasion means breaking the law. Fifth, a poorly designed tax may reduce the benefits of a program it was meant to support by discouraging the very activity that generates revenue.

None of this means that taxes are bad. A government cannot supply defense, courts, or roads without revenue, and the benefits of those services can far exceed what taxpayers pay. The question is never tax or no tax. It is whether a particular tax raises needed revenue at a reasonable cost, and whether the spending it supports is worth the burden. Economists and citizens disagree about where the line falls, which is why Section 3 and Section 4 examine spending as well as taxes.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVEOne Idea Most Countries Use: The Value-Added Tax

The United States collects no national sales tax or value-added tax (VAT), but most of the world's other wealthy countries do. A VAT is collected in small pieces at each stage of production, from the farmer to the miller to the baker to the store, and the final consumer bears the burden. Because each business subtracts the VAT it already paid on its inputs, the tax is collected all along the chain, which makes it harder to evade. Many countries use a VAT because it raises a large amount of revenue with a broad base.

Tax levels also vary. In 2023, tax revenue at all levels of government was equal to about 25.2 percent of the U.S. economy's output, compared with an average of 33.9 percent among the countries of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), a group of mostly wealthy democracies. Supporters of larger public programs point to that gap as evidence that the United States could afford to do more. Critics of larger government point to it as a reason for the strength of American private investment. The numbers describe a fact, and people disagree about what the fact means.

Key Concepts

The Individual Income Tax

The individual income tax is the single largest source of federal revenue. In the federal budget year that ended on September 30, 2026, individual income taxes brought in about $2.8 trillion, or a little more than half of all federal revenue. The tax is also the one most people experience directly, because it comes out of nearly every paycheck. Understanding how it works takes four steps: earn income, have tax withheld, compute what you owe, and file a return.

When you take a job, you complete a form that tells your employer how much federal income tax to subtract from each paycheck. This system is called withholding: the employer deducts an estimated amount of tax from your pay and sends it directly to the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), the federal agency that collects taxes. Withholding began on a large scale during World War II, when the government needed money fast and wanted to avoid the risk that taxpayers would spend their wages and be unable to pay a huge bill the following spring. Today it spreads the payment of the tax across the year. Workers who are self-employed, such as many farm owners, contractors, and delivery drivers, do not have an employer who withholds tax. They must estimate their taxes and send payments to the IRS every quarter.

After the year ends, each taxpayer determines the amount actually owed. The starting point is total income from wages, interest, business profits, and similar sources. The taxpayer then subtracts amounts the law allows, called deductions. Most taxpayers take the standard deduction, a fixed amount that depends on filing status. For the 2026 tax year, the standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer, $24,150 for a head of household, and $32,200 for a married couple filing jointly. What is left after deductions is called taxable income, and it is the number to which the tax rates apply.

By April 15 of the following year, taxpayers file a tax return, a form that reports income, deductions, and the tax owed, and compares the tax owed with the amount already withheld or paid. If more was withheld than owed, the taxpayer receives a refund. If less was withheld, the taxpayer must pay the difference. A refund is not a gift from the government, since it simply returns the taxpayer's own money after the year's final calculation. A large refund means the taxpayer lent the government money interest-free all year.

Taxpayers can also subtract tax credits after the tax is calculated. A credit reduces the tax owed dollar for dollar. Some credits are refundable, meaning that if the credit is greater than the tax owed, the taxpayer receives the difference as a payment. The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) is the best-known example. It supplements the earnings of low- and moderate-income workers, and for 2026 its maximum is $8,231 for a worker with three or more qualifying children. Supporters describe the EITC as a way to reward work and reduce poverty. Critics say it is complicated and sometimes claimed in error. Both agree that it is one of the largest federal programs aimed at working families.

Because most taxpayers have wages with withholding, the task of filing is mainly a matter of checking the numbers. Many people in Kern County, including students with part-time jobs, file a return even if they owe nothing, because it is the only way to get back any tax that was withheld.

Key Concepts

Brackets: Marginal and Average Rates

The federal individual income tax is progressive. It achieves this with tax brackets, ranges of taxable income that are each taxed at a different rate. The rate on the lowest bracket is 10 percent, and the rates rise through 12, 22, 24, 32, 35, and 37 percent. The most common misunderstanding about brackets is the belief that moving into a higher bracket makes all of your income taxed at the higher rate. That is not how brackets work. Only the income inside a bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate.

Economists use two measures to describe a taxpayer's rate. The marginal tax rate is the rate paid on the last (or next) dollar of income. It tells you how much of an additional $100 you would keep. The average tax rate is total tax divided by total income. Because lower slices of income are taxed at lower rates, the average rate is always below the marginal rate for taxpayers above the first bracket.

Figure 18.3 shows the 2026 brackets for a single filer. Suppose a single worker named Maya has taxable income of $35,900 (wages of $52,000 minus the $16,100 standard deduction). The first $12,400 is taxed at 10 percent, which is $1,240. The remaining $23,500 falls in the 12 percent bracket, which is $2,820. Her total income tax is $4,060. Her marginal rate is 12 percent, but her average rate is only $4,060 ÷ $35,900, or about 11.3 percent of her taxable income, and just 7.8 percent of her total wages. If she earned $1,000 more, she would pay $120 more in tax and keep $880.

Key Concepts

Social Insurance Taxes: FICA

If you have ever looked at a pay stub, you probably noticed lines labeled "FICA," "Social Security," and "Medicare." These come from the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA), a law that created payroll taxes to fund two major social insurance programs. Social Security provides monthly benefits to retired workers, people with disabilities, and the families of workers who have died. Medicare provides health insurance to people 65 and older and to some younger people with disabilities.

The rates are set by law. Employees pay 6.2 percent of their wages for Social Security and 1.45 percent for Medicare, a total of 7.65 percent. The employer pays an equal amount, so the total contributed on behalf of each worker is 15.3 percent of wages. Self-employed workers pay both halves themselves. The Social Security portion applies only to wages up to a limit called the taxable maximum, set at $184,500 for 2026. Earnings above that amount are not subject to the 6.2 percent tax, though the Medicare tax applies to all earnings. Because of this cap, the Social Security tax takes a smaller share of income from very high earners, which makes that portion regressive at the very top, even though benefits are calculated in a way that favors lower earners.

Payroll taxes work differently from the income tax in one important way: they are tied to benefits. The money goes into trust funds, and workers earn the right to benefits based on their work history. In 2026, the Social Security trustees reported that the trust fund for retirement benefits is projected to be depleted in the last quarter of 2032, at which point incoming payroll taxes would cover about 78 percent of scheduled benefits. For the retirement and disability funds combined, depletion is projected in 2034, at which point about 83 percent of scheduled benefits could be paid. These are projections, not certainties. They mean that Congress will face choices about raising revenue, changing benefits, or both. Different groups favor different combinations, which is why the question is so debated.

Employers also pay a federal and state unemployment insurance tax, which funds benefits for workers who lose their jobs through no fault of their own. Unlike the FICA taxes, it is paid by the employer and not withheld from the worker's pay.

Key Concepts

Corporate Income Taxes

Corporations also pay federal income taxes. The corporate income tax is a tax on a corporation's profits, which are its revenue minus its costs. Since 2018, the federal tax rate on corporate profits has been a flat 21 percent. States may tax corporate income as well. California, for example, imposes its own corporation tax.

Two ideas help you think about this tax. The first is double taxation. A corporation pays tax on its profits, and then a shareholder who receives part of those profits as dividends pays individual income tax on them too. Some economists think this discourages investment in corporations, while others argue that corporations enjoy special privileges, such as limited liability, that justify the tax. The second idea is incidence. A corporation is a legal entity, not a person, so the burden must ultimately fall on people: shareholders through lower returns, workers through lower wages, or customers through higher prices. Economists disagree about the division of the burden, and studies reach different conclusions.

Corporate tax revenue is volatile. In the 2026 budget year, it fell to about $381 billion from $452 billion a year earlier, according to the Congressional Budget Office. CBO attributed the drop to the scheduled decline in payments of a one-time tax on previously untaxed foreign profits and to a 2025 law that let businesses deduct more of certain investments immediately. The example shows how tax law changes, and not just business profits, can move revenue.

Key Concepts

Excise Taxes, Tariffs, and Estate and Gift Taxes

An excise tax is a tax on a particular product. The federal government taxes gasoline at 18.4 cents per gallon, a rate unchanged since 1993, along with diesel fuel, tobacco, alcohol, and airline tickets. California adds its own, more than 63 cents per gallon in 2026, which funds road repair. Excise taxes are easy to collect and raise a stable amount of revenue because the quantity demanded of items like gasoline changes little when prices rise. Some excise taxes are intended to make people pay the costs they impose on others, such as the pollution from burning fuel or the health costs of tobacco, and economists call this approach taxing an externality.

A tariff, or customs duty, is a tax on goods imported from other countries. The United States collected about $173 billion in customs duties in the 2026 budget year, down from $195 billion the year before. CBO reported that the decline followed a Supreme Court decision in February 2026 that led to refunds of certain tariffs imposed in 2025, with about $126 billion refunded in duties and interest. Tariffs raise revenue, but they are also a tool of trade policy, which you will study in Chapter 21. Supporters say tariffs protect domestic industries and bargain with other countries. Critics say they raise prices for consumers and invite retaliation, harming exporters such as Central Valley farmers.

Finally, the federal government taxes wealth when it is transferred. The estate tax applies to the property of a person who has died, but only above a high exemption: for people who die in 2026, the first $15 million of an estate is excluded. The gift tax applies to large gifts made during a person's life, with an annual exclusion of $19,000 per recipient in 2026. Very few estates owe the tax, and it raises a small share of revenue. Supporters see it as a way to limit the concentration of inherited wealth. Opponents argue that it penalizes family farms and small businesses and taxes money that has already been taxed once. Because many Kern County farms are family-owned, this debate has special meaning in the Central Valley.

Key Concepts

Two Kinds of Federal Spending

In the budget year that ended on September 30, 2026, the federal government spent about $7.4 trillion. That is a hard number to imagine. If you spent one dollar every second, it would take nearly 32,000 years to spend one trillion dollars, and the government spends more than seven times that much in a single year. To make sense of it, economists sort federal spending into a few categories. The most important distinction is between spending that Congress decides on every year and spending that continues automatically under existing law.

Mandatory spending is spending required by laws that set eligibility rules and benefit formulas. Congress does not vote on this spending each year. Instead, anyone who meets the conditions in the law receives the benefit, and total costs rise or fall with the number of eligible people and the cost of their care. Most mandatory spending goes to entitlements, programs that give eligible people a legal right to benefits. Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid are the largest. In fiscal year 2025, the Congressional Budget Office (CBO) reported that mandatory spending totaled about $4.2 trillion, roughly 60 percent of all federal outlays.

Discretionary spending is spending that lawmakers control through annual appropriations, bills that give federal agencies permission to spend money. It pays for national defense, federal law enforcement, highways, scientific research, education grants, and the salaries of most federal workers. In 2025, discretionary spending was about $1.9 trillion, or roughly 27 percent of the total, of which about $893 billion was for defense and about $980 billion was for everything else. A third category, net interest, is the interest the government pays on the money it has borrowed, minus interest it receives. Net interest was about $1.0 trillion in 2025 and rose to about $1.1 trillion in 2026. Chapter 19 explains how borrowing builds up that interest.

Figure 18.7 shows how the 2026 total divides among the largest categories. The five largest items, Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, defense, and net interest, together account for about three-quarters of all spending. Notice that net interest is now larger than spending on national defense and is the fastest growing of the major categories. When you read about whether the budget is under control, much of the debate concerns these large items, because small programs cannot add up to the total, whatever their merits.

The Big Programs

Social Security is the largest single program. In fiscal year 2026, it cost about $1.65 trillion. It pays monthly benefits to retired workers, to people with disabilities, and to survivors, and the amount depends on a worker's earnings history. For many older Americans, including thousands of retired farmworkers and oil workers in Kern County, it is the main source of income. Both programs are funded mainly by the payroll taxes you studied in Section 2, but their costs have been rising faster than those revenues as the population ages.

Medicaid is a joint federal-state health insurance program for low-income people, including children, pregnant women, people with disabilities, and many older adults who need long-term care. The federal government pays a share of each state's costs, and states manage the program. In California the program is called Medi-Cal. Federal spending on Medicaid reached about $723 billion in 2026, up from $668 billion a year earlier, as cost per enrollee rose. Because Medicaid reaches a large share of children in the Central Valley, changes in the program have direct local effects, and lawmakers and states debate how much it should cost and how it should be run.

National defense is the largest part of discretionary spending, at about $917 billion in 2026. It pays for military personnel, weapons, research, and operations, and it supplies the public good of national security. Other federal spending includes veterans' benefits, nutrition assistance programs, highways and transit, scientific research, education grants, border and law enforcement, and the costs of running the government itself. The category the CBO labels "all other" amounted to roughly $1.9 trillion in 2026 when all of these are added together.

Transfer payments, a term from the opening of this section, are payments that move money from one person or group to another without any good or service being exchanged. Most of the big programs are transfers. When the government pays a retiree a Social Security benefit, the retiree does not provide a product in return. In contrast, when the government buys a ship or pays a teacher in a federal program, it is purchasing a good or service. Economists call that government purchases. Counting both is essential because they affect the economy differently, a point developed in Chapter 19.

Alice RivlinCounting the Costs, for Everyone

Before 1974, Congress had a problem. The President's budget office could tell lawmakers what the administration's plans would cost, but Congress had no independent staff of its own to check the math. When Congress passed the Congressional Budget and Impoundment Control Act of 1974, it created budget committees in the House and Senate and a new agency, the Congressional Budget Office (CBO), to give lawmakers of both parties neutral estimates. In 1975, it chose an economist named Alice Rivlin to be the agency's first director.

Rivlin had studied economics at Bryn Mawr College and earned her Ph.D. at Radcliffe College. She worked at the Brookings Institution, a research organization in Washington, and in government. At the CBO, she helped establish a simple rule: the agency would not take a side in a policy fight. It would estimate what laws would cost and what the economy would probably do, and let elected leaders decide. That reputation for nonpartisanship became the agency's most valuable asset. Its cost estimates for proposed laws, called "scores," still guide debates in both parties today.

Later, Rivlin served as President Clinton's budget director, helping negotiate deals to reduce the deficit in the 1990s, and then as a vice chair of the Federal Reserve Board. After leaving government, she worked with Republican Senator Pete Domenici of New Mexico on a bipartisan plan to reduce the federal debt, an example of how people with different views can still look for shared numbers. Her colleagues in both parties remembered her as a clear, plain-spoken explainer of the budget.

Rivlin's career illustrates two ideas from this section. First, a budget is a set of choices in a world of scarcity, and every dollar spent has an opportunity cost. Second, good decisions depend on trustworthy information. Her work helped make sure that Congress, voters, and economists argue over the same facts.

Key Concepts

How the Federal Budget Is Made

The federal budget is the government's plan for how to raise and spend money. It is organized around a fiscal year, a twelve-month accounting period that may begin on any date. The federal fiscal year runs from October 1 to September 30, and it is named for the year in which it ends. The year that began on October 1, 2025, and ended on September 30, 2026, was fiscal year 2026. Students are often surprised that the government's year is not the calendar year, but a separate fiscal year helps Congress organize its work around the budget.

The process has several steps, shown in Figure 18.9. By law, the President sends Congress a budget proposal in early February, about eight months before the fiscal year begins. It is based on the work of the Office of Management and Budget (OMB) and the many agencies of the executive branch. Congress then takes over. The House and Senate Budget Committees, with estimates from the CBO, draft a budget resolution that sets overall targets. The Appropriations Committees divide the discretionary money among twelve subcommittees, and each subcommittee writes a bill for the agencies it oversees, such as defense or transportation. Both chambers must pass each bill, and the President must sign it, or veto it, in which case Congress can override with a two-thirds vote of each chamber.

In practice, the schedule is often missed. When Congress has not passed all the appropriations bills by October 1, it can pass a continuing resolution, a temporary measure that keeps agencies funded at last year's level. If Congress and the President fail to agree even on a temporary measure, a funding gap occurs, and many agencies must stop nonessential work, an event called a government shutdown. The most recent was the longest in U.S. history. It began on October 1, 2025, and ended on November 12, 2025, after 43 days. During a shutdown, hundreds of thousands of federal employees may be furloughed or required to work without pay until it ends, and some services pause.

Once appropriations pass, money is not simply handed out. Federal agencies spend funds under rules set by Congress, and the Treasury Department issues the payments, most of them electronically. Mandatory programs follow a different path: payments flow automatically to eligible people under the laws that created the programs. Final decisions on spending therefore take place on two tracks, one a yearly review and one a long-term legal commitment.

Key Concepts

When Costs Can Outweigh Benefits

California's economics standards ask you to identify the factors that may cause the costs of government actions to outweigh the benefits. This is a good question to ask about any federal program, and economists have developed a set of tools for answering it. The simplest tool is cost-benefit analysis: add up the value of everything a project is expected to produce, add up everything it costs, including what the resources could have produced elsewhere (the opportunity cost), and compare. A project passes the test if the benefits exceed the costs.

Suppose a state considers a bridge that is expected to cost $40 million to build and maintain. Engineers estimate that the bridge will save drivers time and reduce accidents, and they place a dollar value of $55 million on those benefits over its lifetime. The benefit-cost ratio is $55 million ÷ $40 million = 1.375, so the project passes. If the value of the benefits turned out to be only $30 million, the ratio would fall to 0.75, and the money would do more good elsewhere. The numbers in this example are hypothetical, but this is the kind of reasoning that officials try to use in decisions about real projects.

Why do real projects sometimes fail the test? Economists point to several factors. First, poor information: officials may not know what citizens value or what a project will really cost, and cost overruns are common. Second, political pressure: spending that benefits a small, well-organized group, such as a firm or a region that wins a contract, can be easy to pass because the group lobbies hard, while the costs are spread across millions of taxpayers who barely notice them. Third, weak incentives: a business that wastes money loses profit, but a government agency may face no similar discipline. Fourth, unintended consequences: a program may change behavior in ways its designers did not expect, such as a benefit that is reduced as earnings rise and therefore discourages a recipient from working more hours. Fifth, opportunity cost: money spent on one program cannot be spent on another or returned to taxpayers.

A fair evaluation applies the same tests to every kind of program, including those you favor. Defenders of government programs often note that markets also fail, which is why governments act in the first place, and that private firms, too, make costly mistakes. Skeptics of large programs answer that the market's profit-and-loss feedback fixes mistakes faster than the political process does. In practice, informed citizens can judge each program by asking: What problem is it solving? What does it cost, and who pays? What happens to people's incentives? Is there a cheaper way to reach the goal?

Key Concepts

State Revenue and Spending

The United States has three levels of government, and each has its own sources of money. The federal government depends on income and payroll taxes. State governments, which Section 4 examines first, collect their own taxes and also receive money from Washington. The largest sources of state tax revenue are individual income taxes and sales taxes. Some states, such as Texas and Florida, have no income tax and rely more heavily on sales and other taxes. Most also tax corporate profits, and many charge excise taxes on fuel, alcohol, and tobacco, as well as fees for licenses and tuition.

Money also arrives from the federal government as a grant, a payment from one level of government to another for a stated purpose. Federal grants pay for a large share of state health care programs such as Medicaid, as well as for highways, education, and other programs. When states accept grants, they usually have to follow federal rules about how the money is spent. In California's 2026–27 budget, federal funds are estimated at about $187 billion, which is a significant part of the state's spending on health and human services.

California is the largest state economy in the nation, and its budget is large. The state's enacted 2026–27 budget plans total state spending of about $347 billion from the General Fund and special funds, which is the money the Legislature controls most directly, and the General Fund alone pays for about $251 billion. The General Fund gets most of its money from three taxes, which together are estimated to bring in about $226 billion in 2026–27. As Figure 18.11 shows, the personal income tax is by far the largest, bringing in about 64 percent of the three. The corporation tax contributes about 20 percent, and the sales and use tax contributes about 16 percent.

Because California's income tax is progressive and the highest earners pay a large share of it, the state's revenue tends to rise and fall with the incomes of high earners, including the profits from stock and other investments. In a strong stock market, revenue can surge. In a downturn, it can fall quickly, even though many costs, such as schools and health care, do not. For this reason, the state sets aside reserves. The enacted 2026–27 budget leaves about $19.6 billion in total reserves at the end of the year, according to the Legislative Analyst's Office, the Legislature's nonpartisan fiscal adviser.

Key Concepts

Balanced Budgets

A balanced budget is a budget in which spending does not exceed revenue. Nearly every state has a rule that requires one in some form, though the rules differ in strength. Unlike the federal government, a state cannot print money, and its borrowing is limited. California's constitution requires the Legislature to pass a balanced budget, in which estimated spending does not exceed estimated revenue, and voters strengthened that rule with Proposition 58 in 2004. The state may still rely on reserves, borrow temporarily, or delay payments, and critics note that estimates and accounting choices give legislators flexibility.

States and local governments divide their budgets into two parts. The operating budget covers day-to-day expenses, such as teacher salaries, the electricity for a courthouse, and the gasoline for fire engines. These expenses recur every year and are meant to be paid from current revenue. The capital budget covers long-lasting assets, such as a new school building, a bridge, or a water treatment plant. These projects may cost far more than a single year's revenue, so governments often pay for them by selling bonds, which are loans from investors that the government repays over many years. Borrowing for a long-lived asset can be fair, because future residents who benefit from the school or bridge help repay the cost.

That principle distinguishes sensible borrowing from a temptation. A government that borrows to pay for ongoing salaries is shifting the cost of today's services to the future without leaving anything behind. When you hear that a state or city has a "balanced budget," ask whether the claim applies to operating spending, to the capital budget, or to both.

Key Concepts

Local Revenue and Spending

Local governments, including counties, cities, school districts, and special districts for water, fire, or hospitals, are the governments closest to your daily life. Their largest source of tax revenue is the property tax. The tax is based on the value of real estate. Each county has a tax assessor, an official who determines the value of each property, called its assessed value, for tax purposes. The county then applies a tax rate to the assessed value, collects the tax, and distributes the money to the cities, schools, and other agencies that serve that property. Property is a good tax base for local governments because land cannot move away, and the tax is relatively stable year to year.

In California, the rules for property taxes were rewritten in 1978 by Proposition 13, a ballot initiative approved by voters. It limited the combined property tax rate to 1 percent of a property's assessed value, with some exceptions for debt approved by voters. It also limited how fast the assessed value can rise: by the lesser of 2 percent a year or the rate of inflation, until the property is sold. When the property changes hands, it is reassessed at the new purchase price. Finally, it required a two-thirds vote of local voters to approve special taxes dedicated to a specific purpose.

People disagree about Proposition 13, and the disagreement is a good case study in weighing costs and benefits. Supporters say it protects homeowners, including retirees and family farmers, from sudden property tax increases, and it makes tax bills predictable. Critics say it creates inequities, since two neighbors with identical houses may pay very different taxes depending on when each bought, and that it limits the revenue available for local services. Some also argue that it pushes cities to favor shopping centers, which generate sales tax, over housing, which generates less. Because local governments have less control over property taxes, they depend more on sales taxes, fees, and state aid.

Sales taxes are a second major source. In California, the statewide base rate is 7.25 percent, and cities and counties may add district taxes, usually with voter approval. As a result, the rate varies from place to place within Kern County, as Figure 18.13 shows for rates in effect from April 1 to September 30, 2026. A shopper in Shafter pays 7.25 percent, while one in Bakersfield pays 8.25 percent and one in McFarland pays 9.25 percent. Because shoppers can cross a city line to buy goods, local governments may compete with one another for retail stores and the sales tax revenue that comes with them. Many local governments also collect fees, such as building permits, and receive grants from the state and federal governments.

CASE STUDY

Should Online Sales Be Taxed?

For decades, a shopper who bought a pair of boots at a store in Bakersfield paid sales tax, while a shopper who bought the same boots from a website often did not. In 2018, the Supreme Court changed that. The case raises a question that every government faces: how should the rules of taxation adjust when the way people shop changes?

Background

A Tax That Did Not Reach the Internet

A sales tax is collected by the seller at the time of sale. But which state's tax does a seller have to collect when the seller and buyer are in different states? In 1992, in a case called Quill Corp. v. North Dakota, the Supreme Court ruled that a state could require a seller to collect its sales tax only if the seller had a physical presence in the state, such as a store, warehouse, or employees. A catalog company in another state did not have to collect North Dakota's tax. Buyers were supposed to pay a matching use tax on those purchases themselves, but very few did.

In the 1990s, this rule affected a small share of sales. As online shopping grew, it mattered more. Local store owners pointed out that they had to charge tax and out-of-state websites did not, so the websites could appear cheaper by an amount equal to the tax. State governments worried about lost revenue, because sales taxes pay for schools, roads, and public safety. In 2017, the Government Accountability Office (GAO), an agency that investigates for Congress, estimated that state and local governments could gain about $8 billion to $13 billion that year if they could require all remote sellers to collect sales tax.

In 2018, South Dakota passed a law requiring sellers with more than $100,000 in sales or 200 transactions in the state to collect its tax, even without a physical presence. The online retailer Wayfair challenged the law. On June 21, 2018, in South Dakota v. Wayfair, the Supreme Court ruled 5–4 that the physical presence rule was out of date and allowed the law to stand. Other states rapidly passed similar laws. In California, out-of-state retailers with more than $500,000 in sales into the state must collect California use tax, starting April 1, 2019, and a separate law that began on October 1, 2019, made large online marketplaces responsible for collecting the tax on sales by other sellers on their platforms. Supporters see the changes as fairness restored. Critics worry about burdens on small sellers and about taxes that follow a seller into every state. DOCUMENT D · COMPOSITE VIEWPOINTSThree Ways to Look at the Question

These viewpoints were written for this textbook. Each combines arguments commonly made in the debate over online sales taxes; they are not quotations from real individuals or organizations.

THE LEVEL-FIELD VIEW
"A store on Ming Avenue pays rent, payroll, and property tax, and it collects sales tax from every customer. If a website can sell the same item without collecting the tax, the playing field is tilted. A tax should depend on what is sold and who buys it, not on how the shopper pays. Taxing online sales removes a distortion and protects local jobs and the services that sales taxes pay for."
THE SMALL-SELLER VIEW
"A seller with one employee who ships crafts from a spare room should not have to learn the tax rules of thousands of cities and districts, each with its own rate and exemptions. The thresholds help, and software can help, but compliance still costs time and money that a large corporation can easily spread over millions of sales. Rules that are fair on paper can still favor the biggest sellers in practice."
THE TAXPAYER-AND-CHOICE VIEW
"Consumers who buy online have always owed use tax in principle, so collecting it at checkout simply enforces existing law. But every new tax collection also raises prices for households, and governments may be tempted to treat easy-to-collect sales taxes as a source of endless new revenue. Shoppers should ask whether the money will be spent well and whether lower-income families, who spend most of their income, bear more of the burden."
Thinking Economically: Which parts of each view can be tested with evidence? Which parts rest on values such as fairness or limited government?

Chapter 18 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · How Taxes Work
A tax is a mandatory payment to government. Principles such as benefits received, ability to pay, simplicity, and efficiency help judge taxes. Taxes can be proportional, progressive, or regressive. The incidence of a tax depends on elasticity, and taxes change incentives and can create deadweight loss and compliance costs.
Section 2 · Federal Taxes
Individual income taxes are withheld from pay and calculated by brackets, with marginal rates above average rates. Payroll taxes fund Social Security and Medicare. Corporations pay a 21 percent income tax. Excise taxes, tariffs, and estate and gift taxes raise smaller amounts.
Section 3 · Federal Government Spending
Mandatory spending, discretionary spending, and net interest make up the federal budget. Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, defense, and net interest are the largest items. The budget process runs on a fiscal year, and cost-benefit analysis helps judge when costs may outweigh benefits.
Section 4 · State and Local Taxes and Spending
States rely on income and sales taxes and federal grants; local governments rely on property taxes and local sales taxes. States must balance budgets and separate operating and capital budgets. Proposition 13 shapes California's property taxes.
