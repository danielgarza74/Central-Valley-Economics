Concept Review

Chapter 1 taught that scarcity forces choices and that every choice has an opportunity cost. Chapters 4 through 9 showed how prices, businesses, and labor markets work. Unit 3 turns from the economy as a whole to you as an economic decision maker. You sell your labor to earn income, and you must decide how to use that income. This chapter covers the foundations: how to plan your spending, where to keep your money, and how to make it grow.

Chapter 10 Key Concept

A budget is a plan that allocates limited income among competing uses: needs, wants, and savings. Good money management means building the plan on take-home pay, keeping money in a safe and low-cost account, setting aside savings first, and understanding that time, interest, risk, and inflation all affect what savings can become.

Why the Concept Matters

Imagine that you start your first full-time job after graduation, with take-home pay of about $1,950 a month. You rent a room, you need a way to get to work, and your phone, groceries, and insurance all need to be paid. On the first of the month, the rent is due and your balance is lower than you expected. A friend says, "Just use your card." Another says, "Don't worry, you'll earn more later." Without a plan, every money decision becomes an emergency. With a plan, most of them are made in advance, calmly, and in line with what you care about. By the end of this chapter, you will be able to build a monthly budget, choose and use a checking account, and explain how saving and investing can turn small amounts into larger ones over time.

Key Concepts

Why a Budget Is an Economic Tool

Every Friday at the Shafter Cinema or the taco truck outside the high school, you make the same kind of decision that governments and giant companies make: you have a limited amount of money and more things you would like to buy than you can afford. In Chapter 1 you learned that scarcity forces choices and that every choice has an opportunity cost, the next-best alternative you give up. A personal budget is a way of making those choices on purpose instead of by accident.

A budget is a plan for how you will earn, spend, and save money over a period of time, usually a month. It is not a punishment, and it is not a list of things you may not do. It is a written decision about what matters most to you. When you write a budget, you are doing what economists call allocating scarce resources: you are deciding how to divide a limited supply of dollars among competing uses. If you spend $60 on a pair of shoes, that $60 cannot also pay for gas, a phone bill, or the start of an emergency fund.

California is now making sure that every high school graduate studies this subject. In 2024 the state passed Assembly Bill 2927, which requires public high schools to offer a one-semester personal finance course beginning in the 2027–28 school year. Completing the course becomes a graduation requirement for students starting with the 2030–31 school year. The law lists topics the course must include, among them budgeting for independent living, banking, credit, insurance, taxes, and investing. This unit of your textbook covers those topics, one chapter at a time, and this chapter begins with the foundation: how to make a plan for your money, how to keep that money in a safe place, and how to make it grow.

Personal finance is also a place where the ideas from earlier chapters come to life. You face scarcity, because your income is limited. You respond to incentives, such as an employer's matching contribution or a bank's fee. You trade in markets, such as the labor market where you sell your time and the product markets where you spend your pay. As you study this chapter, remember that it offers general educational information, not advice about your own situation. Where the chapter uses made-up people or figures, it says so.

Key Concepts

Income: Gross Pay and Net Pay

Every budget begins with income, the money you receive in a period of time. Income can come from wages for a job, tips, self-employment, gifts, a scholarship or grant, or interest on savings. Most teenagers and young adults depend on wages, so the first thing to understand about income is the difference between what you earn and what you actually take home.

Your gross income is the total you earn before anything is subtracted. If you work 15 hours a week at California's statewide minimum wage of $16.90 an hour, which took effect on January 1, 2026, your gross pay is $253.50 a week, or $507.00 every two weeks. Your net income, also called take-home pay, is what is left after payroll deductions are subtracted. Deductions are amounts that your employer withholds from each paycheck and sends to the government or to another organization on your behalf.

The most common deductions are payroll taxes. Social Security takes 6.2 percent of your gross wages and Medicare takes 1.45 percent, for a combined 7.65 percent. Many workers also have federal and state income tax withheld, and California workers pay into the State Disability Insurance program. A worker with a retirement plan or health insurance through a job may see more deductions. You will study taxes in detail in Chapter 13, but for now remember the rule that matters most for a budget: build your budget on your net income, not your gross income. You can only spend the money you actually receive.

Figure 10.1 follows a made-up example. A student named Dani works 15 hours a week and earns $507.00 gross for two weeks. The deductions in the example use the real Social Security and Medicare rates and an illustrative rate for state disability insurance, and they leave about $461.63 in take-home pay. That is about $45 less than Dani might have guessed from the hourly wage alone. Students who budget with gross pay often run out of money before the next payday.

Key Concepts

Expenses: Fixed, Variable, Needs, and Wants

The second half of any budget is expenses, the money you spend. Economists and financial planners sort expenses in two useful ways. The first way is by how much they change from month to month. A fixed expense stays about the same every month and is usually due on a set date. Rent, a car payment, a phone plan, and an insurance premium are fixed expenses. A variable expense changes from month to month, depending on how much you use or buy. Groceries, gasoline, restaurant meals, clothing, and entertainment are variable expenses.

The second way is by how necessary they are. A need is something you must have to live and to earn income: a safe place to live, basic food, transportation to work or school, necessary clothing, and health care. A want is something you would like to have but could live without, such as a streaming service, a new pair of limited-edition shoes, or a meal at a restaurant. Needs and wants overlap in real life. You need food, but eating at a restaurant three times a week is a want. You may need a phone to get work schedules, but you do not need the most expensive model. The question to ask is not "Is this good?" but "How much of this do I need, and what am I giving up to get the rest?"

Fixed expenses matter especially because they are hard to change quickly. If you sign a 12-month lease at $900 a month, you have committed $10,800 of future income, and you cannot decide next month to spend less on it. Variable expenses are where most people find room to adjust. Cutting a variable expense, such as eating out, is usually easier than finding a cheaper apartment. For that reason, young adults should look hardest at a fixed expense before they sign a contract for it, because the decision is difficult to undo afterward.

Notice that savings does not appear on either list. Many financial planners treat savings as a third category that is paid first: you decide on an amount to set aside, treat it like a fixed expense, and only then plan the variable spending. This habit is often called paying yourself first, and you will return to it in Section 3.

Key Concepts

Building a Budget in Four Steps

Building a budget is a process, not a single act. Financial educators describe it in four steps, shown in Figure 10.2. You will notice that a budget is built on facts about your actual life, so the most important work comes before you write any numbers.

Step 1: Estimate your income. List all the money you expect in the coming month and write down only your net amounts. If your income varies, as it does for many farmworkers, packing-house workers, and seasonal employees in the Central Valley whose hours rise at harvest and fall in the winter, plan with a low estimate. A good habit for irregular income is to base the budget on the lowest month in the past year and treat anything extra as a surplus to save.

Step 2: Track your spending. Before you can plan, you need to know where your money goes. Keep a record of everything you spend for at least a month. You can use a notebook, the notes app on your phone, or a free spreadsheet. Tracking often brings surprises. Small purchases, like a $3 drink every school day, add up to more than $60 in a month. Bank and debit-card statements can fill in the gaps.

Step 3: Make a spending plan. Start with your savings goal, then list fixed expenses, then plan variable expenses. Add up the plan. If the total of your planned spending is lower than your income, you have a surplus. If it is higher, you have a deficit, and you must raise income or cut expenses. A budget that does not balance is not a plan; it is a wish.

Step 4: Review and adjust. At the end of the month, compare what you planned with what you actually spent. The difference is called a variance. A large variance is not a failure. It is information. Perhaps your gas bill was higher because a friend borrowed your car, or perhaps your plan for groceries was too low. Adjust the next month's budget accordingly. Over three or four months, most people find a pattern that works.

Key Concepts

Budgeting Methods and Rules of Thumb

There is no single correct budget, because incomes, costs, and goals differ. Still, planners have developed several methods that help people get started. Each is a tool, and you can mix them.

The 50/30/20 rule. One popular rule of thumb suggests dividing net income into three parts: about 50 percent for needs, 30 percent for wants, and 20 percent for saving and paying down debt. The rule is simple and easy to remember. Its weakness is that it can be hard to follow where living costs are high. In a city where rent alone takes 50 percent of a worker's pay, the rule must be adjusted. Treat it as a starting point rather than a law.

The zero-based budget. In a zero-based budget, you give every dollar a job until income minus planned spending, including savings, equals zero. Zero does not mean you spend everything. It means no money is left unassigned. If $145 is left over, you decide whether it goes to the emergency fund, to a goal, or to a buffer for surprises. Figure 10.3 shows how a zero-based budget for Marisol, the made-up worker from the example above, might look.

The envelope or category method. Some people take their cash for each variable category and put it in a labeled envelope: groceries, fun, gas. When an envelope is empty, spending in that category stops until the next month. Many banks and phone apps now offer digital versions that separate money into labeled "pockets" or "buckets." The method works because it makes the limit visible at the moment of spending.

Goals and SMART targets. A budget works best when it serves a goal that is specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-bound. "Save money" is a wish. "Save $1,200 for a used car down payment by next June, which means $100 a month" is a plan. Writing the goal in dollars and dates lets you check your progress every month.

What Do Americans Spend Money On?

How does a typical household divide its money? Each year, the Bureau of Labor Statistics in the U.S. Department of Labor surveys thousands of households in the Consumer Expenditure Surveys. A "consumer unit" in the survey is usually a family or a person who spends money together. The newest results, for 2025 and released on October 29, 2026, show that the average consumer unit spent $81,905 in the year, while its average income before taxes was $110,847.

Figure 10.4 shows where that spending went. Housing took the largest share, 33.9 percent, which was an average of $27,790 a year. Transportation, mostly buying and operating cars, came next at 16.5 percent. Food was 13.0 percent. Personal insurance and pensions, which includes Social Security payroll taxes and contributions to retirement plans, was 12.4 percent. Health care was 7.9 percent. Together, these five categories made up 83.7 percent of the typical household's spending. Entertainment, at 5.0 percent, was smaller than most people expect.

Averages hide a lot. A household of two retired people in Arvin and a household with five people in Bakersfield do not spend alike, and averages include households with very high incomes. The national figures do, however, show an important pattern: most spending is on a few big categories, and these categories tend to be fixed expenses that are hard to change quickly. That is why financial planners say that the three biggest decisions in a young person's budget are where to live, how to get to work, and how to get through school or training without large debt. The decision about a $5 drink, by comparison, is small.

The data also show why budgeting in California can be challenging. The median household in Kern County had an income of $70,210 in 2024, according to American Community Survey estimates, and the median value of a home in the county was $338,300. The averages in the national survey are not Kern County averages, so use them as a comparison rather than a target.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVETandas, Cundinas, and Savings Circles Around the World

People have always found ways to save together. In many Mexican and Mexican American communities, including families in the Central Valley, a tanda (also called a cundina) is a rotating savings circle. A group of friends or co-workers agrees to put in the same amount, such as $50 each week. Each week, one member receives the whole pot. Because everyone has a turn, each person is forced to save and eventually receives a lump sum large enough for a big expense, such as a down payment or a quinceañera.

Similar groups exist worldwide. They are called susu in parts of West Africa and the Caribbean, chit funds in India, and hui in China. They work because of trust and social pressure: members do not want to let down their neighbors. They also have a risk. Unlike a bank account, a savings circle is not insured, and if the person who runs it disappears, members can lose their money. The idea shows that the desire to save is universal, while the tools and the risks vary.

Key Concepts

The Psychology of Spending

California's personal finance law also asks courses to cover how psychology affects financial well-being, because knowing a rule does not automatically change behavior. Behavioral economists, who study how real people make decisions, have found several patterns that matter for budgeting.

Present bias. People tend to prefer a smaller reward now over a larger reward later. Spending today feels better than saving for the future, even when we know the future matters. A budget helps by turning the long-term goal into a monthly action. Setting up an automatic transfer to savings on payday removes the need for willpower.

Impulse buying. Stores, apps, and advertisers design environments that make buying easy: one-click checkout, limited-time sales, and "only three left" messages. A useful defense is a waiting rule. If a want costs more than a set amount, say $40, wait 48 hours before buying. If you still want it, and it fits the plan, buy it. Many impulses disappear.

Social comparison. Social media shows other people's best purchases and trips, which can make ordinary spending feel inadequate. Remember that you do not see the debts behind a purchase.

Mental accounting. People treat money differently depending on where it came from. A tax refund or birthday cash can feel like "free" money to be spent on wants, although it is worth the same as a paycheck. A budget treats every dollar alike, whether earned or received as a gift.

Key Concepts

Where Should Your Money Live?

Once you have a budget, you need a safe, convenient place to keep the money that you plan to spend. A pile of cash in a drawer can be stolen, lost in a fire, or spent without thinking. For most people, the answer is a financial institution, a business that holds money for customers and provides financial services such as accounts, payments, and loans. The two most common kinds are banks and credit unions.

A bank is a for-profit business owned by shareholders. It takes in deposits from customers and lends part of that money to other people and businesses, charging interest on the loans. It pays depositors a smaller interest rate, if any, and keeps the difference to pay its costs and earn a profit. In economic terms, banks are financial intermediaries: they connect people who have extra money to save with people who need to borrow. You will study this role of banks in detail in Chapter 14, Money and Banking.

A credit union is a not-for-profit cooperative that is owned by its members, who must usually share something in common, such as living in a county, working for the same employer, or belonging to the same school community. Because a credit union does not have outside shareholders, it returns its earnings to members through lower loan rates, higher savings rates, or lower fees. In Kern County, as in the rest of California, there are many credit unions that serve teachers, oil-field workers, farmworkers' families, and residents of particular towns. Credit unions can have fewer branches and ATMs than the large national banks, so convenience is an important factor to weigh.

Both kinds of institutions are protected by federal deposit insurance, which you will read about later in this section. Whichever you choose, the key questions are the same: What fees will I pay? How easy is it to get to my money? How is my money protected? And what does the institution offer that fits my goals?

Checking and Savings Accounts

The most common account for everyday spending is a checking account. A checking account lets you deposit money, then pay people by writing checks, using a debit card, setting up automatic payments, or sending money through an online or mobile service. Money in a checking account is liquid, which means it can be turned into cash or used for payments immediately. Checking accounts usually pay little or no interest. They are designed for spending, not for earning.

A savings account is designed to hold money that you do not plan to spend soon. It pays interest, and the account usually limits how often you can move money out. Many people keep both: a checking account for bills and daily expenses and a savings account, preferably at the same institution or linked to it, for goals and emergencies. You will see in Section 3 that the national average savings rate was only 0.39 percent in November 2026, but some online banks and credit unions pay a higher rate, so shopping around matters.

To open an account, you usually need to present identification, such as a driver license, state ID, or passport, and give your Social Security number or Individual Taxpayer Identification Number. Minors under 18 can usually open an account only with a parent or guardian as a joint owner. Many banks have special accounts for teens and students that carry no monthly fee. Before opening an account, banks often check a database that tracks past account problems, such as unpaid overdrafts; a record there can make it harder to open an account later.

Ways to Pay From a Checking Account

Money in a checking account can move in several ways. The tool you choose affects how fast the money leaves your account, how much protection you have if something goes wrong, and what the transaction costs.

A check is a written order telling your bank to pay a specific amount of money from your account to a person or business. To write a check, you fill in the date, the name of the payee (the person or business receiving the money), the amount in numbers and in words, and a memo line that is optional, and then you sign it. The numbers along the bottom are the routing number, which identifies your bank, and your account number, followed by the check number. To deposit a check you receive, you endorse it by signing the back. For safety, many people write "for deposit only" above the signature. Many banks now allow you to deposit a check by taking a photo of it with a mobile app, but you should keep the paper check for a few weeks in case there is a problem. A debit card is a card that takes money directly from your checking account when you use it. It looks like a credit card, but it is not borrowing; it is spending money you already have. Because the money leaves your account right away, a debit-card purchase that is larger than your balance can cause an overdraft. Debit cards also come with different legal protections than credit cards. Under federal rules, if someone steals your debit card number, your loss is limited to $50 if you report it within two business days of learning about the loss. If you wait longer, your loss can be as high as $500, and if you wait more than 60 days after the statement shows the unauthorized charge, you could lose all the money taken. The lesson is to check your account often and report problems fast.

Automatic and online payments. You can authorize a business to withdraw a bill, such as a phone payment, from your account each month, or you can use your bank's online bill-pay service. These payments, often called ACH payments after the Automated Clearing House network that processes them, are convenient but can cause overdrafts if you forget that a large payment is coming. Peer-to-peer apps let people send money to each other using a phone number or email address. They are fast, but a payment sent to the wrong person, or to a scammer, can be very hard or impossible to get back. Send money only to people you know and always double-check the recipient.

Direct deposit works in the opposite direction. Instead of receiving a paper paycheck, you give your employer your routing and account numbers, and your pay goes straight into your account on payday. It is faster and safer than a paper check, and many banks waive monthly fees for customers who use it.

Key Concepts

Fees, Overdrafts, and Your Budget

Banks earn money from loans, but they also earn money from fees. Fees can be a small nuisance or a large drain on a budget, especially for people who keep low balances. Common fees include a monthly maintenance fee for keeping the account open, an out-of-network ATM fee, a fee for a paper statement, a stop-payment fee, and fees for wire transfers. Many banks waive the monthly fee if you set up direct deposit or maintain a minimum balance. Before opening an account, ask for the fee schedule in writing and check which of the fees you are likely to trigger.

The most costly fee for many customers is the overdraft fee. An overdraft occurs when you spend more money from your account than you have in it. The bank may pay the transaction anyway, covering the shortfall as if it were making you a very short loan, and charge you a fee. Or the bank may decline the transaction, possibly with a fee for insufficient funds. Suppose a bank charged a hypothetical $35 overdraft fee, and you buy a $4 coffee when your balance is $2. The coffee costs you $39 in total, and the fee is 875 percent of the purchase (35 ÷ 4 = 8.75). Overdraft fees are one of the most expensive ways to borrow small amounts of money.

Overdraft fees fall unevenly. The Federal Reserve's 2025 survey of household economics found that 12 percent of adults with a bank account had paid at least one overdraft fee in the previous 12 months. Among adults with household incomes of $25,000 to $49,999, the share was 20 percent; among those earning $100,000 or more it was just 6 percent (Figure 10.6). Black adults (25 percent) and Hispanic adults (17 percent) were more likely to report paying overdraft fees than white adults (8 percent). People with the smallest cushions pay the most in fees, which makes it harder to build a cushion.

Rules on overdraft fees have changed. In 2024, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau finalized a rule that would have capped overdraft fees at large banks, but in May 2025 Congress and the President nullified that rule, so it never took effect. Federal law still requires banks to get your permission, called opting in, before charging a fee for a debit-card purchase or ATM withdrawal that overdraws the account. You can decline overdraft coverage, in which case a transaction that you cannot afford will simply be declined. Many people also link a savings account for overdraft protection, so that the bank moves money from savings to checking.

Key Concepts

Keeping Track: Registers, Statements, and Reconciling

Your checking account balance is not just a number on a screen. Because some payments take a day or more to clear, the balance you see may not match what you have available to spend. The ledger balance includes every transaction that has been posted, while the available balance subtracts pending transactions and holds. The safest habit is to keep your own record of what you have deposited and spent, called a register. You can keep a register on paper, in a spreadsheet, or in your bank's app.

A register has columns for the date, a description, the amount of each deposit or withdrawal, and a running balance. After each transaction, you calculate the new balance: previous balance + deposits − withdrawals = new balance. Once a month, your bank sends or posts a bank statement, which lists every transaction in a period, along with fees and interest, and the balance at the end. To reconcile an account means to compare your register to the statement, find the differences, and explain them. If they do not match, you may have forgotten a transaction, made an arithmetic error, or discovered an error or fraud at the bank.

Common reasons for a difference are outstanding checks (checks you wrote that have not yet been cashed), deposits in transit (deposits you made that have not yet been posted), and fees or interest that appear on the statement but not in your register. Figure 10.7 shows a short example. You start with the bank's ending balance, add deposits in transit, subtract outstanding checks, and the result should equal your register balance. If it does not, search for the difference by checking each entry against the statement, starting with the largest.

Reviewing your statements has another benefit. Fraud often begins with a small test charge of a few dollars. Setting up text or app alerts for every card transaction or any balance below a number you choose can help you catch problems early.

Key Concepts

Deposit Insurance and Security

What happens to your money if a bank fails? Before the 1930s, depositors often lost everything. During the Great Depression, thousands of banks closed, and families lost their savings. Congress responded by creating the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, or FDIC, in 1933. The FDIC insures deposits at member banks, and the National Credit Union Administration, or NCUA, does the same for federally insured credit unions. Each insures up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category. The FDIC reports that since it began, no depositor has lost a penny of insured funds.

This protection is called deposit insurance. It applies to checking, savings, money market, and certificate of deposit accounts, but not to stocks, bonds, mutual funds, or cryptocurrency, even if you buy them through a bank. Look for the FDIC or NCUA sign, and check that an app or financial company is actually backed by an insured bank. If you keep more than $250,000 in one bank, you can spread it among institutions or ownership types so that all of it is covered.

Security matters just as much as insurance. Protect your account by never sharing your PIN or online password, using a different password for each financial account, turning on two-step verification, and not clicking on links in unexpected texts or emails that claim to be from your bank. A real bank will never ask you to read back a one-time code. If something seems wrong, call the number on the back of your card. You will study scams and identity theft in Chapter 11.

Who Is Left Out of the Banking System?

Not everyone has a bank account. People who do not use checking, savings, or money market accounts are said to be unbanked. Why Save? Paying Yourself First

Saving means setting aside part of your income now so that you can use it later. Economists define saving as income that is not spent on current consumption. The money you save can be placed in a bank account or invested in assets that you hope will grow. Saving is a choice with an opportunity cost, because every dollar saved is a dollar you cannot spend today. The reward is security and opportunity in the future.

People save for different reasons, and a good plan names each one. The first is safety: money for emergencies such as a car repair, a medical bill, or a gap between jobs. The second is goals: a used car, a security deposit on an apartment, a trip, or tuition and supplies at Bakersfield College. The third is big, future needs, such as a down payment on a home or the start of a family. The fourth is retirement, which seems far away but turns out to be the most expensive goal of all because people may live for decades after they stop working.

Many people find that it is easier to save if they pay themselves first. Instead of saving whatever is left at the end of the month, which is often nothing, you decide on a savings amount when you are paid, set up an automatic transfer from checking to savings on payday, and then plan your spending with the rest. In your budget, savings is treated like a fixed expense. An emergency fund is money set aside in a safe, easy-to-reach account to cover unexpected costs. Financial educators often suggest working toward three to six months of essential expenses. In the budget for Marisol in Section 1, essential expenses (housing, groceries, transportation, phone, and health) total $1,310 a month, so three months would be $3,930 and six months would be $7,860. Because that number can seem huge, a good first goal is smaller, such as $500 to $1,000, which can handle many common surprises.

How well are Americans doing? The Federal Reserve's 2025 survey found that 63 percent of adults said they would cover a $400 emergency expense entirely with cash or its equivalent. That figure was unchanged from the previous three years and down from 68 percent in 2021, which means more than one in three adults would have to borrow, sell something, or go without. In the same survey, 73 percent of adults said they were doing at least okay financially, and only 35 percent of adults who were not retired thought their retirement savings were on track. The case study at the end of this chapter looks at these numbers more closely.

The National Saving Rate

For the whole country, economists measure saving with the personal saving rate. The U.S. Bureau of Economic Analysis calculates it as the share of disposable personal income (income after taxes) that households do not spend. Interest and the Power of Compounding

When you deposit money in an account, the bank pays you for the use of your money. That payment is called interest. When you borrow, the opposite happens: you pay interest to the lender. The interest rate is the percentage of the amount, called the principal, that is paid per year.

The simplest kind of interest is simple interest, which is paid only on the original principal. The formula is I = P × r × t, where P is the principal, r is the annual rate written as a decimal, and t is time in years. If you deposited $1,000 at 4 percent simple interest for 5 years, you would earn $1,000 × 0.04 × 5 = $200. Most savings accounts, however, pay compound interest: interest is paid on the principal and on interest already earned. In the first year, $1,000 at 4 percent compounded annually earns $40 and grows to $1,040. In the second year, you earn 4 percent of $1,040, which is $41.60, so the balance is $1,081.60. After 5 years the balance is about $1,216.65, which is $16.65 more than with simple interest. The longer the time, the bigger the gap.

Banks advertise a rate called the annual percentage yield (APY), which includes the effect of compounding. When you compare savings accounts, compare the APY, since the same stated rate compounded daily gives a slightly higher yield than the same rate compounded yearly.

A useful shortcut is the Rule of 72. To estimate how many years it takes money to double, divide 72 by the annual interest rate in percent. At 4 percent, money doubles in about 72 ÷ 4 = 18 years. At 7 percent, it doubles in about 72 ÷ 7 ≈ 10 years. At the 0.39 percent national average savings rate, it would take about 72 ÷ 0.39 ≈ 185 years. The rule is an approximation, but it shows why both the rate and the time matter.

Figure 10.10 puts these ideas together using a made-up example. It shows what could happen if a person saved $100 every month for 40 years at three different interest rates. At 0.39 percent, the average rate on a savings account in November 2026, the balance would be about $51,937, barely above the $48,000 that was deposited. At a hypothetical 7 percent a year, which is the kind of return that the stock market has sometimes produced over long periods but never guarantees, the balance would be about $262,481. Most of that final balance is interest earned on earlier interest. The numbers are hypothetical and are not a forecast.

Inflation: The Hidden Cost of Standing Still

Interest is only part of the story. Inflation is a general rise in prices over time. When prices rise, each dollar buys less than it did before. The Bureau of Labor Statistics measures inflation with the Consumer Price Index (CPI). In September 2026, the CPI was 3.7 percent higher than a year earlier, up from 3.4 percent in August, with energy prices up 20.4 percent over the year.

Economists distinguish between nominal and real values. The nominal interest rate is the rate you see advertised. The real interest rate subtracts inflation to show the change in what the money can buy. A good approximation is real rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate. If your savings account pays 0.39 percent and prices are rising by 3.7 percent, the real rate is about 0.39 − 3.7 = −3.31 percent. Your account balance grows by a few cents, but what the money can buy is shrinking. For that reason, long-term money such as retirement savings is usually invested in assets that have historically grown faster than inflation, while emergency money is kept safe even though it may lose purchasing power.

Inflation is one reason that saving cannot be only about safety. It is also an incentive: if you expect prices to rise, you might want to buy something now. A high inflation rate also raises the cost of the goals that you are saving toward, such as tuition or rent.

Where to Put Your Money: Risk, Return, and Liquidity

Not all savings and investments are alike. When you choose among them, you weigh three features. Return is the money you gain from an asset, as a percent of what you put in. Risk, defined here, is the chance that the actual return will be lower than expected, including the chance of losing money. Liquidity is how quickly you can turn the asset into cash without losing value. In general, there is a trade-off: assets that offer higher possible returns come with higher risk, and the safest assets tend to pay the least. This is the risk-return trade-off, and it is one of the most important ideas in finance.

The safest place for money is an insured deposit account. A savings account and a money market account pay interest, are insured, and let you withdraw money easily. A certificate of deposit (CD) is an insured deposit that pays a set interest rate if you leave the money for a fixed time, such as 6 months or 5 years. If you withdraw early, you usually pay a penalty. The government also sells Treasury securities, loans to the federal government that are considered very safe.

To earn higher returns, people invest in assets whose value can rise or fall. A stock is a share of ownership in a company. If the company grows and earns profits, the stock may rise in price and may pay dividends, a share of profits paid to owners. If the company does poorly, the price can fall, and in extreme cases the stock can become worthless. A bond is a loan that you make to a company or government in exchange for a promise of regular interest payments and the return of your principal at a set date. Bonds usually have lower risk and lower expected return than stocks, but they are not risk-free: the issuer may fail to pay, and bond prices fall when interest rates rise.

Buying individual stocks and bonds is risky because the fortunes of one company can change suddenly. A mutual fund is a pool of money from many investors that is managed to buy a collection of stocks, bonds, or both. An index fund is a mutual fund (or an exchange-traded fund, ETF) that does not try to pick winners. Instead, it holds all the securities in a market index, such as the S&P 500, which tracks 500 large U.S. companies, and earns about what the index earns. Because they require little management, index funds usually have very low fees. Their main appeal is diversification: by holding many assets, you reduce the harm if any one of them fails. You may have heard the advice, "Don't put all your eggs in one basket." Diversification is that advice in economics. It lowers the risk of an individual company but does not remove the risk that the entire market will fall.

Fees matter. Every mutual fund charges an annual expense ratio, a percent of your balance taken to pay for management. Over decades, a difference of one percentage point a year can reduce a final balance by tens of thousands of dollars. Also remember that investments are not insured. Unlike a bank account, the value of a stock fund can fall, and deposit insurance does not protect you from market losses. Time also matters: the shorter the time until you need the money, the less risk you should take with it. Money you need within a few years belongs in safe accounts.

Saving for Retirement: 401(k)s and IRAs

Retirement may seem too far away to matter, but the power of compound interest explains why the best time to begin is early. A retirement account is an account that holds long-term savings and, in most cases, offers tax advantages in exchange for limits on when the money can be taken out. Two main types are sponsored by employers or opened individually.

A 401(k) (or a 403(b) for employees of public schools and nonprofit organizations) is a retirement plan offered through an employer. The employee chooses a percent of each paycheck to contribute, and the money is invested in funds that the plan offers. In many plans, the employer also adds money, called a match. For example, an employer might add 50 cents for every dollar the employee contributes, up to 6 percent of pay. This is hypothetical, but the principle is real: a match is part of your compensation, and not taking it is like turning down a raise. For 2026, the Internal Revenue Service lets workers put up to $24,500 into a 401(k), 403(b), or similar plan.

An individual retirement account (IRA) is opened by an individual, usually at a bank, brokerage, or fund company, and does not require an employer. For 2026, the IRA limit is $7,500. In a traditional account, contributions may lower your taxable income now, and you pay taxes when you take the money out in retirement. In a Roth account, you contribute money that has already been taxed, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. Roth contributions are phased out at higher incomes: in 2026 the phase-out for a single filer begins at $153,000. Withdrawals before age 59½ can be subject to penalties, so retirement accounts are for long-term money, not for the emergency fund.

Many young people do not have a plan at work. California created CalSavers to help. Under this state program, employers with at least one employee that do not offer a retirement plan must either enroll in CalSavers or establish a plan of their own. Employees who are enrolled contribute from each paycheck to an IRA that belongs to them, and they can change the amount or opt out. Self-employed workers and others can also open a CalSavers account. Workers with low or moderate incomes may qualify for the federal Saver's Credit, which for 2026 is available to single filers with income up to $40,250, heads of household up to $60,375, and joint filers up to $80,500.

Social Security, which you will study in Chapter 13, replaces part of your pay in retirement, but it was never designed to be the only source of income. Retirement accounts, along with Social Security, are called a "three-legged stool" along with other savings.

ECONOMICS PACESETTER

John C. BogleMaking Investing Cheap and Simple

Before the 1970s, most mutual funds hired managers to pick stocks that they believed would beat the market. Investors paid for that effort through annual fees, and many funds did not succeed in beating the market after those fees. John C. Bogle, a Princeton graduate who wrote his senior thesis on mutual funds, became convinced that small investors were paying too much for too little.

In 1974, Bogle founded The Vanguard Group, a company organized so that the funds were owned by the people who invested in them and so that savings from lower costs would go to investors rather than outside owners. In 1976, Vanguard launched the First Index Investment Trust, which did not try to pick winning stocks. Instead it held the stocks in the S&P 500 index and tried to match the market's return at a very low cost. Critics scoffed. Skeptics nicknamed the fund "Bogle's Folly," arguing that aiming for average performance was settling for less. The fund grew slowly at first. Over time, the evidence persuaded many investors that after fees, a large share of professional managers did not beat the market, and that the cost of investing mattered a great deal.

The idea Bogle popularized has become one of the most widely used tools in personal finance. Today, index funds and exchange-traded funds are available in many retirement plans, including 401(k)s and IRAs, and they hold trillions of dollars. The lesson for a young saver is not that any one fund is right for everyone. It is that costs compound too: a fee of 1 percent a year takes a bigger bite out of a long-term account than most people realize, and diversification is a low-cost way to lower risk.

Key Concepts

Giving and Spending With Purpose

California's personal finance requirement also includes charitable giving. Charitable giving means donating money, goods, or time to organizations that work for the common good, such as food banks, churches and temples, scholarship funds, and animal shelters. Giving is a spending choice like any other, so many people include it in their budget as a line item. It has an opportunity cost: a dollar given is a dollar not saved or spent. But many people consider it a priority because it matches their values, helps neighbors, or strengthens their communities.

A few practical rules help. First, give to organizations you have checked. Several independent websites rate charities on how they use donations, and the Internal Revenue Service has a tool to confirm that an organization is a tax-exempt charity. Second, be wary of high-pressure requests, especially after a disaster, when scammers copy the names of real charities. Third, keep records. Donations to qualified charities can sometimes lower your taxes if you itemize your deductions, a topic for Chapter 13. Finally, giving does not have to be money. Time given to a school fundraiser, a youth team, or a neighbor in need also counts.

CASE STUDY

The $400 Question:
Could You Cover a Surprise?

Every year since 2013, the Federal Reserve has asked thousands of Americans the same simple question: if you had an unexpected expense of $400, how would you pay for it? The answers show how well a typical household's budget can absorb a surprise, and what happens when it cannot.

Background

A Small Shock, a Big Test

A flat tire, a broken phone screen, a dental bill, and a day of missed work are examples of expenses that nobody puts in a monthly budget because nobody knows exactly when they will happen. Economists call this kind of event a financial shock. For a household with savings, a $400 shock is an annoyance. For a household without savings, it can set off a chain of other problems: a late rent payment, a high-interest loan, or a skipped bill.

Since 2013, the Federal Reserve Board has surveyed adults in its report on the economic well-being of U.S. households. One question asks how a person would handle a $400 emergency expense. A person is counted as prepared if he or she would pay for it entirely with cash, savings, or a credit card paid off at the next statement. The others would borrow, sell something, ask for help, or not be able to pay. In the survey for 2025, released in May 2026, 63 percent of adults said they would cover the expense with cash or its equivalent. That share had been 68 percent in 2021 and has held at 63 percent in each of the surveys since 2022.

This case study asks you to think about what the number means and how budgeting, banking, and saving relate to it. As you read, remember that the same information can be read in more than one way. A share of 63 percent means that most adults are prepared, and it also means that more than one in three are not. Both statements are true, and the way you frame the number can change how a reader reacts.

Chapter 10 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Budgeting
A budget is a plan for earning, spending, and saving that helps you choose among scarce uses of income. Build it on net income, sort expenses as fixed or variable and as needs or wants, and follow the four-step cycle of estimating income, tracking, planning, and reviewing. Psychology, such as present bias and impulse buying, affects spending.
Section 2 · Checking Accounts and Banking
Banks and credit unions hold deposits and are protected by deposit insurance up to $250,000. A checking account lets you pay with checks, debit cards, and electronic payments. Fees such as overdraft fees can be costly, and keeping a register and reconciling a statement help prevent errors and fraud.
Section 3 · Saving and Investing
An emergency fund protects a budget. Compound interest and time make savings grow, but inflation reduces real returns. Savings accounts, CDs, bonds, stocks, and index funds trade off risk, return, and liquidity, and retirement accounts such as a 401(k) and an IRA offer tax advantages for long-term goals.
Case Study · The $400 Question
In 2025, 63 percent of adults could cover a $400 emergency with cash. Surprises are certain even if their timing is not, so households prepare through budgets, accounts, and savings, and they weigh the costs of other options such as borrowing.
