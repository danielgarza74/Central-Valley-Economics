Concept Review

You already think like an economist more often than you realize. Every day you decide how to spend things you have only so much of: the money in your wallet, the hours between the last bell and bedtime, and the energy you bring to practice, work, or homework. When you decide, you are weighing what you get against what you give up. This chapter gives that everyday habit a name and a set of tools.

Chapter 1 Key Concept

Scarcity is the condition that exists because people's wants are unlimited while the resources needed to satisfy them are limited. Because of scarcity, every person, business, and society must make choices, and every choice has a cost.

Why the Concept Matters

Picture a Saturday in October. Your manager at the packing house texts asking if you can work a six-hour shift. Your cousin's quinceañera starts at four that afternoon, and your family expects you there early to help set up. You also have a chemistry test on Monday that you have not studied for. You cannot do all three, because there are only so many hours in a Saturday. Whatever you choose, something valuable gets left behind. Economists call that problem scarcity, and the way you sort through it is called economic reasoning. By the end of this chapter you will be able to name the cost of your choice, explain the incentives pulling you in each direction, and use the same tools that economists use to study farms, businesses, and entire nations.

Case Study Preview

Kern County is one of the most productive farming counties in the United States, and almost all of that production depends on water. As California limits how much groundwater farms may pump, growers, towns, and energy companies all have to decide how to use a resource that is running short. Key Concepts

What Is Scarcity?

Think about the last time you wanted something you could not have. Maybe it was a new phone, a pair of shoes, a car, a trip, or simply more time on a Sunday afternoon. Now imagine that you got it. Would you stop wanting things? Almost certainly not. Within days or weeks, a new want would appear: a better phone case, a different color of shoe, insurance for the car, or another trip. Human wants, the desires people have for goods, services, and experiences, have no natural limit. As soon as one is satisfied, another takes its place.

Some of what we want, we truly need. Needs are the things a person must have to survive: food, water, clothing, and shelter. Beyond these basics, nearly everything else falls into the category of wants. The line between the two is not always sharp. You need food, but you want a carne asada burrito instead of rice and beans. You need clothing, but you want a particular brand. You need a way to get to work, but you want your own car rather than a ride or a bus pass. Economists notice that even our needs can be met in many different ways, and most of those ways involve wants as well.

Now consider the other side of the problem. Everything people want has to come from somewhere. The food on your plate began with farmland, water, seed, fuel, machinery, and many hours of human work. All of those resources are limited. There are only so many acres of farmland in the San Joaquin Valley, only so much water flowing in the Kern River and stored underground, and only so many people available to plant, pick, pack, and drive the trucks. Even the most basic resource of all, time, is limited. Every person on Earth, whether a farmworker or a billionaire, has exactly 24 hours in a day.

When unlimited wants meet limited resources, the result is scarcity. Scarcity is the condition that exists because human wants are always greater than the resources available to satisfy them. Because of scarcity, people cannot have everything they want. They must choose. That simple chain, from scarcity to choice, is the foundation of the entire subject you are about to study.

Scarcity Is Not the Same as a Shortage

In everyday conversation, people often use the word scarce to mean "hard to find." When a new gaming console sells out before the holidays, or when store shelves run out of bottled water before a heat wave, people say those items have become scarce. Economists use a different word for those situations. A shortage occurs when people want to buy more of a product at its current price than sellers have available. Shortages are usually temporary. The store restocks, the factory catches up, or the price rises until the number of buyers matches the number of items for sale. (You will learn exactly how that happens in Unit 2.)

Scarcity is different because it never goes away. Even when the shelves are full, the console is still scarce in the economic sense, because the plastic, metal, chips, factory time, and labor that went into making it could have been used to make something else. Scarcity describes the basic relationship between all human wants and all available resources. A shortage describes one product, at one price, at one moment. Every economy on Earth, rich or poor, has always faced scarcity, and every economy always will.

Economics: The Study of Choices

Because scarcity forces choices, the study of those choices has its own name. Economics is the study of how individuals, businesses, and societies use limited resources to satisfy their unlimited wants. Notice what that definition does and does not say. Economics is not only about money, although money is part of it. It is not only about business, although businesses are important actors. It is not a branch of mathematics, although economists use numbers and graphs as tools. Economics is a way of thinking about choices: what people choose, why they choose it, and what happens as a result.

That way of thinking can be applied almost anywhere. A family deciding whether to buy a used car or keep taking the bus is making an economic decision. So is a school board deciding whether to hire more counselors or replace aging Chromebooks, a farmer deciding whether to plant carrots or let a field rest, and a nation deciding how much to spend on defense, health care, or highways. In each case, resources are limited, the options compete with one another, and choosing one path means giving up another.

Key Concepts

Scarcity Affects Everyone

It is tempting to think that scarcity is only a problem for people without much money. It is true that scarcity hits hardest when a family struggles to afford rent, groceries, or a doctor's visit. In every country in the world, including the United States, some people go without enough food, safe housing, or health care. But scarcity touches everyone, including the wealthy. A person with millions of dollars still has to decide how to spend limited time. A successful company still has a limited number of engineers and a limited budget for new projects. Even the federal government, which spends trillions of dollars each year, cannot fund every program that someone wants, and every dollar it spends must come from taxes or borrowing.

Scarcity affects people in two different roles. First, it affects people as buyers. Almost everything you use in daily life is either a good or a service. Goods are physical objects that can be bought and sold, such as tacos, textbooks, tires, and tractors. Services are actions or activities that one person performs for another, such as a haircut, a doctor's checkup, a bus ride, or a tutoring session. A person who buys goods and services for personal use is a consumer. Because of scarcity, consumers must decide which goods and services to buy with their limited income, and which ones to go without.

Second, scarcity affects people as sellers. A producer is a person or business that makes goods or provides services. The owner of a taquería, a grower who sells pistachios to a processor, a nurse who works at Adventist Health, and a teenager who mows lawns on weekends are all producers. Producers face scarcity because the resources they need, such as ingredients, land, machinery, and workers, are limited and costly. A producer must decide what to make, how much of it to make, and how to make it with the resources available.

Most people play both roles. On Friday afternoon you might work a shift as a cashier, which makes you a producer of a service. On Friday night you might spend part of your paycheck at the movies, which makes you a consumer. Scarcity follows you through both parts of the day.

Key Concepts

Three Basic Economic Questions

Scarcity does not only affect individuals. It also forces every society, from a small village to a nation of 340 million people, to answer the same three basic questions. Different societies answer these questions in very different ways, as you will see in Chapter 2. But no society can avoid them.

Question 1: What will be produced? Because resources are limited, a society cannot produce everything that its people want. It must decide which goods and services to make and in what amounts. Should a valley's farmland grow almonds, which earn high prices but need water all year long, or annual crops such as carrots and tomatoes, which can be left unplanted in a dry year? Should a stretch of open land become a solar farm, a housing development, or habitat for wildlife? Should a nation make more cars or more buses? Every answer to the "what" question means that some other good or service will not be produced, or will be produced in smaller amounts.

Question 2: How will it be produced? Once a society decides what to make, it must decide how to make it. Most goods can be produced in several ways. Wine grapes can be picked by hand by crews of farmworkers, or they can be harvested by a machine that shakes the fruit off the vines. A restaurant can take orders at a counter or through a touch-screen kiosk. The choice usually depends on which resources are most plentiful and least costly. Where workers are plentiful and machines are expensive, producers tend to rely more on human labor. Where workers are harder to find or more expensive, producers tend to invest in machines.

Question 3: For whom will it be produced? Finally, a society must decide how to divide what it produces among its people. Who gets the newest cars, the best apartments, the front-row concert seats, or the limited spots in a popular summer program? In some cases, goods go to whoever is willing and able to pay the highest price. In others, they are handed out first come, first served, by a lottery, by need, or by a government decision. The way a society answers the "for whom" question shapes how fair or unfair its people believe their economy to be, and it is often the most hotly debated of the three questions.

Key Concepts

The Factors of Production

To answer the three basic questions, a society has to understand what it has to work with. Economists sort all the resources used to make goods and services into four broad categories called the factors of production: land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship. Every good you have ever bought, from a bag of Kern County carrots to a smartphone assembled on the other side of the world, was made by combining these four factors. And because each factor is limited, each one is part of the scarcity problem.

Land

In everyday speech, land means the ground. In economics, land means all natural resources that are used to produce goods and services. It includes the soil itself, but also water, minerals, forests, fish, wind, sunlight, and the oil and natural gas found deep underground. Kern County is unusually rich in land in this economic sense. Its farm soil and long, sunny growing season support crops worth billions of dollars each year. For more than a century its oil fields have made it the center of oil production in California. And the steady winds of the Tehachapi Mountains and the strong desert sun now power large wind and solar projects. Even so, every one of these natural resources is limited. Oil fields eventually run dry, groundwater can be pumped faster than rain and rivers refill it, and a field covered with solar panels cannot also grow crops.

Labor

Labor is the effort that people devote to tasks for which they are paid. It includes the physical work of a farmworker pruning grapevines, the skilled work of an electrician wiring a new house, the caring work of a home health aide, and the mental work of an accountant or software engineer. The amount of labor available in an economy depends on how many people are able and willing to work and how many hours they work. Like land, labor is limited. There are only so many workers in any community, and each of them can work only so many hours in a week.

Capital

In everyday conversation, people often use the word capital to mean money. Economists use the word differently. Capital refers to the human-made resources used to produce other goods and services. Physical capital includes tools, machines, buildings, vehicles, and equipment: a tractor, a cold-storage warehouse, a delivery truck, a commercial oven, or the laptop that a graphic designer uses for work. Money by itself is not capital in this sense, because money cannot directly produce anything. Money becomes useful in production only when it is used to buy capital, hire labor, or pay for land. (Economists call the money that businesses use to buy capital financial capital, a term you will meet again in Chapter 15.)

There is a second, equally important kind of capital. Human capital is the knowledge, skills, and experience that make workers more productive. A welder who completes a certification program, a nurse who earns a degree, and a new employee who learns to run a packing line safely have all increased their human capital. When you study in school, you are building your own human capital. That is one reason, as you will see in Section 4, that workers with more education tend to earn higher wages.

Entrepreneurship

Land, labor, and capital do not organize themselves. Someone has to bring them together, decide what to produce, and take the risk that the effort might fail. Entrepreneurship is the combination of vision, skill, creativity, and willingness to take risks that is needed to start and run a business. An entrepreneur might be a woman who saves for years to open a bakery on Central Valley Highway, a pair of students who start a car-detailing business in a driveway, or an engineer who founds a company that makes better irrigation sensors. Entrepreneurs make the key decisions, and they accept the possibility of losing their money and time. In return, they hope to earn a profit. Without entrepreneurs, many of the goods and services we take for granted would never exist.

Scarcity and the Factors of Production

Scarcity exists because every one of the four factors is limited. A farm business, for example, must work within the acres it owns or rents, the water it can buy or pump, the number of workers it can hire at the going wage, the tractors and packing equipment it can afford, and the time and attention of its owners. When any one factor runs short, production is limited, even if the other factors are plentiful. A grower with plenty of land and plenty of workers still cannot produce a crop without water. A new restaurant with a great location and a talented chef still cannot open without ovens, refrigerators, and an owner willing to sign the lease.

This is why the three basic economic questions are so important. Deciding what to produce is really a decision about how to use scarce land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship. Deciding how to produce is a decision about which combination of factors to use. And deciding for whom is a decision about who gets to enjoy the goods and services that the factors made possible. In Section 2 you will see that each of these decisions comes with a cost, even when no money changes hands.

Key Concepts

Choice Is at the Heart of Economics

In Section 1 you learned that scarcity is a permanent condition. Because people can never have everything they want, they are always choosing. Some choices are small and quick: whether to buy a snack from the vending machine, which route to drive home, or whether to hit snooze one more time. Others are large and take months of thought: whether to enroll in college, accept a job in another city, buy a car, or start a business. Economists study all of these choices, large and small, because together they determine how a society uses its scarce resources.

Economists begin with a basic assumption about how people choose. They assume that people generally try to make the choices that leave them better off, given what they know at the time. In other words, people act with purpose. They compare their options, consider what each one offers, and pick the one that seems best to them. This does not mean people always choose wisely, or that they never make mistakes. It does not mean that people care only about money or only about themselves. A person might decide that the best use of a Saturday is volunteering at a food bank, and that choice, too, reflects a purposeful weighing of options. The assumption simply means that choices are not random. They respond to costs and benefits, and they can be studied.

When people make careful decisions about how to use scarce resources, economists say that they economize. To economize is to make decisions by comparing the costs and benefits of each option in order to get the most out of limited resources. A family that plans its grocery list around the weekly sales is economizing. So is a student who maps out the week so that homework, practice, and a part-time job all fit.

Key Concepts

Incentives Shape Choices

If people choose with purpose, then anything that changes the benefits or costs of an option can change what they choose. An incentive is a benefit offered to encourage people to act in a certain way, or a cost imposed to discourage them. Incentives are everywhere. A store offers "buy one, get one free" to encourage shoppers to buy more. A teacher offers extra credit to encourage students to complete an optional project. A city gives tickets to drivers who park in front of fire hydrants to discourage them from doing it again. Economists sometimes summarize the idea in four words: people respond to incentives.

Incentives can be positive or negative. A positive incentive rewards a behavior: a bonus for a salesperson who meets a goal, a discount for paying a bill early, or a scholarship for students with high grades. A negative incentive punishes a behavior or makes it more costly: a late fee on a library book, a fine for littering, or a penalty for turning in an assignment after the deadline. Both kinds work the same way. They change the costs or benefits of an option, and people adjust their choices as a result.

Monetary and Nonmonetary Incentives

Incentives also differ in the type of reward or cost they offer. A monetary incentive is a reward or penalty that involves money. Higher wages for working the night shift, a cash rebate for buying an energy-efficient refrigerator, a tax on cigarettes, and a parking ticket are all monetary incentives. They work by making a choice more or less costly in dollars.

A nonmonetary incentive is a reward or penalty that does not involve money. Praise from a coach, a spot on the honor roll, a "Student of the Month" photo in the front office, the respect of friends and family, a feeling of pride, or the embarrassment of being caught breaking a rule are all nonmonetary incentives. So are many incentives built into daily life: the convenience of a drive-through window, the time saved by taking a shortcut, or the satisfaction of helping your community. Nonmonetary incentives can be just as powerful as money, and sometimes more powerful. Many people work long hours as volunteer firefighters, youth coaches, or caregivers for relatives with little or no pay because of the meaning and respect those roles bring.

Changes in Incentives Change Behavior

Because people respond to incentives, changing an incentive is one of the most common ways that businesses and governments try to change behavior. California provides two good examples that you have probably seen yourself.

The first is the California Redemption Value, or CRV, on beverage containers. When you buy most canned or bottled drinks in California, you pay a small deposit, 5 cents for a container smaller than 24 ounces and 10 cents for a larger one. You get the deposit back when you return the empty container to a recycling center. The deposit gives people a monetary incentive to recycle rather than throw containers away, and it gives others a reason to collect containers that someone else discarded.

The second is the state's limit on single-use grocery bags. Since 2016, California has banned free single-use plastic bags at grocery stores, pharmacies, and many other stores, which must charge at least 10 cents for any bag they provide at checkout. (A 2024 law went further, ending the sale of plastic bags at checkout altogether beginning in 2026.) Ten cents is a small cost, but it is enough to change many shoppers' behavior. Large numbers of shoppers began bringing reusable bags from home or carrying a few items out in their hands. A small change in a monetary incentive led to a large change in what people did.

Incentives do not always work the way their designers expect, however. Sometimes people respond in ways no one predicted. If a school rewarded students only for the number of books they read, some students might choose the shortest books they could find. If a city charged a high fee to dispose of old couches at the dump, some people might leave them in alleys instead. Economists call these results unintended consequences, and spotting them is one of the most useful skills the economic way of thinking can give you.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVECan a Bag of Lentils Save Lives?

In rural areas of the state of Rajasthan in northwestern India, very few young children were receiving the full set of vaccines that protect against measles, tetanus, and other serious diseases. The vaccines were free. So why weren't parents bringing their children in?

A team of economists, including Abhijit Banerjee and Esther Duflo of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, worked with Seva Mandir, a local nonprofit group, to find out. They suspected that the problem was not that parents did not care, but that the small costs of each trip, such as a lost morning of work, a long walk, or a clinic that was often closed, kept getting in the way. So they ran an experiment in 134 villages. Some villages received a reliable monthly immunization camp. Others received the same camp plus a small incentive: a bag of raw lentils at each visit and a set of metal plates when a child completed the full schedule. A third group of villages received nothing new, so that researchers could compare.

The results, published in 2010, were striking. Reliable camps alone roughly tripled full immunization compared with villages that received nothing new. Adding the bag of lentils more than doubled the rate again. A small nonmonetary reward, food worth very little money, changed the choices of hundreds of families.

Key Concepts

Utility: Why People Choose Differently

If everyone responds to incentives, why do two people facing the same options often make different choices? One student spends her paycheck on concert tickets, while another saves every dollar for a car. One family spends Saturday at the lake, while their neighbors spend it at a swap meet. Economists explain these differences with the idea of utility. Utility is the benefit or satisfaction a person gets from using a good or service. Utility is personal. The same plate of menudo might give great utility to someone who grew up eating it on Sunday mornings and very little to someone who dislikes tripe.

Because utility varies from person to person, so do choices. Economists do not judge whether one person's preferences are better than another's. Instead, they assume that each person tries to get the greatest total utility from his or her limited resources. When economists say that a choice is "rational," they usually mean only that the person is pursuing his or her own goals in a sensible way, not that the goals themselves are right or wrong.

Key Concepts

Trade-Offs and Opportunity Cost

Every choice involves giving something up. When you choose one option, you must pass up others. A trade-off is the set of alternatives that you give up when you make a choice. A student deciding what to do after school might have several alternatives: work at a part-time job, go to basketball practice, study at the public library, or hang out with friends. If she chooses basketball, all the others become part of her trade-off.

Of all the alternatives you give up, one usually matters most to you. Economists focus on that one. Opportunity cost is the value of the next-best alternative that is given up when a choice is made. Opportunity cost is not the sum of everything you could have done instead. It is only the single most valuable thing you gave up. If the student's second choice, after basketball, was working at her part-time job, then the opportunity cost of practice is the wages and experience she could have earned at work that afternoon.

Opportunity cost is one of the most important ideas in all of economics, because it reminds us that every choice has a cost even when no money changes hands. You may have heard the old saying, "There is no such thing as a free lunch." Even if someone else pays for your lunch, the lunch is not truly free. The time you spend eating it could have been spent doing something else, and the money your friend spent could have bought something else. The resources used to make the lunch, the farmland, the cook's labor, and the stove, could have been used to make something else. Someone, somewhere, gave something up.

Key Concepts

Thinking at the Margin

Many decisions are not all-or-nothing. You rarely decide whether to study or not study; you decide whether to study one more hour. A business rarely decides whether to be open or closed forever; it decides whether to stay open one more hour tonight. A farmer rarely decides whether to irrigate or not irrigate; she decides whether to run the pumps for one more day. Economists call this kind of reasoning thinking at the margin, which means deciding whether to do a little more or a little less of something. The word margin refers to the edge, the next small step beyond where you are now.

To think at the margin, you compare two things. Marginal benefit is the extra benefit of adding one more unit of something: one more hour, one more worker, one more taco. Marginal cost is the extra cost of adding that same unit. The rule is simple: keep doing more of something as long as the marginal benefit is greater than the marginal cost. Stop when the marginal cost becomes greater than the marginal benefit.

Notice that the marginal benefit of an activity often shrinks as you do more of it. The first slice of pizza when you are hungry is wonderful; the fifth is much less so. The first hour of studying, when you are fresh and the material is new, usually helps your grade more than the fourth hour, when you are tired and reviewing what you already know. At the same time, the marginal cost often grows. Each extra hour of studying eats into time that is more and more valuable to you, such as sleep on the night before the test. Somewhere along the way, the line where benefits outweigh costs is crossed. That is the point where a smart decision-maker stops.

That is the power of marginal thinking. It breaks a big, fuzzy decision into small, clear steps.

Thinking at the margin also explains a puzzle that troubled early economists. Water is necessary for life, yet it is usually cheap. Diamonds are not necessary for anything, yet they are expensive. Why? Because people make choices about one more unit, not about all water or all diamonds. In most places, water is plentiful, so one more gallon adds little benefit and sells for little. Diamonds are rare, so one more diamond adds a great deal of satisfaction for the people who want one. As you will see in this chapter's Case Study, however, when water becomes truly scarce, as it has in parts of the San Joaquin Valley, the value of one more acre-foot of water rises sharply.

Key Concepts

Economists Use Models

In Section 2 you learned that every economic choice involves trade-offs. When a single person chooses between studying and working, the trade-off is easy to picture. But how can we picture the trade-offs facing a whole farm, a whole company, or a whole nation, where millions of choices are being made at the same time? Economists handle this problem the same way that scientists, engineers, and architects handle complicated problems. They build models.

An economic model is a simplified representation of reality that helps economists understand how something works. A model might be a diagram, a graph, a table, or a set of equations. You already know how useful simplified models can be. A map of Kern County leaves out almost everything about the real county, including every house, tree, and pothole. Yet it is far more useful for finding your way to Tehachapi than a satellite photo showing every detail would be. A good model, like a good map, leaves out the details that do not matter for the question at hand so that the details that do matter stand out.

Every model rests on assumptions, statements that the model accepts as true in order to keep things simple. One of the most common assumptions in economics is expressed by a Latin phrase, ceteris paribus, which means "all other things held constant." When an economist uses a model to study how one change affects the economy, she imagines that everything else stays the same. Of course, in the real world, many things change at once. But by changing only one thing at a time in the model, economists can see more clearly what effect each change has.

Key Concepts

Graphing the Possibilities

One of the simplest and most useful economic models is the production possibilities curve. A production possibilities curve (PPC) is a graph that shows all the different combinations of two goods or services that can be produced in a given time period using all available resources and technology. (Some textbooks call it a production possibilities frontier, or PPF, because the curve marks the outer limit, or frontier, of what can be produced.)

To build a PPC, economists make three assumptions. First, they look at only two goods or services, so that the model can be drawn on a flat graph. Second, they assume that the amount of resources, meaning land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship, is fixed. Third, they assume that the technology for producing the goods does not change. These assumptions describe a single moment in time. Later in this section you will see what happens when they change.

A Farm's Production Possibilities

Imagine a family farm near Arvin with 200 acres of cropland, a fixed amount of water, a crew of workers, and a set of tractors and other equipment. The family has decided to grow some combination of two vegetable crops this season: tomatoes and bell peppers. If they plant every acre in peppers, they can grow 100 tons of peppers and no tomatoes. If they plant every acre in tomatoes, they can grow 100 tons of tomatoes and no peppers. Or they can plant some of each. The table in Figure 1.7 shows six of the possible combinations, labeled A through F.

To draw the PPC, we place tomatoes on the horizontal axis and peppers on the vertical axis. Then we plot each combination as a point and connect the points with a smooth curve. Every point on the curve represents a combination that the farm can produce if it uses all of its resources fully.

Key Concepts

What a PPC Shows

A production possibilities curve may look like a simple line on a graph, but it illustrates almost every idea you have studied so far in this chapter. Reading a PPC carefully is an excellent way to review the whole economic way of thinking.

1. Scarcity

The PPC is a boundary. Points on or inside the curve can be produced. Points outside the curve, like point H in Figure 1.7, cannot be produced with the resources and technology the farm has right now, no matter how much the family might want them. The curve itself is a picture of scarcity. It shows that the farm's wants (more tomatoes and more peppers) run up against limited land, water, labor, and capital.

2. Trade-offs and Opportunity Cost

Because the farm is limited to points on or inside the curve, producing more of one crop means producing less of the other. Moving along the curve from point C to point D, the farm gains 20 tons of tomatoes but gives up 18 tons of peppers. Those 18 tons of peppers are the opportunity cost of the extra 20 tons of tomatoes. The PPC makes opportunity cost visible: it is the distance the curve drops as you move to the right.

3. Efficiency

Efficiency means using resources in such a way as to get the most output from them. Every point on the curve, from A through F, is efficient. At each of those points, the farm is using all of its land, water, labor, and equipment as fully as possible. There is no way to grow more of one crop without growing less of the other. Notice that the PPC does not tell us which efficient point is best. That depends on what the family wants to produce and what buyers are willing to pay, questions you will explore in Unit 2.

4. Underutilization

Point G in Figure 1.7 lies inside the curve. At point G, the farm produces 40 tons of tomatoes and 40 tons of peppers, which is less than it could. Perhaps a tractor broke down, some fields were left unplanted, the crew was short-handed during a heat wave, or water deliveries arrived late. Underutilization means using fewer resources than an economy is capable of using. Any point inside the curve shows underutilization. The farm could move from G to a point on the curve and have more of both crops, without giving up anything, simply by putting its idle resources to work.

5. The Law of Increasing Opportunity Costs

Look again at the table in Figure 1.7 and focus on the peppers column. Moving from A to B, the farm gives up only 4 tons of peppers to get its first 20 tons of tomatoes. From B to C it gives up 10 tons of peppers. From C to D, 18 tons. From D to E, 28 tons. And from E to F, a full 40 tons of peppers must be given up to get the last 20 tons of tomatoes. Each additional 20 tons of tomatoes costs more peppers than the 20 tons before it.

This pattern is so common that economists call it the law of increasing opportunity costs: as production shifts from one good to another, more and more resources are needed to produce each additional unit of the second good, so its opportunity cost rises. The law explains why most PPCs are bowed outward rather than drawn as straight lines.

Why does this happen? Because resources are not equally good at producing everything. Some of the farm's fields have soil and drainage that are ideal for tomatoes. Others are better suited to peppers. Some workers are experienced at staking and pruning tomato vines; others are expert pepper pickers. When the farm first switches a few acres to tomatoes, it uses the fields and workers best suited to tomatoes, so it gives up very few peppers. But as it keeps switching, it must eventually use land and workers that were excellent at peppers and only fair at tomatoes. Each new ton of tomatoes then costs many more tons of peppers. The same reasoning applies to a whole nation. Resources that are perfect for one use are rarely perfect for every use.

EXAMPLEA Nation's Choice: Guns or Butter

Economists often use a famous PPC to describe the choices a whole nation faces. On one axis is military goods, sometimes called "guns." On the other is consumer goods, sometimes called "butter." Every nation must decide how to divide its resources between defending itself and producing goods for its people's everyday use.

The United States made a dramatic move along its guns-or-butter curve during World War II. In February 1942, shortly after the attack on Pearl Harbor, American automakers stopped building cars for civilians. Factories in Detroit and elsewhere switched to producing tanks, aircraft engines, trucks, and other military equipment, and civilian car production did not resume until 1945. Gasoline, tires, sugar, coffee, and meat were rationed at home. The nation moved toward the "guns" end of its curve, and Americans experienced the opportunity cost directly: fewer consumer goods on store shelves.

Key Concepts

Underutilization in the Real World

For a whole economy, underutilization usually shows up as unemployment, idle factories, and empty storefronts. The most extreme example in American history is the Great Depression of the 1930s. In 1933, about one out of every four American workers who wanted a job could not find one. Factories sat silent and farm crops rotted unsold, even as many families went hungry. The U.S. economy was operating far inside its PPC. It had the land, labor, and capital to produce much more, but those resources were not being used.

A more recent example came in the spring of 2020. To slow the spread of COVID-19, many states ordered restaurants, stores, gyms, and schools to close. In April 2020, the U.S. unemployment rate jumped to nearly 15 percent, the highest level since the Great Depression. Millions of willing workers and thousands of businesses sat idle for weeks. The economy moved suddenly inside its PPC, and much of the following year was spent moving back toward the curve. When you study unemployment and the business cycle in Unit 4, you will learn more about why economies sometimes fall inside their production possibilities and what governments try to do about it.

Key Concepts

Economic Growth: Shifting the Curve

A PPC describes an economy at one moment in time, with fixed resources and fixed technology. But resources and technology do not stay fixed forever. When an economy gains more resources or finds better ways to use them, its production possibilities expand. Economic growth is an increase in a nation's total output of goods and services over time. On a graph, economic growth is shown by the entire PPC shifting outward, away from the origin, as in Figure 1.9. Combinations that were once impossible, outside the old curve, become possible.

Three main forces cause a PPC to shift outward:

  • More resources. A larger labor force, newly discovered natural resources, or additional capital such as new factories and machines all allow an economy to produce more. When a farm buys or rents more acres, or secures a new source of water, its own PPC moves out.
  • Better technology. New technology lets producers make more with the same resources. Drip irrigation, which delivers water directly to the roots of plants, allows many San Joaquin Valley growers to produce the same crop with less water than flood irrigation. GPS-guided tractors, better seed varieties, and new software do the same in other industries.
  • More human capital. When workers gain more education and training, they become more productive. A labor force with stronger skills can produce more with the same land and capital. This is one reason that investment in schools, community colleges, and job training is often described as an investment in economic growth.

A PPC can also shift inward, which means an economy's ability to produce has shrunk. A major earthquake, a war that destroys factories and roads, a disease that sickens many workers, or a long drought that reduces the water available for farming can all move the curve inward. As you will see in the Case Study, Kern County's farms face a serious question: what happens to their production possibilities as the amount of groundwater they can pump is reduced?

Key Concepts

Economics as a Social Science

Economics is a social science, which means it uses scientific methods to study human society and behavior. Like biologists, chemists, and physicists, economists try to explain how the world works by observing it, asking questions, forming possible explanations, and testing those explanations against evidence. The steps are the same ones you may have learned in a science class: observe something, form a hypothesis that might explain it, gather data, test the hypothesis, and draw a conclusion.

But economists face a challenge that chemists usually do not. Chemists can mix two substances in a laboratory, control the temperature, and repeat the experiment a hundred times. Economists study people, and people cannot be placed in a test tube. An economist cannot double the minimum wage in Bakersfield for a year just to see what happens, or force half the country into a recession to compare it with the other half. Instead, economists rely on three main tools: models, which you studied in Section 3; data, which are facts and figures collected about the real world; and, when possible, carefully designed experiments, such as the immunization study you read about in Section 2.

Economists also look for what they call natural experiments. These are real-world situations in which something changes for one group of people but not for another similar group, almost as if a researcher had planned it. When one state raises its minimum wage and its neighbor does not, for example, economists can compare what happens to workers and businesses on each side of the state line.

Models Help Explain Economic Activity

The production possibilities curve is one of many models that economists use. Another basic model shows how money, goods, and resources move through an entire economy. The circular flow model is a diagram that shows how households and businesses interact in two kinds of markets. In the product market, households buy the goods and services that businesses produce. In the factor market, businesses buy or rent the factors of production, such as labor and land, that households own.

Money flows in one direction around the circle, while goods, services, and resources flow in the opposite direction. Key Concepts

Using Statistics, Tables, and Graphs

Models help economists organize their thinking, but models must be tested against the real world. To do that, economists rely on statistics, numerical data, or facts, that describe the economy. Government agencies collect enormous amounts of economic statistics. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) surveys about 60,000 households every month to find out how many people are working and how much they earn. The U.S. Census Bureau counts the population and tracks incomes, housing, and business activity. The California Department of Food and Agriculture tracks the value of every major crop in the state, and each county's agricultural commissioner publishes an annual crop report. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis gathers hundreds of thousands of data series into a free online database called FRED that anyone can use.

Averages: Mean and Median

When economists describe a group with a single number, they usually use some kind of average. There are two common types. The mean is what most people call "the average": add up all the values and divide by the number of values. The median is the middle value when all the values are lined up from lowest to highest. Half of the values are above the median, and half are below it.

The difference matters. Imagine five friends who work part-time jobs and earn $300, $350, $400, $450, and $500 a week. Both the mean and the median are $400. Now suppose a sixth person joins the group: a professional athlete who earns $100,000 a week. The mean jumps to more than $17,000 a week, a number that describes nobody in the group. The median moves only slightly, to $425. Because a few very high incomes can pull the mean far upward, government agencies usually report the median when they describe what typical workers earn. As you read the charts in this section, notice that they report median earnings.

Microeconomics and Macroeconomics

Economics covers a great deal of ground, from a single teenager deciding whether to take a job to the entire world economy. To make the subject easier to study, economists divide it into two main branches. One way to picture the difference is to think about studying a forest. One scientist might study a single oak tree: its leaves, its roots, the insects that live on it. Another might study the whole forest: how much rain it gets, how fires spread through it, and how it changes over decades. Both are studying the same forest, but from different distances.

Microeconomics is the study of the economic behavior of individual people, households, and businesses. It asks questions like these: How does a family decide how much of its income to spend on food? How does a grower decide whether to plant almonds or pistachios? Why does the price of strawberries rise in the winter? How do businesses decide how many workers to hire? Units 2 and 3 of this book focus mainly on microeconomics.

Macroeconomics is the study of the behavior of the economy as a whole. It asks questions like these: Why do some years bring rapid growth while others bring recessions? What causes the unemployment rate to rise and fall? Why do prices across the economy sometimes rise quickly? How do government spending, taxes, and the actions of the Federal Reserve affect the nation's economy? Units 4, 5, and 6 focus mainly on macroeconomics.

The two branches are closely connected. Millions of individual micro decisions, by workers, families, and businesses, add up to the macro economy. And the condition of the macro economy, such as whether jobs are easy or hard to find, shapes every micro decision. A business is far more likely to hire new workers when the overall economy is growing.

Key Concepts

Positive and Normative Economics

Economics can also be viewed through a second pair of lenses. Some economic statements describe the world as it is. Others express opinions about how the world should be. Learning to tell the difference is one of the most valuable skills you can take from this course, because news reports, political speeches, and social media posts often mix the two together.

Positive economics is a way of describing and explaining economics as it is. Positive statements can be tested by checking them against facts and data. "California's minimum wage was $16.90 an hour in 2026" is a positive statement; you can check it in the official records, and it is either correct or incorrect. "Raising the minimum wage reduces the number of jobs for teenagers" is also a positive statement, even though economists argue about whether it is true. It makes a claim about cause and effect that can, at least in principle, be tested with evidence.

Normative economics is a way of describing and explaining what economic policy should be. Normative statements involve values and judgments, so they cannot be proved true or false with data alone. "The minimum wage should be raised to $20 an hour" is a normative statement. So is "Growers should be allowed to pump as much groundwater as they need" and "The government ought to do more to help families afford housing." People who agree about every fact can still disagree about normative questions, because they weigh values such as fairness, freedom, security, and growth differently.

Words such as should, ought, better, worse, fair, and unfair are often signs of a normative statement. Economists try to keep the two kinds of statements separate. Positive analysis can tell decision-makers what is likely to happen if they choose a particular policy. It cannot, by itself, tell them which outcome they ought to prefer. That decision belongs to citizens and their elected leaders.

ECONOMICS PACESETTER

Esther DufloBringing the Experiment to Economics

For most of the history of economics, economists could only observe the world and build models to explain it. They could not run experiments the way chemists or medical researchers do. Esther Duflo helped change that.

Born in Paris in 1972, Duflo first studied history and planned an academic career far from economics. A year spent working in Moscow in the early 1990s, as Russia struggled to rebuild its economy after the fall of the Soviet Union, convinced her that economics could make a real difference in people's lives. She earned her doctorate at MIT and stayed on as a professor there for many years.

Duflo became famous for a simple but powerful idea. Instead of debating in general terms whether a program to fight poverty "works," economists should test it, in the same way that doctors test a new medicine. In a randomized controlled trial, researchers randomly divide villages, schools, or families into groups. One group receives a program, such as free textbooks, extra tutors, or a bag of lentils at a vaccination camp, and another similar group does not. Because the groups were chosen at random, any difference in results can be traced to the program itself. In 2003, Duflo, Abhijit Banerjee, and Sendhil Mullainathan founded the Abdul Latif Jameel Poverty Action Lab (J-PAL), a research center that has since helped run more than a thousand such studies around the world.

In 2019, Duflo, Banerjee, and Michael Kremer shared the Nobel Prize in economics for this experimental approach to fighting poverty. At 46, Duflo became the youngest person ever to win the prize and only the second woman. Her work shows that the economist's toolbox keeps growing, and that careful testing can turn big arguments into practical answers.

CASE STUDY

The Real Cost of Water
in Kern County

Almost everything grown in the southern San Joaquin Valley depends on irrigation. As California requires communities to stop pumping more groundwater than nature can replace, growers, towns, and workers face hard choices about who uses a scarce resource, and for what.

Background

A Valley Built on Water

Kern County receives only about six inches of rain in a typical year on the valley floor, less than many deserts. Yet it ranks among the most productive farming counties in the United States. The difference is irrigation. Growers bring in surface water from the Kern River and from canals that carry water hundreds of miles from Northern California through the State Water Project and the federal Central Valley Project. In dry years, when those deliveries shrink, many growers make up the difference by pumping groundwater from wells.

For decades, farms, towns, and businesses across the San Joaquin Valley pumped more water out of the ground each year than rain and rivers put back. Economists and hydrologists call this overdraft. Overdraft is like spending more from a savings account than you deposit: it works for a while, but eventually the account runs low. As water tables dropped, some shallow household wells in rural communities went dry, and in some places the land itself sank as underground layers compacted, damaging canals, roads, and bridges.

In 2014, during one of the worst droughts in California history, the state passed the Sustainable Groundwater Management Act, usually called SGMA (pronounced "SIG-muh"). The law requires local agencies to create plans that bring groundwater use into balance by the early 2040s. For Kern County, that means less water for farming in the years ahead. The documents that follow will help you analyze the real cost of that change, using the economic way of thinking.

DOCUMENT B · GOVERNMENT FACT SUMMARYWhat SGMA Requires, and What Happened in Kern
  • SGMA, enacted in 2014, requires local groundwater sustainability agencies to write plans that end overdraft. In the San Joaquin Valley, where pumping has long exceeded replenishment, groundwater use must be brought down to sustainable levels by the early 2040s.
  • If the state Department of Water Resources (DWR) finds a basin's plans inadequate, it can refer the basin to the State Water Resources Control Board. The Board can then place the basin on probation. In a basin on probation, most well owners must measure and report their pumping and pay fees to the state.
  • After DWR found the Kern County Subbasin's plans inadequate, the State Water Board scheduled a probation hearing for February 20, 2025. Recognizing that local agencies had made progress, the Board postponed its decision to give them more time to fix the remaining problems.
  • On September 17, 2025, the Board decided that probation was not necessary for the Kern County Subbasin and voted to return it to DWR's oversight, once the revised 2025 plans were confirmed. Board staff confirmed that the revised plans resolved the priority problems, and the return was formalized in December 2025.

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · Scarcity: The Basic Economic Problem
Scarcity exists because wants are unlimited and resources are limited. It forces every society to answer three questions: what to produce, how to produce it, and for whom. The four factors of production are land, labor, capital, and entrepreneurship.
Section 2 · Economic Choices
People respond to monetary and nonmonetary incentives. Every choice involves a trade-off, and the value of the next-best alternative is the opportunity cost. Cost-benefit analysis and marginal thinking help people decide whether to do a little more or a little less.
Section 3 · Analyzing Production Possibilities
A production possibilities curve shows scarcity, trade-offs, opportunity cost, efficiency, and underutilization. Opportunity costs usually increase as production shifts, which bows the curve outward. Economic growth shifts the curve outward.
Section 4 · The Economist's Toolbox
Economists use models, statistics, tables, and graphs. Microeconomics studies individuals and businesses; macroeconomics studies the whole economy. Positive statements can be tested; normative statements involve value judgments.
