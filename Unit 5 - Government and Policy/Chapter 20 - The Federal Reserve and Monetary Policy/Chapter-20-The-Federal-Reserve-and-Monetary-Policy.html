Concept Review

In Chapter 19 you learned that fiscal policy is the use of government taxes and spending to influence the economy. Congress and the President control it, and it has one of two aims: to fight a recession by raising total spending or to fight inflation by reducing it. Fiscal policy is slow, because laws take time to pass, and it changes the government's own budget. Another institution, one that does not collect taxes or write budgets, also steers the economy. Its tool is the price of borrowing money.

Chapter 20 Key Concept

Monetary policy includes all of the Federal Reserve's actions that change the money supply and interest rates in order to influence the economy. Its purpose is to keep prices stable and employment high, by fighting inflation when prices rise too fast and by supporting spending and jobs when the economy weakens.

Why the Concept Matters

A family in Shafter wants to buy a house, a farmer near Wasco wants to finance a new almond-harvest shaker, and a graduate in Bakersfield wants a car loan. All three pay interest, and the rate they are quoted depends partly on decisions made by a committee that meets in Washington about eight times a year. When that committee raises or lowers its target for short-term rates, borrowing costs, savings account rates, stock prices, and the number of jobs can all move. You will study how the committee works, what tools it uses, and why economists disagree about how much it can do.

Case Study Preview

Every statement from the Federal Reserve is read closely by investors, bankers, and workers. At the end of this chapter, you will study Interpreting Signals from the Fed and learn how to read the Fed's own words and the market's reaction.

Key Concepts

What Is a Central Bank?

Every time you pay with a dollar bill, you rely on an institution you may never think about. A central bank is a government-chartered institution that serves as the bank for a country's banks and its government, and that conducts the country's monetary policy. It does not take deposits from you or make car loans. Instead, it works at the top of the financial system. The central bank of the United States is the Federal Reserve System, often called the Fed. Nearly every country has a central bank: the Bank of England, the Bank of Japan, the Bank of Canada, and the European Central Bank, which serves the countries that use the euro.

The word monetary refers to money and the rules that govern it, including how much money is in the economy and what it costs to borrow. In Chapter 14 you learned that money works as a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value, and that banks create much of the money supply when they make loans. In Chapter 19 you learned that fiscal policy works through taxes and government spending. Monetary policy works through a different channel: it changes the amount of money available to banks and the interest rates that follow, and those changes spread to households and businesses.

Why does a country need a central bank? Consider the problems that arise without one. Banks keep only a fraction of deposits on hand and lend out the rest. If depositors fear a bank will fail, they rush to withdraw their money, and even a healthy bank can run out of cash. This is a bank run, and it can spread from bank to bank in a panic. Before 1913, the United States had no permanent institution that could lend to banks in a crisis. In the Panic of 1907, bank runs and falling stock prices threatened the whole system, and a group of private bankers led by J. P. Morgan had to organize a rescue. Many Americans concluded that the nation could not depend on a private rescue every time a panic struck.

Congress responded by passing the Federal Reserve Act, which President Woodrow Wilson signed on December 23, 1913. The act created the Federal Reserve System to serve as a lender of last resort for banks, to supply a flexible and safe currency, and to supervise banks. Over time, Congress has added to the Fed's duties. After the Great Depression, when about 9,000 banks failed in the early 1930s and the money supply shrank sharply, Congress created federal deposit insurance and gave the Fed a stronger role in guiding the economy. In 1977, Congress told the Fed to pursue maximum employment, stable prices, and moderate long-term interest rates. In 2010, after the financial crisis, Congress expanded the Fed's role in supervising large financial firms.

Key Concepts

The Structure of the Fed

The Federal Reserve is unusual because it is neither a single bank nor a government department. The authors of the Federal Reserve Act, wary of giving too much power to one place or one group, spread authority across the country. The result is a system with three main parts, shown in Figure 20.1.

The first part is the Board of Governors, which sits in Washington, D.C. It has seven members, called governors. The President nominates each governor, and the Senate must confirm each one. A governor's term lasts fourteen years, and the terms are staggered so that one expires every two years. The long terms mean that no single President can appoint the whole Board, and that governors do not face the voters. The President also nominates one governor to serve as Chair and another as Vice Chair, each for a four-year term that can be renewed. The Chair leads the Board, testifies to Congress, and is the most visible voice of the Fed. The Board supervises the Reserve Banks, sets some of the Fed's rules for banks, and votes on the discount rate you will learn about in Section 3.

The second part is the twelve Federal Reserve Banks. Each serves one of twelve districts, shown in Figure 20.2, and each is headquartered in a major city: Boston, New York, Philadelphia, Cleveland, Richmond, Atlanta, Chicago, St. Louis, Minneapolis, Kansas City, Dallas, and San Francisco. The Reserve Banks are the Fed's operating arms. They hold the reserve accounts of banks in their districts, move cash and checks, examine banks, and study the regional economy. The twenty-four branches extend the system, including a branch in Los Angeles that serves Southern California. Kern County lies in the Twelfth District, which is the largest district by area and covers nine western states, including Alaska and Hawaii. Each Reserve Bank has a president chosen by its own board of directors, whose members come from local banks, businesses, and communities, and who are approved by the Board of Governors. The directors include bankers and also representatives of the public, such as business owners and leaders of community organizations. Because the directors are local, the Reserve Banks gather timely information about conditions in their regions, such as the harvest, housing, and the oil industry.

The third part is the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC), the group that makes the Fed's decisions about monetary policy. The FOMC has twelve voting members: the seven governors, the president of the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and four of the other eleven Reserve Bank presidents, who take turns serving one-year terms. The remaining seven presidents attend the meetings and take part in the discussion but do not vote. The committee meets at least eight times each year. After each meeting it publishes a statement, and a few weeks later it publishes minutes with more detail. Its decisions affect interest rates throughout the economy, and you will analyze its statements in the Case Study.

Banks are also part of the system. All national banks, which are chartered by the federal government, must belong to the Federal Reserve System, and state-chartered banks may choose to join. Member banks own the stock of their regional Reserve Bank, but this stock cannot be sold or traded and gives them no control over monetary policy. The ownership is not like owning a share of a company, and the Reserve Banks do not operate to earn profits for their owners. After paying expenses and a limited dividend to member banks, the Fed sends its remaining earnings to the U.S. Treasury.

Key Concepts

Independence and Accountability

The Fed is often described as independent. That does not mean that it is free of oversight. It means that its monetary policy decisions are not approved in advance by the President or Congress. The Fed's independence rests on several features: long terms for governors, who can be removed only for cause; a source of funds that does not depend on yearly appropriations, because the Fed earns income from the securities it holds; and a structure that spreads decision-making across many people. The Fed is a creation of Congress and can be changed by Congress, and the Chair reports to Congress at least twice a year on monetary policy. The Fed also publishes its decisions, minutes, and economic projections so that the public can evaluate its performance.

Why would a country want an independent central bank? Many economists argue that elected officials face an incentive to cut interest rates before an election. Lower rates make borrowing cheaper, spending rises, and unemployment may fall in the short run. But if the economy is already near capacity, the extra spending can push prices up, and the cost of inflation arrives later. By placing monetary decisions in an institution whose officials serve long terms, the system tries to protect decisions that pay off over years from the pressures of election calendars. Studies that compare countries often find that countries with more independent central banks have had lower inflation, though economists note that other differences between countries also matter.

Others raise questions about independence. A group of unelected officials has a large effect on jobs, prices, and the cost of a home, and some argue that this power should be answerable to voters more directly. Others point out that the Fed's actions have uneven effects, because higher interest rates affect borrowers, savers, and workers in different ways. Supporters respond that Congress can change the Fed's goals by law and that the Fed's openness gives citizens the information they need. Economists and citizens will continue to disagree about where to draw the line between independence and accountability. You will examine this question yourself in the box below.

Key Concepts

What Is the Fed Trying to Do?

Congress has given the Fed a dual mandate: to promote maximum employment and stable prices. A third goal in the law, moderate long-term interest rates, tends to follow from the first two. Maximum employment does not mean that every person has a job. At any time, some people are between jobs or entering the labor force, so economists treat some unemployment as normal. Stable prices do not mean that prices never change. In January 2012, the FOMC announced that it considers inflation of 2 percent per year, measured by the price index for personal consumption expenditures (PCE), to be consistent with its mandate. In Chapter 16 you learned that the Consumer Price Index measures the cost of a basket of goods for urban households. The PCE index is a related measure from the Bureau of Economic Analysis that covers a broader range of spending, and the Fed prefers it.

Why 2 percent and not zero? A low, steady rate of inflation gives the Fed room to cut interest rates in a recession. If inflation were zero and the economy fell into a downturn, the Fed might run out of room to lower rates, because rates cannot be cut far below zero. A small positive inflation rate also guards against deflation, a general fall in prices, which can make debts harder to pay and cause households to delay purchases. And some measures of inflation tend to overstate real price changes slightly. Many other central banks have chosen the same target, as the Global Perspective in Section 3 shows.

The two goals sometimes pull in different directions. When unemployment is high and inflation is low, the Fed can support spending and jobs without much cost. When both unemployment and inflation are low, policy can stand pat. When inflation is high and unemployment is low, the Fed may raise rates to slow the economy, even though that may cost some jobs. And when both are a problem at once, as in the 1970s, there are no easy choices. Figure 20.4 shows how the two measures have moved since 2000. Notice that PCE inflation was close to 2 percent in many years of the 2010s, rose to 6.0 percent in 2021, and was still above the target in 2025.

Key Concepts

Serving the Banking System

The Fed is often called the banker's bank, because banks use it much as households and businesses use their banks. A bank can hold an account at its Reserve Bank, borrow from it, and use it to move money to other banks. The Fed provides four main services to the banking system.

Clearing checks and payments. When you deposit a check written by a customer of a different bank, the two banks must settle up. The process is called check clearing: the bank that received the check presents it to the bank that must pay, and the money is transferred between the two. The Federal Reserve Banks historically cleared most checks. Today most checks are converted to images, and the Fed's accounts move the money electronically within a day or so, as Figure 20.5 shows. The Fed also runs electronic payment systems. Fedwire moves large payments between banks and businesses on the same day, the Automated Clearing House (ACH) handles payroll deposits and bill payments, and FedNow, launched in July 2023, lets banks send payments between accounts in seconds, at any hour. Together these systems move trillions of dollars daily.

Supervising and regulating banks. The Fed shares responsibility for the safety of the banking system with other agencies, such as the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation and the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency. The Fed supervises state-chartered banks that are members of the system and bank holding companies, which are companies that own one or more banks. Its examiners carry out bank exams, which are inspections of a bank's records, loans, and management to check that the bank is safe and follows the law. Since the 2007–2009 financial crisis, the Fed has also run annual stress tests for the largest banks: it asks each bank to show how it would fare in an imagined severe recession, and it publishes the results.

Acting as the lender of last resort. A bank that faces a sudden shortage of cash can borrow from its Reserve Bank through what the Fed calls the discount window. The loans must be backed by collateral, which means that the bank pledges assets such as loans or securities that the Fed can claim if the bank cannot repay. The loan helps a bank that is sound but short of cash to meet withdrawals without selling assets at a loss. During the 2008 crisis and the pandemic in 2020, the Fed also created temporary emergency programs that reached beyond banks. In 2023, after the failure of Silicon Valley Bank, a Santa Clara bank, and others, the Fed set up a new program to lend to banks against collateral valued at its face amount.

Supplying currency. The Bureau of Engraving and Printing, part of the Treasury, prints Federal Reserve Notes, and the Reserve Banks put them into circulation when banks order cash for their customers. A bank that needs more cash for a busy season, such as the weeks before the holidays, orders it from the Fed, and the amount is subtracted from the bank's account at the Fed. The Fed also destroys worn-out bills and takes counterfeit notes out of circulation. Coins are made by the U.S. Mint. All the cash you carry, whatever its form, is a small part of the total money supply, because most money exists as bank deposits.

Key Concepts

Serving the Federal Government

The Fed is also the federal government's bank. The Treasury Department keeps its main checking account, called the Treasury General Account, at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. Tax payments are deposited into it, and when the government pays its bills, such as Social Security checks, military salaries, and payments to contractors, the money flows out of it. On a typical day, hundreds of billions of dollars can move in and out of this account. The Fed acts as a fiscal agent for the Treasury, which means that it carries out financial tasks on the government's behalf.

One of the most important tasks is selling government debt. In Chapter 19 you learned that the Treasury borrows by selling bills, notes, and bonds. The Treasury runs the auctions, and the Federal Reserve Banks process the bids and handle the payments and the interest. The Fed can also buy Treasury securities in the market as part of monetary policy, which you will study in Section 3. But it does not buy them directly from the Treasury to pay for the government's spending. The law keeps the two functions separate, so that the Treasury must borrow from the public instead of printing money, and so that decisions about interest rates are not mixed with decisions about the budget. Economists see this wall as one of the most important protections against inflation. Governments that have paid their bills by creating money have often suffered very high inflation.

The Fed also gives advice. The Chair and other officials testify to Congress about the economy, and the Fed's research staff produces data and forecasts that Congress, the Treasury, and the public use. The Fed also collects and publishes economic information, such as industrial production and the Beige Book, a report released eight times a year on conditions in each district. In the Twelfth District, the Beige Book has described agriculture, construction, and energy conditions in places such as the Central Valley.

Key Concepts

How Money Is Created

Money in the modern economy is mostly a number in a bank account. The paper money and coins in circulation make up a small share of the money supply, which is the total amount of money in the economy. Economists measure it in several ways. M1 includes currency and the deposits that can be used immediately for payments, and since May 2020 it also includes savings deposits. M2, a broader measure, adds small time deposits and balances in retail money market funds. In September 2026, M2 was about $23.4 trillion. Most of it was created by banks.

To see how, start with a bank's balance sheet. When you deposit $1,000 in a bank, the bank has $1,000 more in reserves, which are the funds it keeps on hand or in its account at the Fed. The bank does not keep the entire deposit idle. It lends out most of it, because lending earns interest. When a borrower takes out a loan, the bank usually credits the amount to the borrower's account, and the borrower spends it. The person who receives the payment deposits it in a bank, and that bank can lend most of it. At every step, new deposits are created, and the money supply grows. This process is called fractional reserve banking, because banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves.

For many years, the Fed required banks to hold a fraction of certain deposits in reserve. That fraction is the required reserve ratio. If the ratio is 10 percent, a bank that receives a $1,000 deposit must keep $100 and can lend $900. If the borrower's payment ends up in another bank, that bank must keep $90 and can lend $810, and so on. Figure 20.6 shows how the rounds add up. The total possible increase in deposits is given by the deposit multiplier formula: 1 ÷ the required reserve ratio. With a 10 percent ratio, the multiplier is 10, so an initial $1,000 of new reserves can support up to $10,000 of deposits in total.

The model is a good way to understand how banks and the Fed interact, but it is also simplified. In practice, not all of the money from a loan stays in the banking system, because some people hold cash, and banks do not always lend all that they can. Banks decide how much to lend based on the demand for loans, the risk, and the rules about how much of their own funds, called capital, they must hold. And since March 26, 2020, the Fed's required reserve ratio has been 0 percent for all banks. The Fed made this change because it now controls interest rates in a different way, which you will study in Section 3. Banks still hold reserves, to meet withdrawals and to settle payments, but the old model of a fixed multiplier no longer limits lending directly.

A Kern County credit union receives a new $5,000 deposit from a farmworker who cashes a check. In this hypothetical example, the required reserve ratio is 10 percent. Key Concepts

What Changes the Money Supply?

The money supply does not stay constant. Several factors push it up or down. The first is the Fed's own policy: when the Fed buys securities or lends to banks, it adds reserves, and when it sells securities, it removes them. You will study these actions in Section 3. The second is the behavior of banks. When banks are confident and loan demand is strong, they lend more, and deposits grow. When banks are worried about losses, they hold back, and money grows slowly even if the Fed provides reserves. The third is the behavior of households and businesses. When people borrow to buy homes and equipment, money is created; when people repay loans faster than they borrow new ones, it can shrink. A fourth factor is the flow of funds across borders, because foreign investors and governments hold deposits and securities in the United States.

Figure 20.7 shows how much the money supply has changed. M2 grew by 3.6 to 6.8 percent in most years between 2000 and 2019, which is a steady pace. In 2020 it jumped by 24.5 percent, the largest rise in this period, as the government sent relief payments, as people saved more because they could not spend, and as the Fed bought large amounts of securities. In 2022 and 2023, as the Fed raised rates and the economy adjusted, M2 shrank by 1.0 and 2.4 percent, the first declines in decades. These swings illustrate that money growth reflects the actions of the Fed, banks, and the public together. Economists, including those who follow the school of thought called monetarism, which you will meet in the Pacesetter in Section 3, pay close attention to the money supply because they believe it is closely related to inflation over time.

Key Concepts

The Fed's Policy Tools

Monetary policy is the Fed's use of the money supply and interest rates to influence the economy. As Section 2 explained, the Fed does not print money to hand out and cannot dictate the interest rate on your car loan. What it controls directly is a short-term interest rate in the market where banks lend to one another. Banks that have extra reserves lend them overnight to banks that need reserves, and the interest rate on these loans is the federal funds rate. The FOMC does not set this rate by decree. It announces a target range, such as 3.75 to 4.00 percent, and then uses its tools to guide the market rate into that range. The tools work together.

Interest on reserve balances. The Fed pays banks interest on the reserve balances they keep at their Reserve Banks. This rate, called the IORB, works as a floor under the market: a bank will not lend reserves to another bank for much less than it could earn risk-free from the Fed. When the FOMC raises the IORB, banks demand higher rates on loans, and the federal funds rate rises with it. The Fed also offers an overnight reverse repurchase agreement facility, in which money market funds and others lend cash to the Fed overnight for a set rate, which sets a second floor. These two administered rates are the main tools today. On September 17, 2026, the Fed set the IORB at 3.90 percent and the overnight reverse repo rate at 3.75 percent, inside the new target range.

The discount rate. The discount rate is the interest rate the Fed charges banks that borrow from it through the discount window. The Fed's main discount rate, called the primary credit rate, is set above the target range so that banks borrow from each other first and use the Fed as a backup. In September 2026, the primary credit rate was 4.0 percent, at the top of the target range. Changes in the discount rate send a signal about the Fed's intentions, and changes in the other tools are announced at the same time.

Open market operations. The oldest and most flexible tool is open market operations: the buying and selling of government securities by the Fed in the open market. When the Fed buys Treasury securities, it pays by crediting the seller's bank with new reserves. The reserves are new money that did not exist before. When the Fed sells securities, banks pay with their reserves, and the reserves leave the system. The Federal Reserve Bank of New York carries out these trades for the FOMC. Before 2008, the Fed relied on small daily open market operations to keep the federal funds rate on target. Since then, reserves have been so plentiful that the Fed relies on the administered rates instead, and it describes its approach as maintaining ample reserves. Open market operations now mostly change the size of the Fed's holdings, as Figure 20.12 shows.

Reserve requirements. The Fed can also change the required reserve ratio you learned about in Section 2. Because it is cumbersome for banks, the Fed rarely used it, and since March 26, 2020, the ratio has been zero. Today it is not an active tool.

Rates that follow. Many other rates move with the federal funds rate. The prime rate is the interest rate that banks charge their most creditworthy business customers. By long-standing practice, it is about 3 percentage points above the top of the Fed's target range, so with a top of 4.00 percent, the prime rate would be about 7 percent. Credit card rates, car loans, and many loans to farms and small businesses are tied to the prime rate or to similar short-term rates. Mortgage rates, which are long-term, follow the market's expectation of future short-term rates and inflation more than the federal funds rate itself, but they tend to move in the same direction, as Figure 20.13 shows.

Key Concepts

Expansionary and Contractionary Monetary Policy

Like fiscal policy, monetary policy comes in two directions. Expansionary monetary policy is policy that increases the money supply and lowers interest rates in order to raise spending and output. It is also called an easy-money policy, because it makes money easy to borrow. The Fed uses it when the economy is weak, unemployment is high, and inflation is low. By lowering the target range, the Fed makes it cheaper for banks to lend. Lower rates encourage households to buy homes and cars, encourage businesses to invest, and make it easier for governments to borrow. Spending rises, and so do output and jobs.

Contractionary monetary policy is policy that decreases the money supply growth and raises interest rates in order to reduce spending and inflation. It is also called a tight-money policy. The Fed uses it when the economy is growing so fast that prices are rising too quickly. Higher rates make borrowing more expensive, so households and businesses postpone purchases, and spending slows. Slower spending takes pressure off prices. The cost is that slower spending may also mean slower hiring or layoffs, which is why the Fed tries to raise rates gradually and to explain its reasons.

Figure 20.9 shows the idea with a model of the market for money. The horizontal axis shows the quantity of money, and the vertical axis shows the interest rate, which is the price of borrowing money. The demand for money (MD) slopes downward: at a lower interest rate, people and businesses want to hold and borrow more money, because the cost of holding it falls. The supply of money (MS) is set by the Fed and the banking system. When the Fed expands the money supply, the supply curve shifts to the right, the equilibrium interest rate falls, and the quantity of money rises. When the Fed contracts the money supply, the curve shifts to the left, and the interest rate rises. The model is a simplification, but it captures how monetary policy changes the cost of borrowing.

Monetary policy affects the economy through a chain of events, shown at the bottom of Figure 20.9. A change in the Fed's target range changes banks' lending rates. Changed rates alter how much households and businesses borrow and spend. Changes in spending alter output, jobs, and prices. Economists call this the transmission mechanism. The chain has several links, and each can be weaker or stronger depending on conditions. In a downturn, for example, even very low rates may not persuade worried businesses to borrow.

Key Concepts

Monetary Policy in Action

The Fed's tools have been tested in several difficult periods. The first is the high inflation of the 1970s. In that decade, price increases grew with repeated oil shocks and rapid money growth, and unemployment rose too, a combination that the policies of the time did not handle well. By 1979, inflation (Dec-to-Dec CPI) reached 13.3 percent. The Fed, then led by Chair Paul Volcker, announced that it would tighten the supply of reserves and let interest rates rise as far as needed. The federal funds rate reached a monthly average of 19.1 percent in June 1981. Inflation fell to 3.8 percent by 1982, but unemployment rose to a yearly average of 9.7 percent in 1982, the highest of the period, and many farmers, builders, and car dealers suffered. Figure 20.11 shows both. Supporters say the policy ended the inflation spiral and gave the country decades of low inflation. Critics say the cost in jobs and bankruptcies was very high, and some argue that other policies could have reduced the pain. Both views draw on the same data.

The second test came in 2007 to 2009, when the housing bubble burst and the financial system faltered. The Fed cut its target for the federal funds rate from 5.25 percent in the summer of 2007 to a range of 0 to 0.25 percent in December 2008. With rates near zero, it could not lower them further, so it turned to a new approach, often called quantitative easing (QE): it bought large amounts of Treasury and mortgage-backed securities in order to lower longer-term interest rates and to supply more reserves. As Figure 20.12 shows, the Fed's total assets rose from $0.9 trillion at the end of 2007 to about $4.5 trillion at the end of 2014. The Fed kept the rate near zero for seven years, until December 2015.

The third came in 2020. When the pandemic closed businesses in March, the Fed cut the target range to 0 to 0.25 percent within days, restarted large purchases of securities, and set up emergency lending programs. Its total assets rose from about $4.2 trillion at the end of 2019 to $7.4 trillion at the end of 2020, and they peaked at $9.0 trillion in April 2022. Then inflation rose to the highest levels in about forty years. The Fed raised the target range from 0 to 0.25 percent in March 2022 to 5.25 to 5.50 percent in July 2023, the fastest increase in decades, and let its holdings of securities shrink, a process sometimes called quantitative tightening. By November 2026, total assets were $6.7 trillion. The Fed began lowering rates in late 2024 as inflation eased. In September 2026, with inflation elevated again, the FOMC raised the target range by 25 basis points to 3.75 to 4.00 percent, its first increase since July 2023, in a unanimous vote of 12 to 0. At its meeting on October 28, 2026, it held the range steady in a 10 to 2 vote; both dissenters preferred another quarter-point increase.

ECONOMICS PACESETTER

Milton FriedmanThe Economist Who Watched the Money Supply

Milton Friedman was born in Brooklyn to immigrant parents who ran small businesses, and he attended Rutgers University on a scholarship. He earned graduate degrees at the University of Chicago and Columbia University, worked for the federal government during the Great Depression and World War II, and then spent three decades teaching economics at the University of Chicago. His work covered many topics, including how people save and spend, how markets work, and the role of government.

Friedman is best known for his study of money. With Anna Jacobson Schwartz, he wrote A Monetary History of the United States, which traced changes in the nation's money from the Civil War to 1960. One of its main claims was that the money supply fell by about one third between 1929 and 1933 and that the Federal Reserve, by not acting to stop that fall, made the Great Depression worse than it had to be. His school of thought, called monetarism, holds that changes in the money supply have a major effect on output in the short run and on prices in the long run. In Friedman's most famous phrase, inflation is "always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." He proposed that the Fed should let the money supply grow at a steady, predictable rate each year rather than adjusting it to the latest economic news, so that the Fed would not make mistakes of its own.

Monetarism influenced central banks in the late 1970s and early 1980s, including the Fed's fight against inflation under Paul Volcker. But the policy of targeting money growth did not work as well in practice, because the relationship between the money supply and spending proved unstable, and the Fed returned to using interest rates as its main target. Economists who follow Keynes, whom you met in Chapter 19, argue that fiscal policy and total spending matter more than Friedman believed. Friedman's supporters argue that his insight about the dangers of inflation and of sudden swings in policy remains important. Today, most economists draw on ideas from both traditions.

Key Concepts

Challenges and Limits of Monetary Policy

Monetary policy has advantages over fiscal policy. The FOMC can decide in a single meeting, and its decisions take effect immediately in financial markets. It does not require approval from Congress, and it does not directly change the federal budget. But it also faces serious challenges, and economists disagree about how well it works. Several are especially important.

Time lags. The effects of a rate change can take many months to appear. Milton Friedman described the lags as "long and variable." A mortgage decision takes weeks, a new factory takes months to plan, and hiring decisions react slowly. The Fed therefore has to act based on forecasts of where the economy will be a year from now, not on where it is today. If the forecast is wrong, the policy can arrive too late or push too hard.

Uncertain measurement and targets. The Fed does not know in real time what the true unemployment rate consistent with stable prices is, nor exactly how much inflation will follow from a policy change. Data are revised, and different measures of inflation can tell different stories, for example the PCE rate of 3.65 percent in September 2026 versus the core rate of 3.1 percent, which leaves out food and energy prices. Economists call the Fed's task "driving while looking in the rearview mirror."

Conflicting goals and supply shocks. When prices rise because oil or shipping costs rise, raising interest rates cannot make more oil. A rate increase would slow spending but might also raise unemployment. The Fed must decide whether to look through the shock or to respond. If it responds too little, inflation may become a habit; too much, and the economy may weaken.

Expectations. If people expect high inflation, workers ask for higher wages and businesses raise prices ahead of time, which creates the inflation they feared. A central bank's credibility helps keep expectations anchored, which is why the Fed communicates clearly. In the rational expectations view you met in Chapter 19, a policy that people anticipate has less effect on output, because they adjust their behavior in advance.

Limits when rates are near zero. When the federal funds rate is near zero, the Fed cannot easily push it lower. Economists sometimes describe this as the problem that the Fed cannot "push on a string": it can make borrowing cheap, but it cannot force households and businesses to borrow and spend. This is one reason fiscal policy and monetary policy often work together in deep recessions, the subject of Section 4.

Different effects on different people. Rate increases hurt borrowers, such as young families with variable-rate loans, but help savers who earn more on deposits. Low rates help borrowers and homeowners but can reduce the income of retirees. Whatever the Fed chooses, some groups gain and others lose, which is one reason that its choices are debated.

GLOBAL PERSPECTIVECentral Banks Around the World

Almost every large economy has a central bank, and many have adopted the same inflation target as the Fed. Each bank has its own structure and rules. The European Central Bank, which serves the countries that use the euro, defines price stability as 2 percent inflation over the medium term. The Bank of England has a 2 percent inflation target set by the government. The Bank of Japan has aimed for 2 percent inflation since 2013, after many years in which prices were flat or fell. The Bank of Canada aims for 2 percent, the midpoint of a range from 1 to 3 percent. Targets and rules can change, so check each bank's website for its current framework.

Central banks often face the same problems at once. After the pandemic, inflation rose in nearly every advanced economy, and central banks raised rates in 2022 and 2023. But each bank must weigh its own conditions, such as the strength of its job market, the size of its government debt, and the value of its currency. When the Fed raises rates, the dollar often becomes more valuable, which makes American goods more expensive abroad and imports cheaper. You will study exchange rates in Chapter 21.

Key Concepts

Two Policies, One Economy

Fiscal policy and monetary policy have the same goals: high employment, stable prices, and steady growth. But they are controlled by different people. Congress and the President decide on taxes and spending, while the Fed's FOMC decides on interest rates. The two groups do not need each other's permission, and they do not always agree. Each policy also works in a different way. Fiscal policy changes the government's own spending or the incomes of households and businesses, while monetary policy changes the price of borrowing. The combination of the two is called the policy mix.

When both policies expand. In a deep recession, the two policies can work together. If Congress cuts taxes or raises spending, and the Fed lowers rates and buys securities, total spending can rise by more than either policy would achieve alone. Lower interest rates also make it cheaper for the government to borrow to pay for the fiscal stimulus. This is the combination that the country used in 2008 to 2009 and again in 2020, as Figures 20.14 and 20.15 show. In 2009, Congress passed the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act, an $787 billion package, while the Fed lowered its target for the federal funds rate to near zero and expanded its holdings of securities from $0.9 trillion at the end of 2007 to $2.2 trillion at the end of 2009. In 2020, Congress passed the CARES Act, estimated at $2.2 trillion, while the Fed cut rates to zero and its total assets grew by about $3.2 trillion in a single year.

When both policies contract. When inflation is high, the two can also work together to cool the economy. A budget that reduces the deficit takes some spending out of the economy, and higher interest rates reduce borrowing. Both policies pull spending down. The combination can bring inflation down faster, but it also raises the risk of a recession. Policy makers therefore use it carefully and usually do so gradually. For example, in the 1990s, Congress passed laws that reduced the deficit and the Fed raised rates in 1994 to head off inflation, and the economy continued to grow, although economists disagree about how much each policy contributed.

When the policies conflict. Sometimes one policy expands while the other contracts. Suppose the government runs very large deficits, which add to spending, while the Fed raises interest rates to hold down inflation. The effects partly cancel, and some sectors, such as home building and business investment, may suffer from the higher rates while the government spends more. Large government borrowing can also push interest rates up, which is the crowding-out effect you studied in Chapter 19. In the early 1980s, for example, Congress cut taxes and raised defense spending, which raised deficits, while the Fed held rates very high to fight inflation. Some economists argue that the mix of loose budgets and tight money led to very high interest rates and a strong dollar, which hurt American farmers and manufacturers that sold abroad. Others argue that the tax cuts, along with the Fed's policy, helped to produce the long expansion that followed. This is one of the most debated questions in modern economic history.

The Great Recession and the Pandemic: Policy in Practice

The 2007–2009 financial crisis and the 2020 pandemic recession show the two policies at work. Figure 20.16 compares the federal funds rate and the unemployment rate from 2007 to 2026. Unemployment rose from 4.6 percent in 2007 to an annual average of 9.6 percent in 2010, the highest of the period, while the Fed held its rate near zero for seven years. When the pandemic hit in 2020, unemployment averaged 8.1 percent for the year, and it had briefly been far higher in April. Both policies were then used quickly and on a large scale. By 2022, the unemployment rate had fallen to 3.7 percent, but inflation was well above target, and the Fed raised rates to 5.14 percent on average in 2024. In 2026, with unemployment at 4.1 percent in October and PCE inflation at 3.65 percent in September, the Fed raised its target range in September and held it in October.

Economists continue to debate what each policy did. Some argue that the fiscal and monetary actions in 2020 and 2021 prevented a long depression and brought unemployment down much faster than after 2009. Others argue that together they added too much demand when supply chains and labor markets were disrupted, and that this contributed to the inflation of 2021 and 2022. Many economists say both statements contain truth, and that the size of each effect is hard to measure. When you evaluate a claim about policy, ask what the economy would have looked like without the policy, because that cannot be observed directly.

Key Concepts

Other Tools for Managing the Economy

Fiscal and monetary policy are the main tools of stabilization, but governments have tried others. The period of the 1970s showed the limits of the main tools. Prices were rising, and unemployment was too, a condition called stagflation, which means a combination of stagnant economic growth and inflation. Standard demand policies seemed to offer a poor choice: policies to cut unemployment risked adding to inflation, and policies to fight inflation risked raising unemployment. Economists and policy makers looked for other solutions.

One was wage and price controls, which are government limits on how much wages and prices can rise. The United States used them in World War II and again in 1971, when President Richard Nixon announced a 90-day freeze on wages and prices on August 15 and then a series of controls that lasted into 1974. Inflation slowed at first, but economists note that it returned when the controls were lifted, and many businesses held back goods or reduced quality because the legal price was too low to cover costs. Most economists today are skeptical of price controls as a way to fight inflation, because they interfere with the signals that prices send, as you learned in Chapter 6. Supporters of temporary controls argue that in an emergency, such as a war, they can prevent a harmful scramble for scarce goods.

Another approach is to change supply. The supply-side measures you studied in Chapter 19, such as tax incentives, deregulation, and investment in education and infrastructure, aim to increase the economy's ability to produce goods and services, which lowers prices over time. Policy makers also use trade policy, such as lowering or raising tariffs, which affects the prices of imported goods, and regulation, such as rules for banks, which affect how much credit is available. You will study trade in Chapter 21.

Governments can also try to influence expectations. Public statements by the President or the Fed Chair that signal their goals may change how people and businesses behave. When the Fed announces that it will keep rates low for a long period, it is hoping that households and businesses will plan on the basis of that promise. If people do not believe the statement, though, it will not work, which is why a central bank's credibility is valuable. In the Case Study you will examine how the Fed's statements are read by investors and the public.

CASE STUDY

Interpreting Signals from the Fed

Investors, bankers, and families read the Federal Reserve's statements word by word, looking for clues about the next move in interest rates. This case study asks you to read the Fed's own words, the data behind them, and the market's reaction, and to decide whether the Fed is steering well.

Background

Why the Words Matter

The FOMC meets about eight times a year and, after each meeting, publishes a statement of a few hundred words. Financial markets treat every change in wording as a clue. The reason is that the value of a bond, the cost of a mortgage, and the price of a share of stock all depend on what investors expect interest rates to do next. If the market expects the Fed to raise rates, long-term rates may rise before the Fed acts. If it expects cuts, they may fall. The Fed's communication is therefore itself a tool of policy, because it shapes expectations.

In 2026 the signals were mixed. Inflation had fallen from its 2022 peak but remained above the Fed's 2 percent goal. In September 2026, the FOMC raised the target range for the federal funds rate by one quarter of a percentage point, to 3.75 to 4.00 percent, its first increase since July 2023. In October it held the range steady, but two of the twelve voting members dissented because they preferred another increase. A family deciding whether to buy a house in Shafter, a farmer deciding whether to finance new equipment, and a bank deciding how much to lend all had to interpret these signals.

DOCUMENT D · COMPOSITE VIEWPOINTSThree Ways to Read the Signals

These viewpoints were written for this textbook. Each combines arguments commonly made in the debate over interest rates; they are not quotations from real individuals or organizations.

THE INFLATION-FIRST VIEW
"Inflation has been above the goal for more than five years. Every month it stays high, families lose purchasing power and people start to expect high prices. The Fed has to be willing to raise rates again, even if it slows hiring, because the longer the inflation lasts, the harder it will be to stop. The October dissents were right."
THE PATIENT VIEW
"Rates have just been raised, and the effects take a year or more to show up. The unemployment rate is low and growth is solid, so the Fed can afford to wait and watch the data. Raising rates again too soon risks overcorrecting, as happens when a driver brakes too hard and then must accelerate. Holding steady was the sensible choice."
THE JOBS-AND-GROWTH VIEW
"Higher rates raise the cost of mortgages, farm equipment loans, and small-business credit. Part of the recent inflation came from supply problems that rate increases do not fix. The Fed should be careful not to weaken a labor market that is working for many families, and should weigh the cost of lost jobs as heavily as it weighs the cost of higher prices."
Thinking Economically: Which parts of each view can be tested with evidence in the other documents? Which parts rest on values, such as how to weigh jobs against prices or how much risk to accept?

Chapter 20 Review

Chapter Summary

Section 1 · The Federal Reserve System
The Fed is the nation's central bank, created in 1913 to prevent panics and supply a safe currency. It has a seven-member Board of Governors with 14-year terms, twelve Reserve Banks, and the Federal Open Market Committee, which makes monetary policy. The Fed is independent but accountable to Congress, and it pursues a dual mandate of maximum employment and stable prices, with a 2 percent inflation goal.
Section 2 · Functions of the Federal Reserve
The Fed clears payments, supervises banks, lends as a last resort, and supplies currency. It is also the government's bank and fiscal agent. Banks create money by lending a fraction of deposits, and the deposit multiplier formula, 1 ÷ the required reserve ratio, shows the maximum expansion. The required reserve ratio has been zero since March 2020.
Section 3 · Monetary Policy
The Fed targets the federal funds rate using interest on reserve balances, the overnight reverse repo rate, the discount rate, and open market operations. Expansionary (easy-money) policy lowers rates; contractionary (tight-money) policy raises them. Time lags, uncertainty, supply shocks, expectations, and rates near zero limit what the Fed can do. Monetarists emphasize money growth.
Section 4 · Applying Monetary and Fiscal Policy
The policy mix can be both expansionary, both contractionary, or conflicting. The 2008–2009 and 2020 responses used both policies at once. Other tools, such as wage and price controls, have had mixed results, and stagflation in the 1970s challenged standard policies.
